# Two Ships, One Name — the plain version

*An explanation for a philosopher who doesn't work in the analytic tradition. No technical apparatus; the depth is in the idea, not the vocabulary.*

---

There's an old puzzle about a ship. A vessel is repaired so often that, plank by plank, every original timber is eventually replaced. Is it still the same ship? Then a sharper twist, from Hobbes: suppose someone had been collecting the discarded planks and quietly reassembles them into a second, complete ship. Now there are two hulls in the water. Which one is the original?

People treat this as a single hard question with a hidden trick answer. The usual move is to say: relax, it's not really a paradox. Just decide in advance what you mean by "the same ship." If you mean *the same matter*, the answer is the reassembled hull. If you mean *the same continuous thing-doing-the-work-of-a-ship*, the answer is the repaired one that never stopped sailing. Pick a definition, apply it consistently, and the paradox evaporates. Confusion cured.

My claim is that this tidy solution is doing something sly, and that seeing through it reveals the real shape of the puzzle. The trouble is that there isn't one question here. There are two, and they come apart.

**The first question is about what makes something the same: its stuff, or its pattern.** This is the genuinely deep disagreement, and it's ancient. Does a thing stay itself because it keeps its material—the actual matter it's made of—or because it keeps its *form*, the organisation that persists even as the material is swapped out? A river keeps no water and is still the river; a statue can be melted down and the bronze survives while the statue doesn't. Stuff and pattern can pull in opposite directions, and each has a real claim to *be* what identity consists in. Call this the question of **ground**: in virtue of what is a thing the same thing?

**The second question is completely different, and almost nobody states it out loud. It's about how many things you're allowed to be talking about in the first place.** When you ask "*which* one is the ship?", you've quietly assumed the answer has to be exactly one hull—that the name "the Ship of Theseus" must land on a single object. But that assumption isn't forced by anything. You could just as easily ask "which things *count as* the ship?"—and then both hulls might qualify, in different senses, for different purposes. The harbour registry might say one; a museum of relics might say the other; the law of salvage might say a third thing again. Call this the question of **cardinality**: how many bearers does the situation permit—one, or more than one?

Here is the whole point in one line: **the tradition has treated these as one question, and they are two.** Worse, it has welded them together so smoothly that the seam is invisible.

Watch what happens when you pull them apart.

In the *first* version of the puzzle—just the repaired ship, no reassembly—there's only one hull in play. The counting question is present, but it has nothing to bite on: with a single candidate, it makes no difference whether you allow one bearer or many—the answer lands the same way. So the whole *live* dispute is the ground question, pure: does *this* thing persist if you swap out its matter? Two schools, two answers, each perfectly consistent on its own. What still makes it feel like a puzzle rather than a mere clash of theories is that we expect there to be one fact of the matter—so the two consistent answers press toward a single verdict the case can't supply.

In the *second* version—Hobbes's, with two hulls—something genuinely new happens, and it's not that the deep question got harder. It's that a *second* question switched on. Now the two grounds don't disagree about whether one thing survived; they point at *different things*. "Same matter" points at the reassembled hull. "Same pattern" points at the repaired one. And here's the move that the tidy solution hides: those two answers only *contradict* each other if you've already insisted that the name can land on just one hull. Insist on that—ask "which is *the* ship?"—and you get a head-on collision. Drop it—allow that the name might pick out different hulls in different contexts—and the collision vanishes. The two answers simply describe two different ships, peacefully.

So the contradiction was never coming from the deep metaphysics. It was coming from a small, unannounced decision about counting, slipped in with the word "which."

The same structure shows up in a second puzzle that looks unrelated but isn't. Take a lump of clay shaped into a statue. They sit in exactly the same place, made of exactly the same matter—yet they seem to differ: squash the statue flat and the lump survives while the statue is gone, so they can't quite be the *same* thing. The deep question is the same as before (does identity track the clay, or the statue-form?). And the hidden second question is the same too—except now it isn't about how many things a *name* points to; it's about how many *objects* are actually there in that one spot. Say there's exactly one object and the two grounds collide, just like the two hulls under one name. Say there are two coincident objects—the statue and the clay it's made of—and they live side by side without conflict. The counting question moved from *language* (one name, how many referents?) to *ontology* (one place, how many objects?), and the puzzle behaved identically. That's the clue that we're looking at a real, general second dimension, not a quirk of how ship-names work.

Which brings the famous "solution" into focus. When the textbook says *just fix your definition and the paradox disappears*, it's quietly turning **two** dials and announcing only **one**. It picks a ground (stuff or pattern), and—by phrasing the puzzle as "which *one* is the ship?"—it also fixes the count at one, without saying so. The paradox "dissolves" not because the hard question got answered but because both dials were set and you were told about one. It's a correct answer to a shallow part of the problem (yes, there's no magic plank where the ship suddenly stops being itself) mistaken for an answer to the whole. A bad answer would have been caught centuries ago. A *good answer to the wrong question* is exactly the kind of thing that survives for twenty-five centuries.

The sharpest pushback here is the obvious one: *isn't a ship just one thing, by definition?* "Ship" is a counting word—one ship, two ships—so perhaps the single-bearer answer isn't a smuggled extra choice at all; it's baked into the concept. That's the strongest reply, and notice what it concedes: it agrees a count is being set, and only insists the word sets it. But the permissive question—"which things *count as* the ship?"—is a perfectly good use of the same word, not a change of subject. A shipping registry, a salvage court, and a museum will each, on occasion, let the reassembled hull wear the name for their own purposes; if "ship" genuinely forced a single answer, they'd be talking about something other than ships, and they aren't. So "one ship" is a defensible setting of the dial—perhaps even the right one—but it's a setting, not something the word decides for us in advance.

So the real result is not a verdict on which hull is Theseus's. I take no side on stuff-versus-pattern; these puzzles don't settle it. The result is structural: there were always two independent questions—*what grounds sameness* and *how many things are in play*—and the long tradition of "just fix your definition" worked by fusing them and confessing to only half the move. Separate them, and the paradox stops being a paradox and becomes a pair of honest choices. You can then see, for any thinker who ever "solved" it, exactly which two dials they set—and that the second one was never forced.

---

*One caution I'd want a careful reader to hold onto: I've spoken of the *counting dial* as a single dial across both puzzles—in the ship case it sets how many things a name points to, in the clay case how many objects share one spot. Whether that's really one dial wired into two places, or two separate dials that just happen to turn alike, is something I flag as an open question rather than a settled point. The core claim—that counting is a *separate* dial from ground—holds either way.*
