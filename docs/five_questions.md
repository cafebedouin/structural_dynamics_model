# Five Questions for Looking at Things Twice

Some things look one way until you look at them from a different angle, and then they look completely different. Learning to do this on purpose — to switch positions before you decide what something is — turns out to be one of the most useful intellectual moves you can make. It's also one of the easier ones to teach, because it's mostly a matter of asking yourself five questions, in order, before you commit to a reading.

This isn't a trick or a debate technique. It's a discipline. The discipline is: don't trust your first reading of any situation that involves people, rules, or institutions. Your first reading came from one position — your position — and the way the situation looks from there is real, but it's not the only real way it looks. Other positions see different things, and some of those things matter for what you should do.

The five questions below are how you check.

---

## Question 1: Who benefits, and who pays?

Most rules, arrangements, and patterns do two things at once: they produce something for somebody, and they cost something for somebody. Often these are different people. Naming them out loud is the first move.

This sounds obvious until you try it. Most rules are presented in a way that hides the answer to this question. They sound neutral. "We do it this way." "It's the policy." "Everyone has to." The words wash out the people. Asking who actually gains and who actually loses puts the people back in.

You don't have to be cynical about it. Sometimes the answer is good — the rule helps a lot of people and costs a small amount that's distributed broadly. Sometimes it's mixed. Sometimes the cost falls on a specific group while the benefit is widely shared. The point is to see it.

## Question 2: What does this look like from the position you'd least want to be in?

Once you've named who pays the cost, go stand in their position for a minute and look at the thing again.

This is harder than it sounds. Your brain will resist. The position you'd least want to be in is, by definition, the one with the worst view of the situation. Your instinct is to stay where the view is good. That's exactly why this question matters: the position you're least drawn to occupy is the position whose perspective most reliably gets ignored.

If a rule looks good to you and you can't construct a coherent objection from the worst-positioned person's view, that probably means you haven't tried hard enough. Try harder. Their objection isn't always right. But it's almost always real, and it almost always reveals something you didn't see from where you were standing.

## Question 3: Is this doing coordination *and* transfer?

Most arrangements that last for any length of time are doing two jobs at once. They're solving a real coordination problem — getting people to do things at the same time, in the same way, for some shared purpose — *and* they're moving something (money, work, attention, status) from one group to another. Both at once.

This is the question that catches the largest number of mistakes. People tend to argue about whether something is a useful coordination tool *or* a power grab. The honest answer is usually that it's both, and the interesting analysis is figuring out the ratio.

A traffic light is mostly coordination, with very little transfer. A toll road is coordination plus transfer in roughly equal measure. A predatory loan is mostly transfer with a thin coordination story painted over it. Most institutions sit somewhere in the middle.

If you can't see the coordination function, you'll dismiss things that are actually serving a purpose. If you can't see the transfer function, you'll endorse things that are bleeding people you don't see. The point is to see both at once.

## Question 4: If everyone agrees this is fine, who's not in the conversation?

When a group reports that everyone is happy with how things work, there are two possible explanations. One is that everyone really is happy. The other is that the people who'd be unhappy aren't there. The second is more common than people realize — through self-selection, exclusion, or by the situation being framed in a way that makes objection sound like complaint. By the time the survey goes around, only the satisfied are left to answer.

So when you encounter a unanimous chorus, ask: who would object, if they were here? And then: where are they? The answer is sometimes "nowhere — this really is fine." But often it's something like: in the hallway, in the other department, in the previous generation, in the population this institution doesn't serve.

## Question 5: If this rule disappeared tomorrow, would the world rearrange itself or stay roughly the same?

Some constraints are real in the sense that you can't change them by deciding to: gravity, the speed of light, the fact that you can't be in two places at once. Other constraints are real in the sense that they exist, but contingent in the sense that they could be different. Confusing the two is one of the most consequential mistakes you can make.

Here's the diagnostic. Imagine the rule disappearing overnight. Does the world rearrange itself, or does it stay roughly the same?

Gravity disappearing rearranges everything — physics, biology, architecture, your morning coffee. No human had a stake in gravity working the way it does; nothing was *organized around* the rule being one way rather than another. Now do the same exercise with tipping. Tipping disappearing rearranges a labor market: restaurants would have to set wages, menu prices would change, customers would pay differently, owners would absorb costs they currently externalize. A whole set of arrangements depends on tipping being there.

The things that would rearrange something if they disappeared are the things that have stakeholders — entities whose arrangements depend on the rule being one way. The things that would stay the same are closer to natural facts.

This isn't about identifying culprits. The stakeholders aren't necessarily villains, and noticing them doesn't mean the rule is wrong. It tells you the rule is contingent — which means it's the kind of thing that can be argued about, organized around, and potentially changed.

## Four examples

The questions are most useful applied. Here are four situations where running them produces a different reading than the obvious one.

### Group projects in school

Group projects are presented as coordination — students learn to work together, the workload is distributed, the teacher sees how students perform in collaborative settings. That's a real story. It isn't a complete one.

Question 1: Who benefits? Teachers (one project to grade per group), students who don't do much (they get the same grade as students who do a lot). Who pays? The student in every group who picks up the slack so the work gets done.

Question 2: From the slacker's position, the group project looks fine — actually pretty great. From the position of the conscientious student, it looks like a mechanism for transferring her labor to people who haven't done the work, with the teacher's grade as the laundering mechanism. Both readings are correct.

Question 3: Coordination *and* transfer. The coordination story is real — students do learn something about working with others. The transfer story is real too — high-effort students subsidize low-effort students through the shared grade.

Question 4: When teachers report that group projects work well, who's typically not in the conversation? The students who carried the project. They tend not to complain because the alternative — having their grade reflect only their own work — would require them to bring the situation to the teacher's attention, which they correctly perceive as a path to social difficulty.

Question 5: Suppose group projects disappeared from school tomorrow. Would the world rearrange itself? Yes — teachers would need different grading workflows, the learning content would have to be restructured, the adults who treat "group projects build collaboration skills" as obvious would have to argue for the claim instead of asserting it. The rearrangement is the giveaway: a real set of arrangements depends on the practice. It's contingent, not natural.

The reading isn't "group projects are bad." It's: they have a coordination function and a transfer function, the cost falls on a specific group, that group has been routed out of the conversation about whether the system is working, and the "they teach collaboration" framing makes the arrangement harder to question. Useful for deciding how to navigate them, when to speak up, and how to think about them as a system.

### Standardized testing

Standardized tests are presented as a neutral measure — an objective signal that lets colleges compare students from different schools fairly.

Question 1: Who benefits? Testing companies (revenue), prep companies (revenue), colleges (a cheap signal that's easier than reading transcripts carefully), students whose schools didn't prepare them well but whose families can buy preparation. Who pays? Students whose families can't afford preparation. Students whose schools don't teach to the test. Students who test poorly but learn well.

Question 2: From a student whose family pays for two years of one-on-one tutoring, the test looks like a fair measure of preparation. From a student whose family didn't know prep existed, the test looks like a check that asks "did your family know to invest in this thing?" — which they failed, through no fault of their own.

Question 3: Coordination *and* transfer. Coordination: colleges need some way to compare students at scale. Real. Transfer: a multi-billion-dollar industry sits on top of this need, moving resources from families to companies, with the gradient of who can pay shaping the outcomes.

Question 4: Everyone seems to agree this is how college admissions works. Who's not in the conversation? Students who didn't apply because they didn't think they could — who took themselves out of the system before the system had to take them out.

Question 5: Suppose standardized testing disappeared from college admissions tomorrow. Test-optional admissions experiments at hundreds of selective schools demonstrate that the world rearranges rather than collapses — colleges develop other signals, applications shift in composition, the prep industry contracts. The "we need standardized tests for fair comparison" framing turns out to be contingent on a particular admissions architecture, not a necessary feature of selecting students.

This doesn't tell you what to do about your own testing. It tells you what the system is doing — which makes it easier to think about your situation strategically rather than morally.

### Tipping at restaurants

Tipping is presented as a way to reward good service. That's the cover story. Here's what's underneath.

Question 1: Who benefits? Restaurant owners (lower posted prices, lower stated wages, the wage cost shifted to customers). Top-tier servers in expensive restaurants (high earnings). Customers who like the flexibility. Who pays? Lower-tier servers (unpredictable income; in many states the wage floor for tipped workers is far below the regular minimum because tips are assumed to fill the gap). Customers (paying a price that doesn't appear on the menu). Servers in categories that get tipped less for reasons that have nothing to do with their service quality.

Question 2: From the position of a server who gets tipped poorly — by gender, by appearance, by section assignment, by the prejudices of the customers who happen to walk in — tipping looks less like reward for service and more like a system that exposes you to those prejudices, with no employer protection against the variance.

Question 3: Coordination (matches customer experience to server compensation) *and* transfer (shifts wage cost from employer to customer, suppresses posted wages, externalizes wage variance to workers).

Question 4: Customers and owners generally agree the system works. Workers' opinions are more split than the consensus suggests. Workers who'd prefer a flat wage tend to be in lower-tipping categories — the ones whose voices weight least in the conversation about whether the system is fine.

Question 5: Suppose tipping disappeared from American restaurants tomorrow. Would the world rearrange itself? Yes — owners would set wages, menu prices would adjust upward, the tipped-minimum-wage exemption would lose its function, customers would pay differently. Other countries have restaurants without tipping; the practice is contingent on the U.S. labor market structure that makes it advantageous for owners.

What the analysis tells you isn't "don't tip." Within the system, tipping well is the right move; the system has externalized the wage to you, and the server doesn't get to opt out. It tells you what the system is, which lets you see future legislative debates about it more clearly.

### "You need to go to college"

This one is harder because the framing is so deeply embedded that it doesn't feel like framing.

Question 1: Who benefits when "everyone needs college" is treated as obvious? Colleges and universities (enrollment, tuition revenue). Lenders (loan volume). Employers using the degree as a cheap filter (cost of filtering transferred to applicants, who pay for college). Who pays? Students who take on debt for degrees that don't pay back. Students who'd be better off in skilled trades but took the prestige path. Workers in trades that suffer from underfilling because the talent went to college.

Question 2: From the position of a student four years out, with debt, with a degree that didn't open the doors that were promised, the "everyone needs college" framing looks less like advice and more like a system that needed students to fill it.

Question 3: Coordination (signals to employers that you can complete a multi-year structured task) *and* transfer (moves significant resources from students to institutions, with the cost falling hardest on students whose families can least afford it).

Question 4: When adults agree college is necessary, who's missing from the conversation? Students who started college and didn't finish — typically more than a third of any starting cohort, often with debt and no degree. Graduates working in fields that didn't require the degree. Skilled trade workers who'd have been steered into something else if they'd grown up under "everyone needs college."

Question 5: Suppose the assumption "you need a college degree" disappeared from American culture tomorrow. The rearrangement would be enormous: universities would shrink, lenders would lose a market, employers would have to develop their own filtering systems, high schools would measure their success differently. The size of the rearrangement is the diagnostic. Something this many entities are organized around isn't a natural fact; it's a contingent arrangement, which means it can be reasoned about as a choice.

The reading isn't "don't go to college." For many people college is the right move. It's that the framing as obviously, universally necessary is doing work — work that benefits some entities more than others — and recognizing that lets you make a college decision based on what's good for you rather than what's good for the system.

## What this is and isn't

These five questions don't give you answers. They give you a way to see what's actually being decided. The answer to "should I do X" still depends on your values, your situation, and what you can afford. What the questions do is make sure you're answering the right question — not the one the situation has been packaged to look like.

The questions also don't always change your mind. Sometimes you ask them and conclude the surface story is roughly right. That's a real outcome, and it's worth more than not having asked.

This discipline is for situations that warrant the effort. Consequential decisions, structural arrangements you're being asked to accept as natural, claims that something is just how things work. It's overkill for choosing a sandwich. Running it on every micro-interaction would be exhausting and would dull the move's edge for the cases that matter. The skill is partly knowing when to deploy it.

There's a harder thing to say, which is that the questions are most resisted exactly where they're most useful: on systems you benefit from. This isn't a personal failing. It's a feature of how perception works. Your position shapes what you see, and the position you're in is the one you can't fully see from. The arrangement that pays your salary, the credential that opens your doors, the rule that gives you the seat at the table — these are the cases where the discipline is hardest to run honestly, because the surface story flatters you and questioning it costs you something. Most people run these questions on other people's institutions. Running them on your own is the harder move and the more valuable one.

One thing helps with this, which is time. The questions you ask now can miss things that only show up by comparing now to before. An arrangement that was clearly coordination ten years ago might be doing more transfer now — institutions drift, capture happens, mission creeps. Your own reading of an arrangement might have shifted in ways you can't see from inside your current view. Running these questions today on a situation, and running them again on the same situation later — including on your own past readings of it — catches things that any single-moment application misses. The discipline is hardest to apply to yourself in the moment; it gets easier across time, by comparing what you used to think about something to what you think now and asking what changed.

You don't have to be combative about any of this. The point isn't to dismantle every institution you encounter. It's to see clearly enough that, when you decide how to engage with one, you're making the decision based on what's actually there rather than on what you've been told is there.

Five questions. Memorize them. Run them on the consequential things. Run them especially on the arrangements you benefit from. Run them again later. The point isn't to reach a verdict; it's to make sure that when you do reach one, it's yours.
