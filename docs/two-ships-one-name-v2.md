# Two Ships, One Name: Reference and the Ship of Theseus

## Abstract

The Ship of Theseus is widely regarded as a paradox that dissolves once we fix a criterion of identity and apply it consistently. I argue that this verdict is correct about one layer of the puzzle and silently wrong about the puzzle. Fixing a criterion does not reconcile the two standard criteria of identity—material continuity and continuity of form—it merely selects one and suppresses the other; the conflict, being a difference in what makes identity-claims true rather than a case of vagueness, survives any discipline about the criterion. I then make a positive claim that the literature has not isolated. The puzzle's two canonical versions are differently *structured*: Plutarch's gradual-replacement version is a dispute over the *predication* of identity to a single object, whereas Hobbes's reassembly version is a dispute over the *reference* of a single name across two objects. The second becomes a genuine mutual exclusion only under a further assumption—that "the Ship of Theseus" has exactly one bearer—and that assumption is a free parameter, not a consequence of either criterion. The difference between the versions is therefore induced by a semantic constraint that the case-by-case literature leaves implicit, and the choice of that constraint is orthogonal to the choice of criterion. I close by turning the result on the dissolution strategy itself: "fix the frame and the paradox disappears" presents a criterion-selection as though it obviated the metaphysics, when it merely declares a reference frame whose privilege is stipulated, not earned.

## 1. The example that was supposed to be easy

In a dry dock in the Piraeus a shipwright stacks the planks he has been quietly saving—every timber pulled from Theseus's ship as it rotted, one per voyage, for ten years. Out in the Aegean the ship he pulled them from is still sailing, sound, every board new. Two hulls, one name. He has not yet decided which is lying.

Plutarch records the original puzzle as a settled fixture of Athenian philosophical life: the ship preserved down to the time of Demetrius Phalereus, its decayed planks replaced one at a time, until the question forces itself—is it the same ship? One school held that it remained the same; another that it did not. The puzzle survives because the disagreement does.

The modern diagnostic treatment of the puzzle has a clean answer, and the answer is correct as far as it reaches. The contradiction is generated by *drift*: the predicate "the same ship" is reapplied at each replacement while the criterion for sameness silently mutates. Fix the criterion at the outset—decide that "the ship" denotes its matter, or that it denotes its continuous functional organisation—hold that criterion across every plank, and the paradox dissolves. No mystery survives strict discipline about the criterion. On this view the Ship of Theseus is the textbook case of a pseudo-problem cured by conceptual hygiene.

I will argue that this account is right about one layer of the object and silently wrong about the object. I claim three things. First, fixing a criterion does not *reconcile* the two criteria of identity; it selects one and suppresses the other, and the conflict between them is not the kind of thing fixing a criterion can touch. Second—and this is the positive contribution—the two canonical versions of the puzzle are not the same problem at two levels of difficulty; they are differently structured, and the structural difference is induced by an assumption about how the name "the Ship of Theseus" refers, an assumption that is independent of the criterion one adopts. Third, and reflexively, the puzzle most often offered as the exemplary case of dissolution is in fact a compact demonstration of why the dissolution strategy misdescribes what it does.

The scope is deliberately narrow. This is an account of the puzzle's structure, not a theory of personal identity and not a verdict on which ship is "really" Theseus's. I take no position on the latter, and—a point I make precise in §2—I take no position on whether the underlying conflict of grounds is in principle decidable. My claim is the weaker and more secure one: that *this puzzle* does not decide it, and that the dissolution strategy mistakes not deciding it for not needing to.

## 2. What fixing the criterion actually does

Watch the dissolution move in slow motion. You are handed a flickering object and told to stop the flicker by choosing a criterion. You choose matter: "the ship" is the timber of which it is composed. The answer is now determinate—the all-new ship in the Aegean is a different ship, cleanly, no paradox. Or you choose form: "the ship" is the continuous functional organisation that persists through replacement of parts. Again determinate—the same ship, cleanly, no paradox. Either way the flicker stops.

But notice *what* stopped it. There were two criteria in the room—material continuity and continuity of form—and they assign different *truth-makers* to one and the same identity-claim. For "this is the same ship as Theseus's," the material criterion makes the truth-maker the persistence of constituents; the formal criterion makes it the persistence of organisation *through* the replacement of constituents. This is the sense of incompatibility I mean, and it is worth being explicit about it, because the claim is easy to overstate. I do not claim that no coherent view could combine the two. A four-dimensionalist may say that matter and form are two descriptions of one spacetime worm; a hylomorphist may treat form as a higher-level property realised in matter rather than a rival to it. Such views do not *abolish* the conflict; they *relocate* it—into a question about which description is identity-conferring, or about whether the realising matter or the realised form fixes persistence. What I claim is the modest version sufficient for the structural argument: *within ordinary endurantist usage*, where the question is which of two truth-makers settles a claim of numerical identity over time, the material and formal criteria are prima facie incompatible—they cannot both be the truth-maker for the same claim without one being made to defer to the other. Fixing a criterion does not adjudicate that deference. It selects one truth-maker and stops asking. The conflict is still in the room; you have agreed not to look at it.

This is the difference between dissolving a paradox and choosing a side in one. And here a careful reader presses: why is selecting the correct criterion not simply what resolution *amounts to* in metaphysics? The answer fixes the paper's commitments, so I state it flatly. Ordinary theory-choice in metaphysics supplies *reasons* for one ground over another—parsimony, fit with our best physics, treatment of related cases—and thereby earns its verdict. The dissolution strategy supplies no such reasons. It does not argue that matter is the truth-maker rather than form; it observes that *once you pick one* the flicker stops, and presents the stopping as the resolution. That is theory-choice with the argument deleted and the result kept. I do not claim the conflict of grounds is in principle undecidable; that would be a metaphysical thesis I have not earned. I claim only that the Ship of Theseus does not decide it, and that the dissolution move converts "undecided here" into "needs no deciding."

The plank replacement—the genuine drift—is the *occasion* that surfaces this conflict, not its *source*. The source is the pair of incompatible truth-makers, and that pair survives any amount of discipline about the criterion, because such discipline operates on the drift and the conflict is not made of drift. The deflationary rejoinder—that the whole affair is a Sorites in a hull, vagueness about the threshold proportion of replaced planks—handles the question *when* identity lapses. It does not handle the question *in virtue of what* it lapses. One can grant that there is no fact about the precise plank at which identity fails, and the deeper disagreement—whether identity is made true by stuff or by structure—remains entirely untouched.

## 3. The ship as a contested term

It helps to regiment the object. "The Ship of Theseus" is a contested term: a designation participants point to when asked what is at stake. Material continuity and continuity of form are two *criteria* for the application of that term, each fixing a different truth-maker for identity. The object is then not one thing oscillating between two answers; it is one disputed term carrying two ratified interpretations, each internally coherent.

The regimentation changes the question the puzzle is asking. The naïve question—*which criterion is correct?*—is a dispute between rival truth-makers that this puzzle does not settle (§2). The productive question is the one the regimentation makes available: *what is the structural relation between the two criteria, and does it change across the versions of the puzzle?* It does—and the change will turn out to depend not only on the criteria but on how the name's reference is fixed. That the bearer of the dispute is a *term* rather than an *object* is not a bookkeeping nicety; it becomes decisive in the next section, where the semantics of the term, and not the criteria it expresses, does the structural work.

## 4. Why Hobbes's ship is a differently structured problem

Here is the move the standard, case-by-case treatments cannot make, because they lack the instrument for it.

It will help to state the operative principle before applying it, since the literature tends to leave it buried.

> **Exclusion Principle.** Two criteria for a term *exclude* one another, relative to a case, just in case the case cannot satisfy both *under one and the same assignment of reference to the term*. Where the term's reference is fixed by a constraint requiring exactly one bearer, criteria that nominate different bearers exclude one another. Where the term's reference is permitted to vary, the same criteria can both be satisfied, each under a different assignment, and do not exclude.

The uniqueness constraint is what matters here, not rigid designation specifically. A definite-description semantics—"the ship that was originally Theseus's"—presupposes a unique satisfier just as a rigid designator stipulates a unique bearer; either delivers the constraint, and other semantics may deliver it too. Rigid designation is one route to uniqueness, sufficient but not necessary. The principle is stated in terms of the constraint, not the route.

Now apply it, and a feature emerges that the difficulty-ranking picture conceals.

In **Plutarch's version**—gradual replacement, nothing reassembled—there is a *single* candidate object, the one hull. The two criteria are brought to bear on that one object and disagree about whether identity is *preserved*: ground identity in matter and the renewed ship is a new ship; ground it in form and it is the old one. This is a dispute over the *predication* of identity to a single object. A reasoner cannot, of course, consistently assert both "it is the same" and "it is not the same"; the two verdicts are contradictory, and I do not claim a single mind holds both in peace. What I claim is structural: each criterion, taken by itself, delivers a *consistent* verdict on the one candidate, and the disagreement is between two such self-consistent positions a reasoner may occupy—which is exactly how Plutarch reports it, one school against another, the ship sailing on under both descriptions in the city's mouth if not in any one head.

In **Hobbes's version**—the discarded planks saved and reassembled into a second hull—the structure changes, and not because the criteria change. The same two criteria are in play. What changes is that there are now *two* candidate objects, and the criteria pick out *different* ones: the material criterion points to the reassembled Piraean hull, the formal criterion to the continuously repaired Aegean hull. The question is no longer *is this one the same?* but *which of these two is the original?* The dispute has migrated from the *predication* of identity to a single object to the *reference* of a single name across two objects.

This migration is the real difference between the versions, and it is the cleaner replacement for the loose talk of "coexistence versus exclusion." Predication-disagreement and reference-disagreement are genuinely different structures, independent of how vexing either feels. But the reference-disagreement becomes a *contradiction*—a genuine mutual exclusion—only when the Exclusion Principle's uniqueness constraint is in force. If "the Ship of Theseus" must have exactly one bearer, two hulls cannot both bear it, the criteria's nominations are jointly unsatisfiable, and they exclude. If the name's reference may vary by context—the registry, salvage law, the museum, and sentiment each fixing reference differently—then both hulls may bear the name under different assignments, and the nominations no longer collide. The reassembly, then, does not by itself *produce* mutual exclusion. It produces a reference-dispute; the uniqueness constraint converts that dispute into exclusion.

This dissolves the natural objection that Hobbes himself feels no conflict, since he holds the formal criterion and is done. Of course he feels none: Hobbes selects a criterion *and* a uniquely-referring reading of the name, and the exclusion is downstream of the latter. His preference for the continuously repaired ship, on grounds of spatial and temporal continuity, is the selection of a criterion together with a referential convention, declared as the reference frame—a stipulation at the origin, position-dependent, no more privileged than the verdict of the Athenians who took the other side.

### 4.1 Is the semantics autonomous, or smuggled in from the criterion?

The deepest objection to the foregoing is that the semantic parameter is not free at all. A defender of the case-by-case picture may say: the choice of referential semantics is *dictated* by the criterion one already holds. Someone who holds the formal criterion will insist that "the Ship of Theseus" is uniquely referring, because the ship's identity is singular and continuous; someone who holds the material criterion will tolerate contextual variation, because matter can be scattered and re-gathered. If that is right, the semantic constraint is a consequence of the criterion, "reference-relative" collapses into "criterion-relative," and the paper has merely renamed the dissolution view.

I grant the observation and deny the entailment. There is indeed a *natural pairing*—form pulls toward uniqueness, matter toward tolerance of scatter—and noticing it is to the objector's credit. But a natural pairing is a defeasible tendency, not a logical dependence, and the off-diagonal cases are coherent and occupiable. Consider all four combinations of criterion and semantics:

- *Material criterion, unique reference.* "The ship just is its original timber; there is exactly one such collection; it is now the reassembled Piraean hull, and the repaired hull is an impostor." A matter-theorist who insists on a single bearer. Perfectly coherent.
- *Material criterion, contextual reference.* "Materially the original planks compose the reassembled hull; but for daily use the name picks out the working ship, for provenance the relic." A matter-theorist who is a contextualist about the name.
- *Formal criterion, unique reference.* Hobbes. One bearer, the continuously repaired ship.
- *Formal criterion, contextual reference.* "Functionally the repaired ship is primary; but the name floats—the museum may bestow it on whichever hull serves the exhibit."

All four are inhabitable. The criterion fixes which truth-maker settles *identity*; it does not fix whether the *name* tolerates more than one bearer. Those are answers to different questions—what makes identity-claims true, and how many things one term may denote—and a position on the first does not entail a position on the second. The objector has detected the gravitational pull between certain pairings and mistaken it for a chain. That the pull is real is itself worth recording, because it explains why the dependence *looks* compulsory and why the semantic parameter has stayed invisible: the typical theorist slides from criterion to matching semantics without noticing the second choice was a choice. Making the second choice visible, and showing it is unforced, is the contribution.

### 4.2 Recap

The criteria are identical across the two versions; what differs is structure. Plutarch poses a predication-dispute over one object; Hobbes poses a reference-dispute over two. The reference-dispute hardens into mutual exclusion only under a uniqueness constraint on the name, and that constraint is a free parameter, orthogonal to the criterion. The case-by-case tradition cannot locate this joint, because it collapses the two criteria into a single flicker to be stopped and never isolates the referential assumption that decides whether the criteria collide. To say "form versus matter" is to operate one level below the joint, and to have no term at all for the parameter that turns a reference-dispute into a contradiction.

## 5. A stronger claim I decline to make

It is tempting to push the thesis one step further, and the boundary is instructive.

The further claim would be that our practices of individuating ships—the way registries, owners, and observers track "the same ship" across repair—do not merely *favour* the formal criterion but *defeat* the material one, so that the material criterion is eliminated rather than suppressed. I decline this claim, but I want to decline it more carefully than by calling it a category error, because that charge is too quick and a real methodological position lies underneath.

Distinguish two ways practice might bear on the metaphysics. On the first, our success in re-identifying objects is *defeasible evidence* about what identity consists in: a four-dimensionalist or stage theorist may hold, reasonably, that a metaphysics answerable to nothing in our practice of reidentification is idle, and that the smoothness with which we track functional continuity is data. This is a legitimate methodological view, and I do not refute it; the paper is neutral on whether practice is evidence for a ground. On the second reading, practice does not merely *evidence* the correct ground but *constitutes or refutes* it—our individuating habits make the formal criterion true and the material one false. *That* is the move I decline, and the reason is the one the careful reader will already have: how we find it useful to pick ships out is a fact about us, and which truth-maker settles numerical identity is not, and the second cannot be read straight off the first without an argument that the paper has not been given and does not supply. The material criterion is therefore, by my lights, unchosen rather than defeated.

The point of marking this boundary is not throat-clearing; it reinforces the central thesis. The criteria remain in *live* conflict rather than collapsing into a single surviving ground. If practice had defeated the material criterion, the Hobbes case would no longer host two genuine nominations and §4 would have nothing to carve. It hosts two because the conflict is suppressed, not settled.

## 6. The objection that has to land: "this is old philosophy in new clothes"

The strongest move against this paper is not a rebuttal of any particular claim. It is a reclassification of the whole enterprise, and a charitable critic will make it, because from where they stand it is largely true. *None of this is new. The matter/form distinction is Aristotle's; the reassembly variant and the preference for continuity are Hobbes's; Locke and Hume and centuries of commentary have mapped this terrain. You have renamed known parts and presented the renaming as a discovery.*

That blow should land before the reply arrives, because it is mostly right. The criteria are ancient; the variant is Hobbes's; the verdicts are taken. If the contribution were "there are two criteria for identity," the critic would win outright.

The contribution is elsewhere, and the reclassification fails at one point. The tradition names the two criteria and *argues for one of them*. What it does not supply is an account of *why Plutarch's puzzle and Hobbes's puzzle are differently structured rather than the same problem at two settings of difficulty.* Both deploy the identical pair of criteria. If the criteria were the whole story, the two versions would share a structure and differ only in how hard they feel. They do not share a structure: one is a predication-dispute over a single object, the other a reference-dispute over two, and the latter becomes a contradiction only under a uniqueness constraint that is no part of either criterion. "Form versus matter" cannot state this, because it has no term for reference and no term for the constraint.

I should be careful with the rhetoric here, because it is easy to oversell. I do not claim a metaphysical chasm between the two versions—two incommensurable kinds of thing. I claim a difference of *structure* in the dispute, plus a second, freely-set parameter (the semantics of the name) that the tradition silently welded to the first. Put to the plain-language test, the surviving claim needs no apparatus: *the two-ship puzzle is structured differently from the one-ship puzzle, because the disagreement is now about which of two things a single name picks out rather than about whether one thing has persisted—and whether that disagreement is a flat contradiction depends not on the criteria, which are the same in both, but on whether the name is required to pick out exactly one thing.* What the regimentation adds is that the last clause names a parameter we are free to set either way, and usually set without noticing.

Two residual objections deserve a sentence each. To "you have merely relocated the problem into language": I have not reduced the metaphysical conflict to a semantic one. The conflict of grounds is present and untouched in *both* versions; the semantic assumption governs only whether it *surfaces* as a contradiction or as parallel verdicts. Semantics determines the expression of the conflict, not its existence. To "you have solved nothing": correct, and by design. For a reader who wants to be told which hull is Theseus's, this contributes nothing; it maps the structure of a disagreement and declines the verdict. But explaining *why two cases with identical criteria are differently structured* is itself explanatory progress, and it is progress the partisan answers do not contain.

## 7. What the founding example says about where we started

The puzzle is most often offered as the clean case of a paradox that is really a confusion, dissolved by fixing the criterion. Turn the analysis back on that classification and it does not survive.

The Ship of Theseus is the easy case only on its surface. The surface is the plank drift and the threshold vagueness—genuinely a matter of conceptual hygiene, genuinely dissolvable. Beneath it lies the conflict of incompatible truth-makers that fixing a criterion can only suppress (§2). And across its versions, "which ship is real" decomposes into *real under which criterion, and under which semantics for the name* (§4). The puzzle was never a single-mechanism affair; it was several mechanisms wearing one hull, and calling it the easy case was the move that hid the rest.

The dissolution stance—*fix the criterion and the paradox disappears*—rests on a presupposition the analysis exposes, and here is the step that makes the charge bite *for this puzzle in particular* rather than against conceptual analysis in general. A defender will say, rightly, that choosing a framework is just what analysis does. Granted. But ordinary analysis chooses a framework *and argues for it*, and presents the verdict as resting on those reasons. The dissolution treatment of the Ship of Theseus does neither: it offers no reason to prefer matter to form, and it presents the disappearance of the flicker—a consequence of having picked *either*—as though no metaphysical question remained to be argued. It is not the choosing that is illicit; it is the dressing of an unargued choice as a dissolution that needs no choosing. The disagreement that remains after the criterion is fixed is not a residue to be cleaned away; it is the metaphysics, still standing.

The Ship of Theseus was never the puzzle that dissolves when you fix the frame. It is the puzzle that shows how fixing the frame conceals which criterion you suppressed—and, in the two-ship case, which semantics you assumed.

## Remaining questions

Three questions remain open, of three different kinds. *First*, whether the Exclusion Principle of §4 can be made fully formal—whether, in a two-dimensional or contextualist semantics that takes the uniqueness constraint as a parameter, one can exhibit the dispute moving from reference-disagreement to contradiction as the parameter is switched on. This is a technical question with a determinate answer, and §4.1 commits to the further prediction that the parameter is independent of the criterion, which such a formalisation could confirm or refute. *Second*, "which is the real ship?" is not one question but a family, each indexed to a purpose—legal title, sentimental continuity, museological authenticity, material provenance—each potentially picking out a different object; its felt depth at this point is underspecification, and it resolves on indexing, the same indexing that fixes the semantic parameter in §4. *Third*, whether identity is made true by constituent matter or by continuity of form is a conflict of grounds that this puzzle does not settle; whether it is settleable *at all*, by other means, is a question on which this paper is deliberately silent.
