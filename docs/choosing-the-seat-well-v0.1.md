# Choosing the Seat Well

**A first draft of the successor problem to the Seat Theorem: if no contentful verdict can be seat-free, by what criteria is a seat well-chosen rather than merely chosen?**

**Version: v0.1 (first draft)**

---

## Abstract

The Seat Theorem establishes that no contentful verdict is seat-free: every answer to an open question is issued from a standpoint, and the only coherent residue of neutrality is *declaration* — naming the seat one occupies. But declaration is a gate, not a metric. It moves a seat from *concealed* to *eligible-for-evaluation*; it says nothing about quality. A declared seat can be terrible. This note takes up the question declaration leaves open: what makes a seat well-chosen?

The answer cannot be a neutral standard of seat-quality, because such a standard would itself be a contentful verdict, hence seated — the theorem forbids it. So the question is relocated rather than answered in the form it was asked. Quality-of-seat is not a one-place predicate (*good simpliciter*) but a two-place relation (*good for a declared purpose*). Given that relocation, four structural virtues are forced on any seat that is to serve any purpose at all — **stability, declared depth, generativity, reversibility** — and a fifth, **fitness to a declared purpose**, is the input the other four are measured against. The fifth is irreducible: the purpose is chosen, contentful, and seated, and the regress the Seat Theorem ran on the situation runs here too. This is not a defect. It is the theorem keeping its word. Declaration was the gate; fitness-to-declared-purpose is the metric; and purpose is the floor under the floor.

This note states the five and the relocation. It does not prove the list complete or the virtues independent; that limitation is itself declared (§7).

## 1. Why declaration is not enough

The Seat Theorem's constructive corollary was that a contentful verdict which does not show its seat is not neutral but concealed, and concealment is the error. This is correct and it is thin. It draws one line — between *concealed* and *declared* — and stops. On the declared side of that line sit excellent seats and disastrous ones, indistinguishable to a discipline that only checks whether the seat is named.

So declaration is necessary and empty: necessary, because an undeclared seat cannot be evaluated at all; empty, because declaring a seat does nothing to recommend it. The work the theorem leaves undone is the work on the declared side of the line — the second cut, between *well-chosen* and *merely chosen*.

## 2. The constraint the theorem imposes on its own successor

Any criterion of seat-quality is a contentful claim. By the Coupling Theorem it is therefore seated: there is no standpoint-free standard of what makes a standpoint good. This is not a wrinkle to be smoothed; it is the governing fact, and a successor that ignored it would be committing the no-seat pose at one level up — offering neutral criteria for the impossibility of neutral verdicts.

The consequence is a forced change of form. *Good seat* cannot be a property a seat has by itself. It must be a relation between a seat and something further — and the only candidate that is not itself an infinite regress of evaluations is a **purpose**: a declared statement of what the inquiry is *for*. With a purpose fixed, "well-chosen" acquires teeth; without one, the virtues below are unconstrained (maximal stability is achieved by trivial seats, maximal generativity by promiscuous ones — only a purpose sets the right amount of each). The relocation is therefore not optional. It is what the theorem permits in place of the neutral standard it forbids.

## 3. The four structural virtues

These are the virtues a seat needs to serve *any* purpose. Each is the negation of a specific way a declared seat can still fail.

### 3.1 Stability

*Does the verdict survive small perturbation of the seat?*

A seat sitting on a threshold — where a hair's change of standpoint flips the verdict — is badly chosen even when fully declared, because the verdict is then an artifact of where a dial happened to land rather than a feature the seat reveals. A well-chosen seat sits in a *basin*: nearby standpoints return nearby verdicts. The failure this negates is the *threshold artifact* — a result that looks like a finding but is a coincidence of placement.

### 3.2 Declared depth

*Is the seat declared all the way to where the work happens?*

Declaration is binary; depth is not. A seat can be announced at the surface while its real load is carried by an undeclared sub-choice two levels down. The failure this negates is *shallow declaration*: a seat that passes the theorem's honesty gate at the surface while the verdict secretly rides a parameter that was never named. The test is not "is it declared" but "is it declared at the level that does the work." This is the only one of the four that is about declaration — it is the depth-of-declaration test, the demanding version of the gate the theorem already installed.

### 3.3 Generativity

*Does the seat let you see things, or only license the verdict it was chosen to license?*

A seat adopted purely to produce a predetermined answer is *ad hoc*: it earns nothing beyond the conclusion it was built for. A seat that pays for itself by revealing structure you were not looking for — opening questions, predicting features, traveling to cases it was not designed for — is earning its keep. The failure this negates is the *excuse*: a standpoint that is all license and no yield. Generativity is the difference between a seat and a rationalization.

### 3.4 Reversibility

*Can you adopt the seat, run it, and get back out to compare it against rivals?*

A seat that forecloses its own audit is badly chosen however well it is declared. The pathological case is the standpoint that rules out, *from within itself*, every standard that could evaluate it — so that once occupied, it cannot be compared, only inhabited. A well-chosen seat permits its own audit: it does not consume the instruments that would grade it. The failure this negates is the *roach motel* — a standpoint you can enter but not assess from, because entering it disqualified the assessment.

## 4. The fifth: fitness to a declared purpose

The four structural virtues float until a purpose anchors them. *Stable at what resolution. Generative toward what domain. Reversible against which rivals.* The fifth requirement is that the seat name what it is for, because that declaration is what converts the other four from free-floating goods into binding constraints with a determinate right amount.

And here the honesty of the whole construction lives: **the purpose is the bottom.** It is the one input not derivable from anything beneath it. It is chosen; it is contentful; it is therefore seated; and the regress the Seat Theorem ran on the situation runs here unchanged — *why that purpose?* answers only from a higher purpose, and so upward without a neutral floor.

This does not undermine the account. It completes it in the only way the theorem allows. A well-chosen seat is one that is stable, declared-to-depth, generative, and reversible *with respect to a purpose that is itself declared* — and the final move the discipline demands is therefore a second declaration on top of the first. Not only *here is my seat*, but *here is what my seat is for*. The first declaration makes the seat honest. The second makes it gradeable.

## 5. The two cuts

The full discipline now draws two lines, not one:

1. **Concealed | Declared** — the Seat Theorem's cut. Honesty. Crossed by naming the seat.
2. **Merely chosen | Well-chosen** — this note's cut. Quality. Crossed by fitness to a declared purpose, measured through the four structural virtues.

The first cut is binary and cheap. The second is graded and expensive, and it is only available on the declared side of the first. A concealed seat cannot be well-chosen, because it cannot be evaluated at all; that is why honesty is prior to quality, and why declaration, though empty as a metric, is indispensable as a gate.

## 6. What this is for (the note's own purpose, declared)

The purpose of this note is to give the *evaluative* successor to a *negative* result — to convert "neutrality is impossible" from a counsel of despair into a working standard. Its intended use is diagnostic: confronted with a contentful verdict and its declared seat, ask the five questions and locate the seat on the second cut. Its intended non-use is as a neutral arbiter; it is not one, and §2 explains why it cannot be.

## 7. The note's own seat, and its open edges

Declared rather than hidden, as the subject requires.

**The seat.** This note is written from a purpose — *make lost neutrality into a usable standard* — and a different purpose would weight the virtues differently or surface a sixth. That purpose is the seat; it is stated in §6 so that the account can be graded by its own second cut.

**The undischarged count.** The Seat Theorem confessed that it runs on an un-audited individuation of "parameter" and "content." That confession recurs here one level up: this note asserts that there are *four* structural virtues plus the purpose, and it does not prove the list complete or the members independent. The parameter-count the theorem could not audit for itself, the successor cannot audit for its own virtues.

**A specific suspected non-independence.** Stability (§3.1) and generativity (§3.3) may not be independent. A seat that is generative across a domain is plausibly stable within it, and a perfectly stable seat that generates nothing may be a degenerate limit of generativity rather than a separate virtue. Whether the two collapse into one — *fertility under perturbation* — or remain distinct is unresolved, and is the first place a second draft should press.

**The conditional, not categorical, status.** Every claim here is conditional on the Seat Theorem, which is conditional on P3 (the situation does not self-partition), which a direct realist denies. The successor inherits the predecessor's one live external dependency and adds no new escape from it.
