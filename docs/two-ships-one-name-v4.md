# Two Ships, One Name: Identity Criteria and the Uniqueness Constraint

## Abstract

The Ship of Theseus is widely regarded as a paradox that dissolves once we fix a criterion of identity and apply it consistently. I argue that this verdict is correct about one layer of the puzzle and silently wrong about the puzzle. Fixing a criterion does not reconcile the two standard criteria of identity—material continuity and continuity of form—it selects one and suppresses the other; the conflict, being a difference in what makes identity-claims true rather than a case of vagueness, survives any discipline about the criterion. I then make a positive claim the literature has not isolated explicitly. The puzzle's two canonical versions are differently *structured*: Plutarch's gradual-replacement version is a dispute over the *predication* of identity to a single object, whereas Hobbes's reassembly version is a dispute over the *reference* of a single name across two objects. The second becomes a genuine mutual exclusion only under a further assumption—that the situation admits exactly one bearer of the name—and that assumption is a free parameter, not a consequence of either criterion. I argue that this generalises: the same orthogonality recurs in the statue/clay puzzle, where the analogous parameter is not a constraint on a name's reference but a constraint on object-count (monism versus the constitution view). In both, fixing the criterion leaves open an independent question of *cardinality*—how many bearers or objects are allowed—and the answer to that question, not the criterion, decides whether the two readings exclude or merely run parallel. I close by turning the result on the dissolution strategy in its strongest reconstructible form: "fix the frame and the paradox disappears" is a real solution to the drift layer that is mistaken for a solution to the whole, and that very reasonableness explains why the puzzle has proved so durable.

## 1. The example that was supposed to be easy

In a dry dock in the Piraeus a shipwright stacks the planks he has been quietly saving—every timber pulled from Theseus's ship as it rotted, one per voyage, for ten years. Out in the Aegean the ship he pulled them from is still sailing, sound, every board new. Two hulls, one name. He has not yet decided which is lying.

Plutarch records the original puzzle as a settled fixture of Athenian philosophical life: the ship preserved down to the time of Demetrius Phalereus, its decayed planks replaced one at a time, until the question forces itself—is it the same ship? One school held that it remained the same; another that it did not. The puzzle survives because the disagreement does.

The modern diagnostic treatment of the puzzle has a clean answer, and the answer is correct as far as it reaches. The contradiction is generated by *drift*: the predicate "the same ship" is reapplied at each replacement while the criterion for sameness silently mutates. Fix the criterion at the outset—decide that "the ship" denotes its matter, or that it denotes its continuous functional organisation—hold that criterion across every plank, and the paradox dissolves. No mystery survives strict discipline about the criterion. On this view the Ship of Theseus is the textbook case of a pseudo-problem cured by conceptual hygiene.

I will argue that this account is right about one layer of the object and silently wrong about the object. I claim three things. First, fixing a criterion does not *reconcile* the two criteria of identity; it selects one and suppresses the other, and the conflict between them is not the kind of thing fixing a criterion can touch. Second—and this is the positive contribution—the two canonical versions of the puzzle are not the same problem at two levels of difficulty; they are differently structured, and the structural difference turns on an assumption about cardinality that is independent of the criterion one adopts and that the case-by-case literature leaves implicit. I show that this assumption is a recurring free parameter by exhibiting it again, in a different form, in the statue/clay puzzle. Third, and reflexively, the puzzle most often offered as the exemplary case of dissolution is in fact a compact demonstration of why the dissolution strategy misdescribes what it does—and why, precisely because the strategy is reasonable, the misdescription has lasted.

The scope is deliberately narrow. This is an account of the structure of a family of identity puzzles, not a theory of personal identity and not a verdict on which ship is "really" Theseus's. I take no position on the latter, and—a point I make precise in §2—I take no position on whether the underlying conflict of grounds is in principle decidable. My claim is the weaker and more secure one: that *these puzzles* do not decide it, and that the dissolution strategy mistakes not deciding it for not needing to.

## 2. What fixing the criterion actually does

Watch the dissolution move in slow motion. You are handed a flickering object and told to stop the flicker by choosing a criterion. You choose matter: "the ship" is the timber of which it is composed. The answer is now determinate—the all-new ship in the Aegean is a different ship, cleanly, no paradox. Or you choose form: "the ship" is the continuous functional organisation that persists through replacement of parts. Again determinate—the same ship, cleanly, no paradox. Either way the flicker stops.

But notice *what* stopped it. There were two criteria in the room—material continuity and continuity of form—and they assign different *truth-makers* to one and the same identity-claim. For "this is the same ship as Theseus's," the material criterion makes the truth-maker the persistence of constituents; the formal criterion makes it the persistence of organisation *through* the replacement of constituents. The incompatibility I mean is not merely that both conditions obtain; it is that each is offered as *sufficient in its own right* to settle the numerical-identity claim, so that the situation cannot answer to both without one being made to defer to the other. This blocks the obvious escape that identity is simply overdetermined—settled twice over, by matter and by form alike. It is not overdetermined here, because the two would-be sufficient conditions deliver opposite verdicts: the material criterion withholds identity from the renewed ship that the formal criterion confers.

The claim is easy to overstate, so let me bound it. I do not claim that no coherent view could combine the two. A four-dimensionalist may say matter and form are two descriptions of one spacetime worm; a hylomorphist may treat form as a higher-level property realised in matter rather than a rival to it. Such views do not *abolish* the conflict; they *relocate* it—into a question about which description is identity-conferring, or whether the realising matter or the realised form fixes persistence. What I claim is the modest version sufficient for the structural argument: *within ordinary endurantist usage*, where the question is which of two purportedly sufficient truth-makers settles numerical identity over time, the material and formal criteria are prima facie incompatible. Fixing a criterion does not adjudicate the deference between them. It selects one truth-maker and stops asking. The conflict is still in the room; you have agreed not to look at it.

This is the difference between dissolving a paradox and choosing a side in one. And here a careful reader presses: why is selecting the correct criterion not simply what resolution *amounts to* in metaphysics? The answer fixes the paper's commitments, so I state it flatly. Ordinary metaphysics removes the inconsistency by *argued privilege*—it supplies reasons for one ground over another (parsimony, fit with our best physics, treatment of related cases) and rests its verdict on them. The dissolution strategy removes the inconsistency by *stipulation*: it gives no reason to prefer matter to form, and treats the disappearance of the flicker—a consequence of having fixed *either*—as though the choice were costless and the question closed. That is not resolution; it is theory-choice with the argument deleted and the result kept. I do not claim the conflict of grounds is in principle undecidable; that would be a metaphysical thesis I have not earned. I claim only that the Ship of Theseus does not decide it, and that the dissolution move converts "undecided here" into "needs no deciding."

The plank replacement—the genuine drift—is the *occasion* that surfaces this conflict, not its *source*. The source is the pair of incompatible truth-makers, and that pair survives any amount of discipline about the criterion, because such discipline operates on the drift and the conflict is not made of drift. The deflationary rejoinder—that the whole affair is a Sorites in a hull, vagueness about the threshold proportion of replaced planks—handles the question *when* identity lapses. It does not handle the question *in virtue of what* it lapses. One can grant that there is no fact about the precise plank at which identity fails, and the deeper disagreement—whether identity is made true by stuff or by structure—remains entirely untouched.

## 3. The ship as a contested term

It helps to regiment the object. "The Ship of Theseus" is a contested term: a designation participants point to when asked what is at stake. Material continuity and continuity of form are two *criteria* for the application of that term, each fixing a different truth-maker for identity. The object is then not one thing oscillating between two answers; it is one disputed term carrying two ratified interpretations, each internally coherent.

The regimentation changes the question the puzzle is asking. The naïve question—*which criterion is correct?*—is a dispute between rival truth-makers that the puzzle does not settle (§2). The productive question is the one the regimentation makes available: *what is the structural relation between the two criteria, and does it change across the versions of the puzzle?* It does—and the change will turn out to depend not only on the criteria but on how many bearers the situation is taken to contain. That the bearer of the dispute is a *term* rather than an *object* is not bookkeeping; it becomes decisive in the next section, where the cardinality of the term's reference, not the criteria it expresses, does the structural work.

## 4. Why Hobbes's ship is a differently structured problem—and why the same joint reappears in the clay

Here is the move the case-by-case treatments have not made explicitly, for want of the instrument.

It will help to state the operative principle before applying it.

> **Exclusion Principle.** Two criteria for a contested term *exclude* one another, relative to a case, just in case the case cannot make both *true* under one and the same cardinality assignment—one and the same answer to how many bearers the term has, or how many objects the situation contains. Where the assignment forces a single bearer (or a single object), criteria that nominate distinct bearers (or distinct objects) cannot both come out true, and so exclude. Where the assignment permits more than one, the same criteria can both come out true, each of a different bearer, and do not exclude.

Two notes on the principle. First, exclusion is a matter of *joint truth* under a fixed cardinality, not of assertion or of pragmatic acceptability; this keeps the relation metaphysical rather than merely linguistic. Second, the cardinality assignment is what matters, not any particular route to it. For a name, a single-bearer assignment may come from rigid designation, or from a definite-description semantics—"the ship that was originally Theseus's"—that presupposes a unique satisfier, or from elsewhere; rigid designation is one route, sufficient but not necessary. For an ontology, the same role is played by a thesis about how many objects occupy a region. The principle is stated in terms of the assignment, because the route varies across the family—as the next two cases show.

### 4.1 The two ships: cardinality at the level of reference

In **Plutarch's version**—gradual replacement, nothing reassembled—there is a *single* candidate object, the one hull. The two criteria are brought to bear on that one object and disagree about whether identity is *preserved*: both answer the question *does this object persist?*, the material criterion No, the formal criterion Yes. This is a dispute over the *predication* of identity to a single object. A reasoner cannot, of course, consistently assert both verdicts; they are contradictory, and I do not claim a single mind holds both in peace. What I claim is structural: each criterion, taken by itself, delivers a *consistent* verdict on the one candidate, and the disagreement is between two such self-consistent positions a reasoner may occupy—which is how Plutarch reports it, one school against another, the ship sailing on under both descriptions in the city's mouth if not in any one head. The two verdicts are not jointly true; they are jointly *maintained*, as rival positions, by a community that has not fixed a criterion.

In **Hobbes's version**—the discarded planks saved and reassembled into a second hull—the structure changes, and not because the criteria change. The same two criteria are in play, but there are now *two* candidate objects, and the criteria pick out *different* ones: the material criterion points to the reassembled Piraean hull, the formal criterion to the continuously repaired Aegean hull. The question is no longer *does this one persist?* but *which of these two is the referent of the name?* The dispute has migrated from the *predication* of identity to one object to the *reference* of one name across two objects.

This migration is the real difference between the versions, and the cleaner replacement for loose talk of "coexistence versus exclusion." But the reference-disagreement becomes a *contradiction* only when the Exclusion Principle's cardinality assignment forces a single bearer. And here a tempting objection—that the single-bearer assignment is not optional but built into the very question—turns out to make the point for me. "Which is the ship?" does indeed presuppose uniqueness; that is exactly why it forces exclusion. But "which things count as the ship?" presupposes no such thing, and is an equally admissible way to pose the puzzle. The uniqueness is therefore not in the world or in the criteria; it is in the *form of the question*, set before any answer is given. If the name's reference may vary by context—the registry, salvage law, the museum, and sentiment each fixing reference differently—both hulls may bear the name under different assignments, and the nominations no longer collide. The reassembly does not by itself *produce* exclusion; it produces a reference-dispute, and the cardinality assignment, smuggled in with the singular question, converts that dispute into exclusion. (This dissolves the objection that Hobbes himself feels no conflict: he selects a criterion *and* a single-bearer reading, and the exclusion is downstream of the latter—a stipulation at the origin, no more privileged than the verdict of the Athenians who took the other side.)

### 4.2 The statue and the clay: the same structure, the parameter shifted from reference to ontology

The same orthogonality reappears in a puzzle the case-by-case treatments file alongside Theseus, but with the cardinality parameter relocated from a name to the ontology itself. Shape a lump of clay into a statue. The two seem to share all their matter and location, yet to differ in their persistence conditions—squash it and the lump survives while the statue does not. By the indiscernibility of identicals, differing in a property is differing in identity, and there appear to be two coincident objects where common sense saw one. (Gibbard's Lumpl and Goliath are the canonical names; the constitution literature is the home of the problem.)

The criteria are the familiar pair: material persistence (identity tracks the clay) and formal persistence (identity tracks the statue-kind). The candidate bearers are the lump and the statue. But the free parameter here is not a constraint on a *name*; it is a constraint on *object-count*. The monist holds that exactly one object occupies the region; the constitution theorist holds that there are two coincident objects, the statue *constituted by* but not *identical to* the clay. The Exclusion Principle applies without alteration. Under monism—a single-object assignment—the two criteria cannot both come out true of the one object, which cannot both survive squashing and fail to, so a reasoner must privilege one persistence condition or deny that the modal difference is a real difference. Under the constitution view—a permissive assignment—the criteria run parallel: each is true, the material of the lump, the formal of the statue, no collision.

This is the second instance, and it does more than replicate the first. In Theseus the candidates are spatially distinct and share no matter at the moment of the puzzle, and the cardinality assignment falls on a *name's reference*. In the statue/clay case the candidates are spatially coincident and share all their matter, and the assignment falls on *how many objects there are*. The parameter survives a change of level, from reference to ontology—which is what suggests it is structural rather than a quirk of how proper names work. The hidden free parameter is not "the semantics of names"; it is, more generally, "how many bearers the situation is allowed."

### 4.3 Is the cardinality assignment autonomous, or smuggled in from the criterion?

The deepest objection is that the parameter is not free at all—that the cardinality one assumes is *dictated* by the criterion one already holds. The form-theorist, it is said, will insist on a single continuous bearer because identity is singular; the matter-theorist will tolerate plurality because matter scatters. In the clay: the constitution view is *adopted precisely to honour both persistence intuitions*, so it looks like a verdict the form-criterion forces. If the cardinality follows from the criterion, "cardinality-relative" collapses into "criterion-relative," and the paper has renamed the dissolution view.

I grant the observation and deny the entailment, and the two cases together make the denial vivid. There is a *natural pairing* in each—form pulls toward a single bearer, matter toward tolerance of plurality; constitution-pluralism is the view that keeps both objects. But a natural pairing is a defeasible tendency, not a logical dependence, and the off-diagonal positions are coherent and occupied. A *material* theorist can be a strict monist: "the only object here is the clay; the statue is a phase of it; there is exactly one bearer." A *formal* theorist can be permissive about bearers without abandoning the formal criterion—a stage theorist, for instance, who holds that the name picks out different temporal stages in different contexts, so that "the Ship of Theseus" may denote distinct stage-bearers under the same formal criterion of what unites a career. All four cells of {material, formal} × {one bearer, more than one} are inhabitable in both puzzles. The criterion fixes which truth-maker settles *identity*; it does not fix *how many things* the situation contains. Those are answers to different questions, and a position on the first does not entail a position on the second. The objector has detected the gravitational pull between certain pairings and mistaken it for a chain. That the pull is real is itself worth recording: it explains why the dependence *looks* compulsory, and why the parameter has stayed implicit—the typical theorist slides from criterion to matching cardinality without noticing the second choice was a choice. Making it visible, and showing it unforced, is the contribution.

### 4.4 A schema, and one caution it makes visible

The structure can be compressed.

> Let $C_m$ and $C_f$ be the material and formal criteria, each offered as sufficient in its own right to settle numerical identity. Let $\kappa$ be the cardinality the situation is allowed—the number of bearers a name may have, or objects a region may contain—with $\kappa = 1$ (unique) or $\kappa \geq 1$ (permissive).
> - **One candidate** (Plutarch): $C_m$ and $C_f$ deliver contradictory predications of the single object $O$. The dispute is over predication; $\kappa$ does not yet arise, there being only one candidate.
> - **Two candidates** (Hobbes: distinct hulls $O_1, O_2$; statue/clay: coincident $O_s, O_c$): $C_m$ and $C_f$ nominate different bearers. Under $\kappa = 1$ they cannot both be true—**exclusion**. Under $\kappa \geq 1$ each is true of its own bearer—**parallel verdicts, no collision**.
>
> The criterion fixes the truth-maker; $\kappa$ fixes whether the criteria's outputs collide; the two are set independently (§4.3).

The schema earns its place by making one caution unhideable rather than by hiding it. The variable $\kappa$ ranges over a name's referents in the Hobbes case and over a region's objects in the statue/clay case. Treating these as values of a *single* parameter is the paper's unifying bet, not a proven identity. It may be that reference-cardinality and object-cardinality are one variable wearing two costumes, or it may be that they are two structurally analogous parameters that happen to toggle exclusion the same way. The independence claim of §4.3 is what a decisive case would test: a puzzle that crosses the two axes—distinct candidates contested under a count noun rather than a name, or coincident candidates contested under a name—would show whether the toggle behaves identically across the crossing. I record the unification as a commitment, not a result. What does not depend on it is already secured: in each puzzle taken on its own, fixing the criterion leaves a cardinality question open, and the answer to *that* question, not the criterion, decides the collision.

So the case-by-case tradition lacks the resources to state the joint in this form. It names the criteria and argues for one; it collapses the cardinality question into the criterion and so never isolates the parameter that decides whether the criteria collide. "Form versus matter" operates one level below the joint, and has no term for the parameter that toggles it.

## 5. A stronger claim I decline to make

It is tempting to push the thesis further, and the boundary is instructive. The further claim would be that our practices of individuating ships—how registries, owners, and observers track "the same ship" across repair—do not merely *favour* the formal criterion but *defeat* the material one, eliminating rather than suppressing it. I decline this, but not by calling it a category error, because a real methodological position lies underneath.

Distinguish two ways practice might bear on the metaphysics. On the first, our success in re-identifying objects is *defeasible evidence* about what identity consists in: a four-dimensionalist or stage theorist may reasonably hold that a metaphysics answerable to nothing in our practice is idle, and that the smoothness with which we track functional continuity is data. This is a legitimate view, and I do not refute it; the paper is neutral on whether practice is evidence for a ground. On the second reading, practice does not merely *evidence* the correct ground but *constitutes or refutes* it. That is the move I decline: how we find it useful to pick objects out is a fact about us, and which truth-maker settles numerical identity is not, and the second cannot be read off the first without an argument the paper has not been given. The material criterion is therefore, by my lights, unchosen rather than defeated—which is just to say the criteria remain in live conflict. Had practice defeated the material criterion, the Hobbes case would no longer host two genuine nominations and §4 would have nothing to carve.

## 6. The objection that has to land: "this is old philosophy in new clothes"

The strongest move against this paper is not a rebuttal of any claim but a reclassification of the enterprise, and a charitable critic will make it, because from where they stand it is largely true. *None of this is new. The matter/form distinction is Aristotle's; the reassembly variant and the preference for continuity are Hobbes's; the statue and clay are the constitution theorists'; Locke and Hume and centuries of commentary have mapped this terrain. You have renamed known parts and called the renaming a discovery.*

That blow should land before the reply arrives, because it is mostly right. The criteria are ancient; the variants are not mine; the verdicts are taken. If the contribution were "there are two criteria for identity," the critic would win outright.

The contribution is elsewhere, and the reclassification fails at one point. The tradition names the two criteria and *argues for one of them*. What it does not supply is an account of *why the versions and cousins of the puzzle are differently structured rather than the same problem at different difficulties.* All of them deploy the identical pair of criteria. If the criteria were the whole story, the cases would share a structure and differ only in how hard they feel. They do not share a structure—predication over one object, reference over two, count over one region—and in each the disagreement hardens into contradiction only under a cardinality assignment that is no part of either criterion. "Form versus matter" cannot state this, because it has no term for cardinality and no term for the parameter that toggles it.

I should be careful with the rhetoric, because it is easy to oversell. I do not claim a metaphysical chasm between the cases—incommensurable kinds of thing. I claim a difference of *structure* in the dispute, plus a second, freely-set parameter—cardinality—that the tradition silently welded to the first. Put plainly, the surviving claim needs no apparatus: *the two-ship puzzle is structured differently from the one-ship puzzle, because the disagreement is now about which of two things a single name picks out rather than whether one thing persisted—and whether that disagreement is a flat contradiction depends not on the criteria, which are the same in both, but on whether the situation is allowed only one bearer; and the same is true of the statue and its clay, one level down.*

Two residual objections, a sentence each. To "you have merely relocated the problem into language": I have not reduced the metaphysical conflict to a semantic one; the conflict of grounds is present and untouched in every version, and the cardinality assignment governs only whether it *surfaces* as a contradiction or as parallel verdicts—expression, not existence. To "you have solved nothing": correct, by design; for a reader who wants to be told which hull is Theseus's this contributes nothing, and such a reader is better served by the four-dimensionalist or hylomorphist attempts, which are genuine attempts at the verdict and are not ruled out here—but explaining *why cases with identical criteria are differently structured* is itself progress the partisan answers do not contain.

## 7. What the founding example says about where we started

The puzzle is most often offered as the clean case of a paradox that is really a confusion, dissolved by fixing the criterion. Turn the analysis back on that classification and it does not survive.

I take the dissolution view in its strongest reconstructible form, and I do not pin it on a proponent, because it does not need one: it is the natural thing a careful person thinks, and that is precisely the point. The Ship of Theseus is the easy case only on its surface. The surface is the plank drift and the threshold vagueness—genuinely conceptual hygiene, genuinely dissolvable. Beneath it lies the conflict of incompatible truth-makers that fixing a criterion can only suppress (§2). And across its versions and cousins, "which one is real" decomposes into *real under which criterion, and under which cardinality* (§4). The puzzle was never a single-mechanism affair; it was several mechanisms wearing one hull, and calling it the easy case was the move that hid the rest.

Here is why the charge bites *for this puzzle in particular* rather than against conceptual analysis in general. The contrast is exact: ordinary analysis removes an inconsistency by the *argued privilege* of one ground over another, and rests its verdict on those reasons; the dissolution treatment removes it by *stipulation*, offering no reason to prefer matter to form and presenting the disappearance of the flicker—a consequence of having fixed *either*—as though no metaphysical question remained. It is not the choosing that is illicit; it is dressing an unargued choice as a dissolution that needs no choosing. And the reason this has gone unnoticed for so long is the very reasonableness of the move: fixing the criterion *is* a correct and sensible solution to the drift layer, which makes it easy to mistake for a solution to the whole. A bad answer would have been caught; a good answer to the wrong question is what survives twenty-five centuries. The disagreement that remains after the criterion is fixed is not a residue to be cleaned away; it is the metaphysics, still standing.

The Ship of Theseus was never the puzzle that dissolves when you fix the frame. It is the puzzle that shows how fixing the frame conceals which criterion you suppressed—and, where there is more than one candidate bearer, which cardinality you assumed.

## Remaining questions

Three questions remain open, of three different kinds. *First*, and most pressing, whether the cardinality parameter of §4 is genuinely *one* parameter or two structurally analogous ones: the schema in §4.4 treats reference-cardinality (Hobbes) and object-cardinality (statue/clay) as values of a single variable, and this is a commitment the paper does not discharge. A case that crosses the two axes—distinct candidates contested under a count noun, or coincident candidates contested under a proper name—would test whether the toggle from parallel verdicts to exclusion behaves identically across the crossing, and so whether §4.4's unification is a result or merely a hopeful bridge. *Second*, "which is the real one?" is not one question but a family, each indexed to a purpose—legal title, sentimental continuity, museological authenticity, material provenance—each potentially picking out a different bearer; its felt depth is underspecification, and it resolves on the same indexing that fixes the cardinality parameter. *Third*, whether identity is made true by constituent matter or by continuity of form is a conflict of grounds these puzzles do not settle; whether it is settleable *at all*, by other means, is a question on which this paper is deliberately silent.
