# Provenance Is Not Proof

*A verification system can prove where a thing came from and never touch whether it is true. That gap is now the most valuable unowned real estate in medicine, media, and money — and the business model is to enclose it while calling it a fact.*

---

Before any team can win the $81 million grand prize in the XPRIZE Healthspan competition, someone has to decide what winning means. The prize will go to a therapy that makes a person "ten years younger" in a year — measured across muscle, cognition, and immune function. But there is no agreed instrument that reads out a number like that, no regulator-blessed ruler for biological age. So the contest is building one. The prize's own materials are unembarrassed about this: the winning treatments will be judged against biomarkers "defined by this prize," and the foundation has handed out grants to researchers to "shape the standards by which the Grand Prize will be awarded." A separate stream of work is assembling reference thresholds from existing cohort data — measuring sticks that, in the organizers' words, become the official standards for awarding the money.

Sit with what that arrangement requires. You cannot score a race against a ruler that does not exist without manufacturing the ruler as you run. And the people manufacturing it are, in part, the people being scored. That is not a scandal hiding in the contest. It is the contest, stated in the open, and the field has mostly declined to hear it because the framing invites you to watch the pill instead of the tape measure.

This essay is about the tape measure — and about a structural mistake that shows up far beyond longevity science, anywhere a system claims to "verify" something. The mistake is that verification quietly does two different jobs, and we keep paying for one while believing we bought the other.

## Two verifications

The first job is provenance: establishing where a thing came from. Did this photo come off a real sensor pointed at a real scene? Was this document signed by the key it claims? Does this cited paper exist, with the DOI it lists, in the journal named? Provenance is mechanical. It terminates. Anyone can check it, and — crucially — checking it does not require trusting the person who produced the thing, because you check the artifact, not their honesty. Provenance is getting cheaper every year, and that is a real gift.

The second job is the one we actually care about: whether the thing is *true*, or *works*, or *means what it's being used to mean*. Did the event in the video happen? Does the signed claim correspond to the world? Does the existing, correctly-cited paper actually support the sentence it's footnoting? Does the therapy make you younger, or move a number that someone chose to call younger? This job does not terminate cheaply, often cannot be mechanized at all, and — this is the part that matters — always contains a judgment call about what counts as enough. Where you decide the checking bottoms out is not read off the world. It is a choice, made by someone, from somewhere.

Almost every verification fraud, and most of the honest confusion around verification, is the same move: present a settled provenance check as though it had settled the second question. The green checkmark means *signed by this key*. We read *true*. The forged-document detector means *the file is internally consistent*. We read *authentic*. The biomarker panel means *this number moved*. We read *younger*. The gap between what was actually checked and what we heard is where the value sits, because whoever controls the second question — who gets to decide what "younger" or "authentic" or "verified enough" means — controls a great deal, and they can do it while pointing at the first question and saying *look, we verified it*.

## The forged index and the honest checkmark

Start with images, because the collapse there is recent and clean. A photograph used to carry its own provenance for free. Light from a real scene physically stamped the film or the sensor; faking that stamp convincingly was expensive. That is why photographs functioned as evidence — not because the image *was* the event, which it never was, but because it had been *caused* by the event, and the causal trail was hard to counterfeit.

A convincing synthetic video is the engineered destruction of that trail. Its whole craft is to satisfy every mark a real capture would have left — consistent shadows, plausible metadata, coherent reflections — so that a real recording and a fabricated one land at the same point and no amount of staring at the file tells them apart. The provenance question stops having a cheap answer. And the moment it does, something outside the file has to break the tie: whose account you trust, which source you believe, where you place the burden of proof. That tiebreaker is a judgment call, and it was forced into existence by the forgery.

Cryptographic provenance — signing a file at the moment of capture — is the obvious repair, and it is a good one. It re-establishes a cheap, mechanical answer to *where did this come from*: signed by this camera's key, unaltered since. This is genuinely the proof-equivalent for origin, and it works. But watch what the checkmark then gets asked to carry. "Verified" on such a system means, precisely, *signed by this key and unmodified*. It does not mean the scene was real, that the key wasn't stolen, or that the signer is who they claim. A perfectly signed, perfectly verified synthetic clip passes. The signature settles provenance and is read as settling truth — the same conflation, now compressed into an icon that most people will never think to unpack. The repair to the first problem becomes a more convincing delivery vehicle for the second.

## The measurement is the product

Now back to the tape measure, where the same structure appears with a price tag. The provenance layer of longevity science is in decent shape: you can confirm a study ran, a compound was administered, a number changed. The contested layer is what the number *means* — whether a biomarker shift is "ten years younger" or just a number moving. There is no settled, regulator-adopted answer. The FDA does not yet treat aging as a condition with an approval pathway; the clocks on offer are not validated against the thing they proxy for. The second question is wide open.

An open second question is not a problem the contest is nobly solving. It is the opening the contest is built to fill. Whoever ends up holding the instrument that certifies "younger" holds a chokepoint on the largest market there will ever be, because aging is the one condition every customer eventually has. The contest is a forcing event that takes a plural, contested field — a dozen competing clocks, none official — and produces a single ruler with sixty-plus teams' buy-in, university data-center machinery, and the legitimacy of a famous prize, all assembled before any regulator convenes a meeting. The agency, when it arrives, inherits a finished standard with the industry already built around it.

The reassuring rebuttal is that no one can really own a measurement — that aging metrics will stay plural, more like a contested market than a single stamp. The credit score is the answer to that. FICO does not own the concept of creditworthiness and never needed to. It became the default; its formula is proprietary and unpublished; no one voted for it; and it now silently gates housing, employment, and the price of money. A privately held, uninspectable number the whole system defers to is not the gentle version of this outcome. It is the finished version.

Two disciplines are worth holding here, because the temptation is to make this a story about a person. First, the simpler explanation: a confidential drug candidate (the contest's most visible entrant is code-named SL-100, its contents described to *MIT Technology Review* as "highly, highly confidential") looks at first like ordinary trade-secret protection. But ordinary secrecy guards a fragile, hard-won result a rival could copy. The field largely agrees the underlying chemistry is a known, shared problem; experts reached by the same reporting cautioned that the chemical approach is, at lab concentrations, harsh and not especially effective. When the recipe is roughly common knowledge, secrecy is not protecting a discovery — it is converting a shared problem into a private position long enough to set the standard the position will be sold against. That is a different function wearing secrecy's clothes.

Second, and this is the load-bearing restraint: nothing here requires reading anyone's intent, and the analysis is stronger for refusing to. Whether the scientists involved are sincere believers or cynical operators, the structure does the same work — a standard authored by the people it will judge, funded by a sovereign wealth fund (the Hevolution Foundation, the contest's single-largest backer, is financed primarily by the Saudi government), legitimized before any public referee weighs in. Intent is not observable from the outside, and the case does not need it. What it needs is the one prospective test that would settle the matter, named below.

## The thing they're selling is already free

A quieter version of the same move is spreading through AI. As automated systems issue more claims — clinical, legal, financial — a market has appeared for layers that "verify" what the machine said. The honest core of these systems is provenance: confirm the cited source exists, the paper wasn't retracted, the data is what it claims. That part is real and useful. But the part that gets sold as the breakthrough is verifying the *judgment* — whether the claim is correct, whether it meets the standard of care — and that is the second question, the one that does not mechanize. Proposals to settle it by consensus among automated reviewers do not escape the problem; they launder a shared bias into a number and call the agreement truth. Rewarding agreement selects for convergence, not correctness, which is the failure mode dressed as the feature.

There is a sharper point, the kind that survives contact with a skeptic. The genuinely useful artifact these systems promise — an audit trail of what the machine relied on, what was contested, what to distrust — frequently already exists, for free. When an automated system tells a professional something, the telling is a logged, timestamped record. The commons is sitting there. Much of what the priced verification layer offers is either a re-solution of a problem the log already solves, or an attempt to enclose that free commons behind a toll and a checkmark. The tell is the same as the contest's: the valuable thing is being made to look like a service when its function is to privatize something that was open.

## What honest verification looks like

It would be easy to read all this as a counsel of despair — verification is theater, trust no checkmark. That is too strong, and the corrective is already running in hospitals. Software that helps triage stroke patients — flagging a large-vessel occlusion, measuring salvageable brain tissue against a threshold — is a genuine, narrow, deterministic verification, and it is honest for three specific reasons. It applies a standard that was *declared elsewhere*, in published clinical guidelines that a committee sat down, argued out, and put in the open. It operates inside a bounded domain where, relative to that declared standard, the facts really do decide. And it leaves the second question — operate or not — with a human clinician who carries the liability and will answer, later, to a court and an expert witness if the call was wrong.

That is the whole shape of an honest system: the provenance layer is declared and checkable, the domain is bounded enough that "the facts decide" is approximately true, and the judgment layer is *located* in someone accountable rather than dissolved into a checkmark. None of the cases above clears that bar. The forged-video detector hides the second question behind the first. The contest owns the second question while pointing at the first. The AI verification layer sells the first as though it were the second. Stroke triage is what it looks like to do neither.

## The one test, and why it is only a wager

So here is the question the longevity contest's organizers could answer tomorrow and have not: publish the terms under which the winning measurement becomes available — open, unlicensed, owned by no competitor and no funder. If the ruler is a public good, say so in writing and the suspicion in this essay evaporates on contact. The refusal to do this, in a contest whose own people say its purpose is to settle how aging is measured, is the most informative fact available. It is the same test that applies to every system in this essay: not *do you trust the checkmark* but *will you show, and give up sole ownership of, the standard behind it*.

The honest ending withholds the consolation that watching for that answer is enough. The test is prospective — it asks what an institution does when its claim finally meets the world — and a patient enough party can pass it in appearance while failing it in fact: open the standard with fanfare, retain control of the thing that matters, perform the disclosure the way a synthetic video performs a provenance trail. The second question can be counterfeited exactly where it most looks settled. What you are left with is not certainty about anyone's intent, which is unavailable, but a declared wager with a visible price — and the knowledge that the price, too, can be paid in forgeries. The checkmark will go on meaning *signed*. We will go on reading *true*. The only defense is to keep asking who owns the ruler, and to treat a confident answer as the place to look harder, not the place to stop.

# Draft apparatus — for review, not publication

## Evidence Framework

### Documented in public records (Tier 1)
- XPRIZE Healthspan is a 7-year, $101M competition; grand prize ~$81M, culminating 2030; criteria are ≥10-year relative improvement in muscle/cognitive/immune function after one year. — XPRIZE Foundation materials; PR Newswire launch release (Nov 29, 2023); xprize.org/competitions/healthspan.
- The winning treatments are judged against biomarkers "defined by this prize"; grant awards explicitly let researchers "shape the standards by which the Grand Prize will be awarded," producing reference thresholds that "become official standards." — xprize.org/competitions/healthspan; longevity.technology, "The race to measure aging" (Sep 12, 2025).
- Hevolution Foundation is the single-largest funder and is financed primarily by the Saudi government. — Science/AAAS (Nov 29, 2023); Hevolution statements in XPRIZE releases.
- David Sinclair (Harvard Medical School; co-founder, Life Biosciences) confirmed to *MIT Technology Review* he plans to give an oral drug mixture to volunteers seeking "evidence for age restoration in humans"; candidate code-named SL-100, contents "highly, highly confidential." Outside experts cautioned the chemical-reprogramming approach is, at lab concentrations, harsh and not particularly effective. — *MIT Technology Review*, Antonio Regalado (Jun 9, 2026).
- FDA does not yet have an approval pathway treating aging as a condition; available aging clocks are not validated against the outcomes they proxy. — Fight Aging! (May 21, 2025); news-medical.net (Jun 5, 2025); biorxiv preprints on clock reliability.
- Synthetic media can satisfy the forensic marks of genuine capture; digital signatures attest origin/integrity, not the truth of depicted content. — general technical record.
- Credit scoring (FICO): proprietary, unpublished formula functioning as a default gate on credit/housing/employment. — general public record.

### Reasonable inferences from documented facts (Tier 2)
- A prize that must define its own scoring metric, judged in part by its contestants, has incentives toward a metric that flatters interventions already in the field — without requiring coordination. (Follows from the documented self-authoring structure; marked as inference.)
- Confidentiality around a candidate whose chemistry the field treats as roughly shared functions less as discovery-protection than as position-holding while a standard is set. (Inference from the documented "shared problem" expert framing + the secrecy; not a claim about intent.)
- A verification market over automated systems largely re-solves or encloses an audit trail the interaction log already provides. (Inference from the structure of logging; not specific to any named vendor.)

### Structural hypotheses requiring additional evidence (Tier 3)
- That the contest is, in effect, importing the ratings-agency model into biology (design the test, fund the grader, hand the regulator a finished standard). Would move toward Tier 2 if the winning metric is adopted as a de facto regulatory or payer standard without independent public governance. Would be falsified if the metric is published open and unlicensed.
- That orientation (enclosure vs. sincere public-good intent) is undeterminable from present evidence and resolvable only prospectively. Falsified if a near-term disclosure of open, unowned terms occurs (resolving toward public-good) — or if the standard is licensed/controlled at consolidation (resolving toward enclosure).

## Alternative explanations considered
- **Ordinary trade secrecy / normal competition.** Insufficient because ordinary secrecy protects a fragile, copyable result; the field's own experts describe the underlying approach as shared and known, which removes the thing secrecy normally guards. The distinguishing evidence is the contest's documented self-authoring of the scoring standard — a feature absent from ordinary drug competition.
- **Metrics will stay plural; no one can own a measurement.** Addressed directly via FICO: default-setting, not formal ownership, is the mechanism; plurality describes the round before consolidation, not a stable end state.
- **The checkmark conflation is just user error, not a fraud.** Granted for many cases — which is why the essay's load-bearing claim is structural (the systems are built so the first answer reads as the second) rather than an accusation of deceit against any actor.

## Institutional actions
1. **XPRIZE / Hevolution** — publish, in writing and before finalists' trials conclude, the licensing and governance terms of the winning measurement standard: who owns it, who can use it, at what price, under what independent oversight. Requires no new authority.
2. **FDA / NIA** — state publicly whether any prize-defined biomarker is on a path to regulatory or payer adoption, and if so, attach independent, public governance to the standard before it consolidates.
3. **Buyers of AI "verification" layers** — require vendors to distinguish, in plain language, provenance checks (source exists, unaltered) from judgment claims (correct, meets standard), and to disclose what the existing interaction log already provides for free.

## Unresolved questions
- Will the winning aging-measurement standard be published open and unowned, or licensed/controlled? (The single most informative prospective fact.)
- Is there any verification scheme that certifies world-correspondence — not custody of bits — while keeping its trust root cheap and uncontested? (Predicted: no; correspondence is the second question and cannot be made seat-free.)
- Can the prospective "open the standard" test be reliably distinguished from a performed version of it, and if so, by what later observation?
