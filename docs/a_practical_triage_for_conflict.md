## A Practical Triage for Conflict

### The foundational premise

Not all disagreements are the same kind of problem. The first move in any conflict is to classify it — not to resolve it. Attempting resolution on the wrong category is the most common way skilled people waste effort and erode trust.

There are four categories. The engine you're using produces them formally, but they can be approximated with a small set of diagnostic questions.

---

### The four categories

**Mountain.** Disagreement that survives every parameter change. Vary the scope, adjust exit quality, shift the time horizon — the same parties still classify the situation oppositely, and neither can unilaterally reclassify without the system forcing them back. The engine calls this Nash-stable: 69% of non-constant orbits have this structure. Your example of *introspective_access_limits* is a mountain — the powerless observer cannot introspect their way to reliable real-time discrimination between depletion and discomfort. That limit is not a social construction; it is a cognitive fact that holds across all observer positions. What changes across positions is not whether the limit exists but what to do about it. Institutions see it as optional (scaffolding can be removed). Individuals experience it as survival-critical. The disagreement over *that* is the mountain.

The correct response to a mountain is management, not resolution. Name it explicitly as structurally irreducible. Identify what each party needs given the constraint — not as a step toward agreement but as a permanent operating condition. Find what actions are feasible in the structure as it is.

**Tangled rope.** Disagreement where at least one party is experiencing an extraction trap dressed as coordination. The engine flags this as "coordination-washed" — the situation looks functional from the institutional observer position and extractive from the powerless observer position, and the gap (called delta_chi) is measurable. Both *misattribution_feedback_loop* and *practice_ethic_boundary* are tangled ropes, with delta_chi of 0.54 and 0.65 respectively — high. The key structural feature is that changing parameters *can* move the classification. The engine shows that scope modulation can resolve some presheaves; exit quality, less so. This is where the levers are.

The correct response to a tangled rope is to identify which parameters, if changed, would move the parties toward a shared classification. Improving exit quality (making leaving easier) almost never resolves the disagreement at the level the papers measure it — but broadening scope (making the frame larger) sometimes does. Coalition-building is possible here, including across parties who disagree about the underlying situation but agree about a specific parameter change.

**Fragile presheaf.** Apparent agreement that is structurally precarious. All observers currently classify the situation the same way, but the consensus is near a threshold — a parameter shift would expose latent disagreement. The engine identifies these through the Arakelov height diagnostic: 238 constraints in the corpus show no observer disagreement but genuine pre-correction probability mass that was split before the structural signature forced consensus. These are invisible to any diagnostic that only asks "do they agree right now?"

The correct response to a fragile presheaf is surveillance and preparation. Identify what change would break the consensus. Do not mistake current agreement for stable agreement. This category matters especially in institutional settings where apparent consensus has been built up over time and a triggering event — a policy change, an economic shift, new information — can suddenly reveal that parties were never actually aligned.

**Genuine sheaf.** Disagreement (or even agreement) where the parties' perspectives assemble into a globally consistent picture. The local data from each observer position pastes together without contradiction. This is where conflict resolution techniques — mediation, negotiation, facilitated dialogue — are correctly targeted. The problem here is real but tractable. The parties have incomplete information, different interests, or mismatched framings, but there is in principle a description of the situation that all of them could recognize as accurate.

---

### The diagnostic sequence

Before doing anything else, run these questions in order.

**First: Do all observers currently agree?** Not do they agree on what to do — do they agree on what the situation *is*? If the powerless party sees an extraction trap and the institutional party sees a coordination mechanism, you have a presheaf. If they agree on the basic description, proceed.

**Second (if they agree): Is that agreement stable?** Ask what would change it. A change in exit conditions? A change in scope? A shift in who has power? If agreement is robust to reasonable parameter variation, you have a genuine sheaf. If it's near a threshold, you have a fragile presheaf — treat it accordingly.

**Third (if they disagree): Does the disagreement survive parameter change?** This is the mountain test. Vary scope, time horizon, exit options. If the disagreement persists across all variations, you have a mountain. If changing some parameter dissolves it or moves the parties toward shared classification, you have a tangled rope.

**Fourth (if tangled rope): Which parameters move things, and in which direction?** The engine's product-site structure tells you: power is the dominant axis, scope is a secondary modulator with an asymmetric effect (broadening scope resolves some presheaves; narrowing it creates them), and exit quality rarely crosses the classification boundary on its own. In practice: look at what changes the *frame*, not what changes the *incentives*. Reframing — expanding who is visible as affected, what time horizon is relevant, what counts as inside or outside the situation — is more likely to move a tangled rope than adding or removing costs and benefits.

---

### Coalition logic

The four categories have different coalition structures.

Mountains generate stable opposing blocs. The 3+1 split the engine consistently finds — three observer positions classifying the situation one way, one (usually institutional) classifying it another — is a pattern, not a coincidence. It tells you something about where power is located relative to the disagreement. Coalitions across the majority bloc are possible and useful even when the mountain cannot be moved. You can coordinate on managing the constraint without resolving the underlying disagreement.

Tangled ropes allow opportunistic coalitions around specific parameter changes. Parties who disagree about everything else may agree that a scope expansion is useful, or that a particular exit option should exist. These coalitions are temporary and transactional — they don't require agreement on the underlying classification. Don't ask for more than the coalition requires.

Fragile presheaves require you to be honest about what you're holding together and why. The parties think they agree. If you know the consensus is structurally precarious, the ethical question is whether to reveal this. Revealing it risks breaking the coalition. Not revealing it risks a worse break later. The answer depends on your time horizon and what the triggering event would be.

Genuine sheafs support durable coalitions because the parties can actually agree on a shared description of the situation. This is the prerequisite for any resolution that sticks.

---

### What this doesn't do

This framework does not tell you what the right outcome is. It tells you what *kind* of problem you're facing and therefore what *kind* of intervention is appropriate. It also doesn't tell you where the lines are with precision — the engine's classifications depend on how you measure power, extractiveness, and scope, and those measurements involve judgment. What the framework offers is a way of thinking, in order, that avoids the most common failure mode: applying resolution techniques to structural disagreements and blaming the parties when it doesn't work.

The introvert essay you provided is a clean example of this in miniature. The mountain (you cannot introspect your way to reliable real-time discrimination between depletion and discomfort) is correctly identified as a permanent constraint. The tangled rope (the feedback loop that reinforces misattribution) is correctly identified as something you can intervene on — not by resolving the underlying cognitive limit, but by introducing a practice (door-holding) that changes the scope of the diagnostic window. The practice/ethics boundary is correctly flagged as contested — a Type C indexical disagreement about what counts as an ethic versus a heuristic, with no resolution available until you specify "ethic for whom, and by what criteria."

That structure — mountain, then tangled rope, then contested boundary — is the framework applied. The essay doesn't call it that, but that's what it's doing.
