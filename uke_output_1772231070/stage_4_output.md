# The Championship Run

I run the old route every Sunday now, alone on the Nankai pitch after the bank men have gone home. The grass is patchy, worn down to dirt in places where feet have passed ten thousand times. I start at midfield, where I started that day in 1926, and I count my breaths. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. My knees ache before I reach the penalty box.

In the memory, I'm not breathing at all. I'm just running. The ball at my feet, the goal ahead, my body knowing exactly what to do. No thought, no effort. The defender comes at me and I'm already past him. The goalkeeper shifts left and I'm putting it right. The crowd roars and I'm raising my arms and the newspaper photographers are running onto the pitch. Perfect motion. Perfect certainty.

Now I'm twenty-four and my knees sound like grinding stone.

---

Suyin is reading when I come home. She's always reading. Today it's a book in English, something by a woman with a difficult name. The apartment is in her father's building in the British concession, third floor, with windows that overlook the river. The furniture is the kind you see in the foreign magazines—low chairs, clean lines, nothing like the heavy carved pieces my parents have in their house in the Chinese district.

"How was football?" she asks, not looking up.

"Fine."

"Did you win?"

"We don't keep score."

She makes a small sound that might be acknowledgment. Her finger traces the line of text. She reads the way she does everything—effortlessly, hungrily, like the words are water and she's been thirsty her whole life.

I go to the kitchen and pour tea. The amah has left dinner warming on the stove. I can hear the river through the open window, the sound of boat horns and dock workers shouting. Three blocks away is the Yong'an Textile Mill, but you can't hear the machinery from here. The British concession is quiet. Ordered. Clean.

"We're invited to the Chens' tonight," Suyin says from the other room. "Dr. Chen's wife is hosting a salon. There's a visiting professor from Beijing University. He's giving a talk on the new poetry movement."

I pour the tea carefully. "What time?"

"Eight o'clock."

"I have reports to finish for the bank."

"Ming," she says, and now she does look up. "You always have reports."

"Mr. Xu wants them by Monday." Her father. My employer. The man whose connections got me the clerk position when my football career ended and I had nothing else.

She marks her place in the book with a ribbon. "The professor is supposed to be brilliant. Everyone will be there."

Everyone. Her friends from McTyeire. The diplomats' wives. The journalists who write for the English-language newspapers. The young professors who've studied abroad and come back full of ideas about modernization and consciousness and the new literature. People who speak in English even when they don't have to, because English is the language of ideas, of sophistication, of the future.

"I'll try to finish early," I say.

She smiles. "Good. Wear the grey suit. It's less formal."

---

The bank is quiet on Sunday afternoon. I'm the only clerk in the office, just me and the sound of my pen scratching across the ledger. Numbers in columns. Debits and credits. Everything balanced, everything in order. I'm good at this work. It's not complicated. You follow the rules and the numbers come out right.

Through the window I can see the street. A rickshaw passes, the puller's back bent under the weight. A woman in a qipao walks by with a parasol. Two men in Western suits stop to talk, their briefcases tucked under their arms. The street is the border between the British concession and the Chinese district. On this side, the buildings are brick and stone, solid, permanent. On the other side, the architecture is older, more crowded, the streets narrower.

I think about Wei Guozhang. I saw him last month at the warehouse where he works security. He was standing by the loading dock, leaning on his cane, watching the workers unload crates from a truck. His knees are ruined from two years playing professionally in Shanghai. The league paid almost nothing and when his knees gave out there was no pension, no medical care, nothing.

"They used up my body," he said when I asked how he was doing. Not bitter, just stating a fact. "I gave them everything and when there was nothing left they were done with me."

He's twenty-five. He walks like an old man.

I was smarter than Wei. I stopped before my body broke completely. I married well. I got the bank job. But some mornings I wake up and my knees are stiff and I think about Wei's cane and I wonder if I was smart or just lucky, and if the luck is running out.

In the memory of the championship run, my body is infinite. I can feel it even now—that certainty, that knowledge that my legs will do exactly what I ask them to do, that the ball will go exactly where I want it to go. For those minutes I was perfect. The crowd saw it. The reporters saw it. Suyin saw it. She told me later that was when she first noticed me, watching me move with absolute certainty across the grass.

I finish the reports at seven-thirty. Time enough to go home and change into the grey suit and accompany my wife to a salon where I will understand perhaps one word in five.

---

The Chens' apartment is larger than ours, with a proper drawing room and a dining room and servants who pass through silently with trays of tea and small cakes. The furniture is European, the paintings on the walls are European, even the tea service is European—delicate porcelain cups with gold rims, the kind that look like they'll shatter if you breathe on them wrong.

Suyin is radiant. She's wearing the blue qipao, the one that makes her look like she stepped out of a Shanghai advertisement. Her hair is done in the new style, shorter, more modern. She moves through the room like water, stopping to greet friends, laughing at something someone says, her hand light on my arm as she introduces me.

"This is my husband, Chen Mingzhe. He played for Nankai. The 1926 championship team."

People nod politely. The championship was six years ago. Six years is long enough that most of these people don't remember, don't care. I am Suyin's husband. That's what matters. That's who I am in this room.

The professor arrives at eight-fifteen. He's younger than I expected, maybe thirty, with wire-rimmed glasses and a Western suit that looks expensive. Dr. Chen introduces him and everyone finds seats and the professor begins to talk.

He talks about poetry. About consciousness. About the new techniques that writers are using to capture the interior experience of modern life. He mentions names—Woolf, Joyce, Eliot—and everyone nods like these are old friends. He reads a passage from something, in English, and people lean forward to listen. The English is fast, complicated, full of words I don't know. I catch fragments: "stream of consciousness," "temporal dislocation," "the dissolution of the unified self."

Suyin is sitting forward in her chair, her eyes bright. She asks a question in English and the professor smiles and responds and everyone laughs at something he says and I don't know what it was, I don't know what was funny. I sit very still and try to look interested and I realize I'm holding my breath.

At the Nankai pitch, when I run the old route, I count my breaths. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. Conscious breathing, measured breathing, the breathing of someone whose body is no longer automatic, no longer certain.

Here, in this room, I'm not breathing at all. I'm suspended. Waiting for the moment to pass. Waiting for the conversation to shift into something I can follow, some topic where I won't be completely lost. But the moment doesn't pass. The professor talks for an hour and then people ask questions and then there's discussion and it's all in English, all moving too fast, all assuming a shared knowledge that I don't have.

Suyin glances at me once. I smile at her. She smiles back and turns her attention back to the professor.

I study the painting on the wall behind the professor's head. It's European, a landscape, hills and trees and a river. The colors are muted, browns and greens and greys. It's a competent painting. It's also completely foreign, depicting a place I've never been, a landscape that has nothing to do with China, with Tianjin, with anything I know.

This entire room is like that painting. Competent. Expensive. Foreign.

At ten o'clock, people begin to leave. Suyin is talking with two women by the door, all three of them speaking in English, their voices animated. I stand nearby, close enough that it's clear I'm with Suyin, far enough that I'm not intruding on their conversation. One of the women says something and all three of them laugh. The laughter is light, musical, the sound of people who understand each other perfectly.

On the street, walking home, Suyin is still glowing. "Wasn't he brilliant? The way he connected the temporal experiments in modernist fiction to the political fragmentation of the contemporary moment—it was exactly what I've been thinking but couldn't articulate."

"Yes," I say.

"And Dr. Chen's wife said she might be able to introduce me to the editor at the North China Daily News. They're looking for someone to write cultural pieces. It wouldn't pay much, but the exposure—"

"That's good."

She looks at me. "You're quiet."

"I'm tired. The reports took longer than I expected."

She links her arm through mine. "Thank you for coming. I know these gatherings aren't really your thing, but it means something to me that you're there."

"Of course."

We walk in silence for a while. The street is quiet, just the sound of our footsteps and the distant horn of a boat on the river. The air smells like coal smoke and the river, that particular Tianjin smell that's both industrial and ancient.

"Do you ever miss it?" Suyin asks. "Football?"

"Sometimes."

"You were so good. I still remember watching you play. You were like—" She pauses, searching for the words. "Like you were doing exactly what you were meant to do. Like there was no gap between intention and action."

In the memory, there is no gap. I think about the goal and my body achieves it. The ball goes where I want it to go. The defenders are where I expect them to be. Everything is clear, everything is certain.

"That was a long time ago," I say.

"Not so long. Six years."

Six years. Half of my adult life. In six more years I'll be thirty. My knees will be worse. The memory will be further away. The gap between who I was and who I am will be wider.

We reach our building. The elevator is out of order so we climb the stairs to the third floor. Suyin is still talking about the salon, about the professor's ideas, about the article she might write for the North China Daily News. I listen to her voice, to the enthusiasm in it, and I think about how alive she is in those rooms, how animated, how completely herself.

With me, she speaks Chinese. With her friends, she speaks English. The English is faster, brighter, more precise. It's the language of ideas, of sophistication, of the future. Chinese is the language of domesticity, of the past, of the life we share when we're alone.

I wonder sometimes which language she thinks in.

---

On Sunday I go to the bank men's game. We play in a dusty lot near the warehouses, no spectators, no reporters, just ten men in their twenties and thirties chasing a ball across hard-packed dirt. I'm one of the older players now. The younger men are faster, hungrier. They work in the lower positions at the bank, clerks and assistants, and they play with the desperation of people who think this might be their path to something better.

I remember that feeling. The belief that your body could carry you somewhere, that physical excellence was a currency that would always have value. I remember the moment after the championship when the reporters crowded around and the photographers took pictures and everyone wanted to know my name, where I was from, what I planned to do next. I remember thinking that this was the beginning of something, that the glory would continue, that I had found the thing I was meant to do.

That was six years ago.

I score once in the Sunday game, a clean shot from outside the box that the goalkeeper doesn't reach. The other men congratulate me and we play on. No crowd roars. No photographers run onto the pitch. It's just a goal in a Sunday game, forgotten by the time we finish.

After, I stay on the pitch alone. The other men head to a teahouse to drink and talk, but I tell them I have work to finish. I walk to midfield, to the spot where I started the championship run, and I begin.

The ball isn't here anymore—we only have one ball for the Sunday games and someone took it—so I run without it. I start at midfield and I run toward the goal, cutting left where I cut left that day, shifting right where I shifted right, past the spot where the defender was, past the spot where the goalkeeper dove. I finish the run at the goal line and I stand there, breathing hard, my knees throbbing.

In the memory, I'm not breathing. I'm just running.

I do it again. Midfield to goal. The same cuts, the same angles. My breath comes in gasps now. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. Counted breathing, conscious breathing, the breathing of someone whose body is no longer automatic.

I do it a third time. My knees are screaming. My lungs burn. But I keep running, trying to recapture that feeling, that certainty, that moment when my body knew exactly what to do.

It doesn't come. It's gone. It's been gone for six years and I'm still here on this empty pitch, running the same route, trying to find something that doesn't exist anymore.

I stop at midfield, bent over, hands on my knees, breathing hard. The sun is setting. The warehouses cast long shadows across the lot. In the distance I can hear the sound of the city—boat horns, traffic, the factory whistles signaling shift change.

I wish I could go back six years. I wish I could stay in that moment forever, the ball at my feet, the goal ahead, the crowd roaring, my body perfect and certain and infinite.

---

Wei Guozhang is standing by the warehouse loading dock when I arrive. He's leaning on his cane, watching the workers unload crates. I almost don't stop—I'm supposed to meet Suyin at the International Club for tea—but Wei sees me and raises his hand and I can't pretend I didn't notice.

"Chen," he says. "Still playing?"

"Sundays. With men from the bank."

He nods. "I watch sometimes. The young ones who work here. They play in the lot during lunch break. They're terrible but they don't know it yet. They think if they're good enough, someone will notice. Someone will give them a chance."

"Maybe someone will."

Wei laughs, a short, bitter sound. "Sure. Maybe. And maybe my knees will heal and I'll play again."

We stand in silence for a moment, watching the workers. The crates are heavy, full of machinery parts. The workers move in coordinated rhythm, passing the crates from truck to dock to warehouse. Their movements are efficient, practiced, the movements of bodies that know their work.

"You were smart," Wei says. "Stopping when you did. Marrying well. Getting out before the system broke you."

"I didn't get out. I just stopped playing."

"Same thing. You saw what it was. What it does." He taps his cane against the dock. "I didn't see it. I thought if I was good enough, if I worked hard enough, they'd take care of me. I gave them everything. Two years in Shanghai, playing hurt, playing through pain, because I thought that's what you did. You sacrificed for the game." He looks at me. "They used up my body and when there was nothing left they were done with me. No pension. No medical care. Nothing."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just don't make the same mistake with something else." He shifts his weight, wincing. "You got lucky with the wife, with the bank job. That's good. That's smart. But don't think any system will take care of you. They'll use you up if you let them."

A worker drops a crate. It hits the dock with a crash and something inside breaks with a sound like shattering glass. The foreman yells and the worker apologizes and they clean up the mess and keep moving. The rhythm resumes. Efficient. Practiced. No one stops working.

"I should go," I say. "Suyin is waiting."

"Give her my regards." Wei turns back to watch the workers. "Tell her congratulations on the newspaper piece. I saw her article in the North China Daily News. She's good. Smart. Going places."

I don't remember telling Wei about Suyin's writing. Maybe I did. Maybe he heard it somewhere else. In Tianjin, news travels through the foreign concessions like water through connected vessels. Everyone knows everyone's business.

At the International Club, Suyin is sitting at a table near the window with two women I don't know. They're speaking in English, their voices bright and animated. I stand in the doorway for a moment, watching. Suyin is gesturing as she talks, her hands moving through the air, shaping something invisible. One of the women says something and all three of them laugh.

I'm holding my breath.

I cross the room and Suyin sees me and her face lights up. "Ming! These are my friends from the salon—Mrs. Morrison and Miss Zhang. We were just talking about the new Woolf novel. Have you read it?"

"No."

"You should. It's extraordinary. The way she handles time, the way consciousness moves backward and forward—" She turns to her friends. "My husband isn't much of a reader. He's more physical. He was a champion footballer."

"How interesting," Mrs. Morrison says in English. Her accent is British, educated, the kind of English that makes every word sound like it has been carefully selected from a list of acceptable options.

Miss Zhang says something in English that I don't catch. Suyin responds and they laugh again. I sit down and a waiter brings tea and I pour carefully, focusing on the precise angle of the pot, the exact level of liquid in each cup.

The conversation continues around me. I catch words—"consciousness," "temporal," "modernist," "Bloomsbury"—but they float past like debris in a river, disconnected from any context I understand. Suyin is fully engaged, leaning forward, her eyes bright. This is her element. This is where she belongs.

I am a piece of furniture. Present but not participating. Decorative but not functional.

At six o'clock, Mrs. Morrison and Miss Zhang leave. Suyin and I walk home together. She's still talking about the conversation, about Mrs. Morrison's ideas about stream of consciousness, about Miss Zhang's theory connecting the new literature to political fragmentation.

"You were quiet," she says.

"I didn't have much to contribute."

"You could try. You're smart, Ming. You just don't make the effort."

I don't respond. What would I say? That I don't make the effort because the effort is impossible? That the gap between understanding words and understanding context is unbridgeable? That every gathering leaves me more aware of how little I know, how far behind I am, how completely I don't belong in her world?

We walk in silence. The street is crowded with people heading home from work. Rickshaws pass, their pullers calling out for passengers. A street vendor is selling steamed buns, the smell mixing with coal smoke and river water.

"Dr. Chen's wife offered me a position," Suyin says. "Part-time, writing cultural pieces for the North China Daily News. It's not much money, but the exposure—"

"That's good."

"I said yes. I start next week."

"Congratulations."

She stops walking and turns to face me. "Are you angry?"

"No. Why would I be angry?"

"You seem—" She pauses, searching for the word. "Distant. Like you're somewhere else."

I look at her. She's beautiful in the evening light, her face serious, concerned. She loves me. I know this. But I also know that the person she loves is becoming smaller, more diminished, more irrelevant to the life she's building.

"I'm just tired," I say. "The bank has been busy."

She studies my face for a moment, then nods. "Let's go home. I'll make dinner."

---

At the bank, Mr. Xu calls me into his office. He's Suyin's father, a compact man in his fifties who made his fortune facilitating trade between Chinese manufacturers and foreign buyers. He speaks English, French, and three Chinese dialects. His office is furnished in Western style, with a large desk and leather chairs and a portrait of Sun Yat-sen on the wall.

"Sit," he says.

I sit.

He studies me for a moment, his fingers steepled under his chin. "How is my daughter?"

"She's well. Busy with her writing."

"Yes. I saw her article in the newspaper. Very good. Very intelligent." He pauses. "And how are you, Mingzhe?"

"I'm well, sir."

"The reports you've been producing are adequate. Accurate. No complaints." He leans back in his chair. "But adequate is not impressive. Accurate is not ambitious."

I wait.

"You've been with the bank for three years. In that time, you've remained a clerk. You've made no effort to advance, to take on more responsibility, to distinguish yourself." He looks at me steadily. "Why is that?"

"I do the work assigned to me, sir."

"Yes. The work assigned to you. But nothing more." He sighs. "Mingzhe, I gave you this position as a favor to my daughter. She was very much in love with you, and you had no prospects after football. I understood that. Young men make choices and sometimes those choices don't lead where they expected. I was willing to help."

The office is very quiet. I can hear the clock on the wall ticking.

"But three years is long enough to prove yourself. Three years is long enough to show that you're more than just my son-in-law, that you have value beyond the connection to my family." He leans forward. "I'm not seeing that, Mingzhe. I'm seeing a man who does adequate work and goes home. A man who has no ambition, no drive, no vision for his future."

"I'm grateful for the opportunity, sir."

"Gratitude is not enough. Gratitude doesn't build a career. Gratitude doesn't provide for my daughter in the manner she deserves." He pauses. "Suyin is going places. She's talented, ambitious, connected. She will have opportunities. She will build a life. The question is whether you'll be able to keep up with her, or whether you'll become a burden she has to carry."

The words hang in the air between us. Burden. The thing I have been trying not to think about, not to acknowledge, not to name.

"I'll do better, sir."

"Will you?" He doesn't sound convinced. "I hope so, Mingzhe. For your sake and for my daughter's. But hope is not a plan. Hope is not action." He picks up a pen, a signal that the conversation is over. "That's all. You can go."

I stand and leave the office. My hands are shaking.

---

That night, Suyin and I have dinner at home. The amah has prepared fish and vegetables and rice. We eat in silence for a while, the sound of chopsticks against bowls, the distant noise of the street through the open window.

"My father talked to you today," Suyin says. It's not a question.

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"That I need to be more ambitious. That I need to do more than adequate work."

She sets down her chopsticks. "He's not wrong, Ming."

I don't respond.

"You're capable of more. You're smart, you're hardworking when you want to be. But you've been—" She pauses, choosing her words carefully. "You've been stuck. For three years, you've been doing the same work, living the same life, going through the same motions. You haven't grown."

"And you have."

"Yes. I have." She says it without apology. "I've been writing, making connections, building something. And I want you to be part of that, Ming. I want us to grow together. But I can't do it alone."

"I come to the salons. I accompany you to the gatherings."

"You come. You sit. You say nothing." She looks at me, her eyes serious. "Do you know what Mrs. Morrison asked me today? She asked if you were happy. She said you always look like you'd rather be somewhere else."

"I don't fit in those rooms."

"You could if you tried. You could read the books, learn the references, engage with the ideas. You're not stupid, Ming. You're just—" She stops.

"Just what?"

"Just stuck in the past. Just holding onto something that's gone." She reaches across the table and takes my hand. "The championship was six years ago. You were brilliant. Everyone saw it. But it's over now, and you need to let it go. You need to build something new."

I look at our hands, hers small and smooth, mine larger, the knuckles scarred from years of football. "What if I can't?"

"You can. You just have to decide to."

But that's not true. Deciding isn't enough. Effort isn't enough. The gap between her world and mine isn't something that can be bridged by reading a few books or learning a few references. It's structural. It's built into who we are, where we come from, what our bodies can do.

Her body is a tool for navigating social spaces, for speaking multiple languages, for moving between worlds. My body was a tool for running and kicking and scoring goals, and now it's failing at even that.

"I'll try," I say.

She squeezes my hand. "That's all I ask."

---

On Sunday, I run the championship route again. Midfield to goal. The same cuts, the same angles. My knees are worse this week. I can feel something grinding in the left one, bone against bone, or cartilage worn down to nothing. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. Counted breathing. Conscious breathing.

In the memory, I'm not breathing. I'm just running.

I do the route three times and then I sit in the grass at midfield, my knees throbbing, my breath ragged. The sun is setting. The shadows are lengthening. The city sounds distant, muffled, like it's happening to someone else.

I think about Wei's cane. I think about my father-in-law's words. Burden. I think about Suyin's face when she talks with her friends, bright and animated and alive. I think about Mrs. Morrison asking if I'm happy.

Am I happy?

I was happy once. In the championship run, I was happy. My body knew what to do and I did it and for those minutes I was perfect. Everything was clear. Everything was certain.

Now nothing is clear. Nothing is certain. My body is failing. My marriage is failing. I'm adequate at a job I got through connections, irrelevant in the world my wife inhabits, obsolete in the sport that once made me extraordinary.

I lie back in the grass and look at the sky. It's that particular blue you get just before sunset, deep and rich and temporary. In ten minutes it will be gone, replaced by darkness.

I close my eyes and try to remember the feeling. The ball at my feet. The goal ahead. The crowd roaring. The certainty. The perfection.

It's getting harder to remember. The memory is fading, worn down by repetition, by time, by the accumulation of everything that's happened since. In another six years, will I even be able to remember what it felt like? Will the memory be completely gone, replaced by this—lying in the grass with bad knees, breathing hard, trying to recapture something that doesn't exist anymore?

I open my eyes. The sky is darker now. The blue is fading.

I stand up and walk home. My knees throb with each step. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. Counted breathing. Conscious breathing. The breathing of someone whose body is no longer automatic, no longer certain, no longer infinite.

---

Suyin is waiting when I get home. She's sitting at the table, her hands folded, her face serious. There's an envelope on the table between us.

"We need to talk," she says.

I sit down. My knees are grateful for the chair.

"I've been thinking about what you said. About not fitting in. About the gap between us." She pauses. "And I've realized that you're right. There is a gap. And I don't think it's closing."

I don't say anything.

"I love you, Ming. I do. But I don't think love is enough anymore. I don't think it's fair to either of us to keep pretending that this is working." She pushes the envelope across the table. "I've talked to a lawyer. I want a divorce."

The words hang in the air. Divorce. The end. The final confirmation of what I've known for months but haven't wanted to acknowledge.

"Is there someone else?" I ask.

"No. This isn't about someone else. This is about us. About the fact that we've grown in different directions. About the fact that I'm building a life and you're—" She stops.

"I'm what?"

"You're stuck, Ming. You're stuck in the past, stuck in the memory of who you used to be. And I can't be stuck with you." Her voice is gentle but firm. "I need to move forward. And I don't think you can come with me."

I look at the envelope. Inside are papers, legal documents, the formal dissolution of everything we built together. Three years of marriage. Three years of trying to bridge the gap, trying to be enough, trying to keep up with a world that moves faster than I can follow.

"Where will I go?" I ask.

"My father will help you find another position. Not at his bank—that would be too awkward—but somewhere else. And the apartment—you can stay for a month while you find something."

A month. Four weeks to pack up my life, to find a new job, to start over. Four weeks to figure out who I am without Suyin, without the bank, without the last remnants of the championship glory.

"I'm sorry," Suyin says, and I believe her. "I really am. But I think this is better. For both of us."

She stands and leaves the room. I hear the bedroom door close. I sit at the table, looking at the envelope, and I realize I'm holding my breath.

In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four.

Counted breathing. Conscious breathing. The breathing of someone whose body is no longer automatic, no longer certain, no longer infinite.

---

On Sunday, I go to the Nankai pitch one last time. The grass is patchy, worn down to dirt in the places where feet have passed ten thousand times. I walk to midfield, to the spot where the championship run started, and I stand there for a moment, just breathing.

In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four.

I start running. Midfield to goal. The same cuts, the same angles. My knees are screaming but I keep going. Past the spot where the defender was. Past the spot where the goalkeeper dove. I finish at the goal line and I stand there, bent over, hands on my knees, breathing hard.

In the memory, I'm not breathing. I'm just running. The ball at my feet, the goal ahead, my body knowing exactly what to do. Perfect motion. Perfect certainty.

I understand now what I couldn't understand then. The championship wasn't the beginning of something. It was the peak. It was the single moment when everything aligned—my body, the game, the crowd, the future. And everything since has been a descent from that moment, a slow fall from perfection to adequacy to obsolescence.

I practiced the wrong thing. I built my life on something temporary, something that would fade, something that couldn't carry me forward. And now I'm twenty-four with bad knees and no wife and no clear future, standing on an empty pitch, running the same route over and over, trying to recapture something that's gone.

Wei was right. The system used me up. Football used me up. Marriage used me up. Suyin's world used me up. I gave everything and when there was nothing left they were done with me.

But I'm not broken yet. Not like Wei. I can still walk without a cane. I can still work. I can still start over, build something new, find a different path.

I just don't know what that path is.

I do the route again. Midfield to goal. My knees throb. My breath comes in gasps. In-two-three-four, out-two-three-four. The sun is setting. The shadows are lengthening across the pitch.

In the memory, I'm not breathing. I'm just running.

But this isn't the memory. This is now. This is real. This is my body, failing but still moving. This is my breath, labored but still coming. This is my life, diminished but still continuing.

I finish the route and I stand at the goal line, looking back at midfield, at the path I've run a hundred times, trying to recapture a moment that's gone forever.

The sky is that particular blue you get just before sunset. Deep and rich and temporary.

I close my eyes and breathe.

In-two-three-four.

Out-two-three-four.

And then I walk home, away from the pitch, away from the memory, toward whatever comes next.
