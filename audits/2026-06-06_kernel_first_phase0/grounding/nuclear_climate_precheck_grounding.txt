## FACTUAL BACKGROUND

### Climate Context and Nuclear's Role


Nuclear power is enjoying increasing interest around the world as countries seek to strengthen energy security and decarbonize their economies, with the IAEA projecting a capacity increase of 2.5 times the current level by mid-century in its high case scenario.
 
Nuclear power is now officially recognized as crucial for global decarbonization, complementing renewables such as wind and solar.



The inclusion of nuclear energy in the first Global Stocktake at the 2023 United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP28) is clear evidence of this growing acknowledgement.


### Investment Requirements


Global investment in nuclear energy must increase to 125 billion USD annually, up from the around 50 billion USD invested each year from 2017-2023, to meet the IAEA's high case projection for nuclear capacity in 2050.
 
The landmark agreement between the World Bank and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) in June 2025 signals a new era: financial institutions are now firmly backing nuclear energy.


### Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)


Small modular reactors are emerging as flexible, resilient and cost-effective solutions, with strong potential in both advanced and developing economies.
 However, 
SMR concepts envisage planned electrical outputs of 1.5-300 MWe, meaning that a 3-1000 times larger number of units would be required to provide the same electrical output as today's plants, requiring the construction of several thousand to ten thousand SMR units.

## KEY ACTORS

### Pro-Nuclear Environmental Groups


In June 2020 Zion Lights, spokesperson of Extinction Rebellion UK declared her support for nuclear energy as critical part of the energy mix along with renewable energy sources.
 
In March 2021, 46 environmental organizations from the European Union wrote an open letter to the President of the European Commission calling to increase share of nuclear power as the most effective way of reducing EU's reliance on fossil fuels.



Organizations such as the Natural Resources Defense Council and the Union of Concerned Scientists now talk in terms of the proper role of government in energy policy and ensuring the safe operation of nuclear plants, rather than whether nuclear power should even be considered.


### Anti-Nuclear Environmental Groups


A July 2025 analysis from the Capital Research Center examined more than 300 nonprofits that opposed nuclear energy and conservatively estimated that the total combined annual revenue of the American opponents of nuclear power exceeded $3.3 billion, with some of the largest including the World Wildlife Fund, the World Resources Institute, the Environmental Defense Fund, the Natural Resources Defense Council, the Sierra Club, the Rocky Mountain Institute, Grid Alternatives, and the League of Conservation Voters.



In August 2021, the League signed on to a letter opposing provisions in the bipartisan infrastructure bill that "would provide billions of dollars in subsidies to aging and uneconomical nuclear power plants," calling nuclear energy a "false solution" and stating "Nuclear power is too dirty, too dangerous, too expensive, and too slow to solve the climate crisis."


### Scientific and Policy Advocates


In 2013, four scientists, including former NASA climatologist James Hansen, Kerry Emanuel of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Tom Wigley of the University of Adelaide in Australia and Ken Caldeira of the Carnegie Institution published an open letter stating renewable-energy sources like wind and solar "cannot scale up fast enough to deliver cheap and reliable power at the scale the global economy requires" and concluded that "there is no credible path to climate stabilization that does not include a substantial role for nuclear power."

## STRUCTURAL TENSIONS

### Cost and Timeline Challenges


When it comes to building a nuclear power plant in the United States—even of a well-known design—the total bill is often three times as high as expected.
 
A survey of plants begun after 1970 shows an average overnight cost overrun of 241%, with the VC Summer project in South Carolina abandoned in 2017 with sunk costs of $9B, and the Vogtle project in Georgia severely delayed.



Despite nuclear energy's potential to meet rising U.S. electricity demand, high costs, long timelines and unresolved technical challenges cast doubt on its viability, with critics worrying the costs and time required make the solution impractical.


### Speed vs. Necessity Debate


The cost savings from nuclear energy emerge primarily in the final phase of decarbonization (after 2040 in most modelled countries), but construction timelines mean that planning and building must begin decades earlier, well before market signals alone would trigger investment.



According to Ed Lyman, nuclear power safety director at the Union of Concerned Scientists: "The fundamentals are still not there to support a massive expansion of nuclear energy in a short amount of time frame. It's just not there … Too many difficult questions are just waved away."


### Renewables vs. Nuclear Positioning


Groups like the Sierra Club use their millions to continue peddling the myth that the United States can run its entire economy solely on solar and wind energy, while ignoring the landscape- and seascape-destroying energy sprawl — plus the huge number of bird and bat kills — that would accompany an attempt to rely on renewables alone.


Conversely, 
SMRs face significant hurdles, including the high costs of advanced fuels like high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) and the challenge of scaling production to achieve meaningful economic benefits, raising important questions about whether SMRs can deliver on their promises or risk diverting resources from established renewable energy solutions.


### Financing and Market Structure


Providing incentives for long-term, high-capital investment in deregulated markets driven by short-term price signals presents a challenge in securing a diversified and reliable electricity supply system.
 
Recent years have seen utilities actively pursuing state legislation and contracts to provide financial support for economically unviable reactors, with among the 23 reactors scheduled for early retirement between 2009 and 2025, 13 already closed, eight experiencing delayed closure due to subsidy programs.


### Competing Visions of Decarbonization

The fundamental tension is whether decarbonization requires a diverse portfolio including nuclear (the pro-nuclear position) or whether resources should focus on rapidly scaling renewables and storage (the anti-nuclear position). This disagreement cuts across traditional environmental coalitions and reflects deeper questions about technological pathways, risk tolerance, and the timeline for climate action.
