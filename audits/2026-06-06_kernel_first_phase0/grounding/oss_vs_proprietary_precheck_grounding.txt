## FACTUAL BACKGROUND

### Historical Origins


In the 1950s and 1960s, computer operating software and compilers were delivered as part of hardware purchases without separate fees, and source code was generally distributed with the software, providing the ability to fix bugs or add new functions.
 
This trend died in the 1970s when proprietary software became more common, prompting the Free Software Movement of the mid-1980s to return to the 1960s system of software distribution.



Richard Stallman launched the GNU Project in January 1984 as a deliberate effort to build a complete, Unix-compatible operating system under terms ensuring user freedoms, formalized in his definition of "free software" as respecting the rights to use for any purpose, study its workings, redistribute copies, and distribute modified versions.


### The "Open Source" vs. "Free Software" Terminology Split

A critical historical divergence occurred in 1998. 
The "open source" label was created at a strategy session held on February 3rd, 1998 in Palo Alto, California, shortly after the announcement of the release of the Netscape source code. The conferees believed the pragmatic, business-case grounds that had motivated Netscape to release their code illustrated a valuable way to engage with potential software users and developers, and they believed it would be useful to have a single label that identified this approach and distinguished it from the philosophically- and politically-focused label "free software."



As the enormous success of the open source term buried Stallman's free software term and his message on social values and computer users' freedom, later Stallman and his FSF strongly objected to the OSI's approach and terminology.


### Commercialization and Mainstream Adoption


In 1999, the IPOs of VA Linux and Red Hat proved that open source was now going commercial and mainstream, and IBM announced that they were supporting Linux by investing $1 billion in its development.
 
By 2011, Microsoft ranked among the top five corporate contributors to the Linux kernel, focusing on hypervisor integration for Azure, driven by empirical realities—Linux powered 60-70% of cloud servers by the early 2010s—rather than ideological alignment, as Microsoft prioritized developer ecosystems and revenue from services over pure proprietary lock-in.

## KEY ACTORS

### Ideological/Philosophical Leaders

**Richard Stallman & Free Software Foundation (FSF)**


Richard Matthew Stallman is an American free software movement activist and programmer who campaigns for software to be distributed in such a manner that its users have the freedom to use, study, distribute, and modify that software.
 
In 1980, Stallman and some other hackers at the AI Lab were refused access to the source code for the software of a newly installed laser printer, the Xerox 9700. This experience convinced Stallman of people's need to be able to freely modify the software they use.



Stallman is the main author of free software licenses which describe those terms, most notably the GNU General Public License (GPL), the most widely used free software license.
 
For him, "Free Software" is a social imperative involving human rights.


**Linus Torvalds & Pragmatist Approach**


Linus Torvalds views software as an engineering challenge. He supports "Open Source" because it creates better code through collaboration, not because of a moral crusade. He is willing to use proprietary tools (like BitKeaker) if they do the job.


### Institutional Actors


The OSI and the Free Software Foundation have become the free and open source developer community's two principal advocacy organizations.
 
The Free Software Foundation (FSF), the Apache Software Foundation (ASF), and the Linux Foundation became the largest foundations for open-source software.


### Corporate Actors


Since 2017, Microsoft is one of the biggest open-source contributors in the world. In 2018, Microsoft acquired GitHub, the largest open-source development platform to this date.
 
For decades, some companies have made servicing of an open-source software product for enterprise users as their business model. These companies control an open-source software product, and instead of charging for licensing or use, charge for improvements, integration, and other servicing.

## STRUCTURAL TENSIONS

### 1. **Freedom vs. Pragmatism**

The fundamental divide is philosophical. 
The free software movement campaigns for freedom for the users of computing; it is a movement for freedom and justice. By contrast, the open source idea values mainly practical advantage and does not campaign for principles.



"Open source" addresses software being open as a practical question rather than an ethical dilemma – non-free software is not the best solution but nonetheless a solution. The free software movement views free software as a moral imperative: that proprietary software should be rejected, and that only free software should be developed and taught in order to make computing technology beneficial to the general public.


### 2. **Copyleft vs. Permissive Licensing**


The free software movement champions copyleft licensing schema. In its strongest form, copyleft mandates that any works derived from copyleft-licensed software must also carry a copyleft license, so the license spreads from work to work like a computer virus might spread from machine to machine.



Critics of copyleft licensing challenge the idea that restricting modifications is in line with the free software movement's emphasis on various "freedoms", especially when alternatives like MIT, BSD, and Apache licenses are more permissive.


### 3. **Control & Ownership vs. Transparency & Collaboration**

**Proprietary Model:**

A proprietary license model is based on the concept that the software company creates software and maintains control over its code, and therefore, its features and use.
 
Proprietary software grants its creator, publisher, or other rightsholder a legal monopoly by modern copyright and intellectual property law to exclude the recipient from freely sharing the software or modifying it.


**Open-Source Model:**

Open-source software presents a stark contrast. Its source code is intentionally made public, inviting anyone to view, use, modify, and distribute it. This model is built on principles of transparency, community collaboration, and shared innovation.


### 4. **Security: Transparency vs. Obscurity**

This is a major contested area:

**Open-Source Argument:**

One of the most significant advantages of open source software is its transparency. The open nature of the code allows anyone to inspect, audit, and improve it. This openness fosters a collaborative environment where developers worldwide can contribute to identifying and fixing vulnerabilities. Such community-driven efforts can lead to more robust security measures and quicker response times to emerging threats.


**Proprietary Argument:**

Proprietary software is often more secure than open source as the source code is not available for hackers to view.
 However, 
the closed nature of the code can obscure vulnerabilities, delaying their detection and resolution. Users must rely on the vendor to identify and address security issues, which can sometimes result in slower response times compared to the open source model.


### 5. **Cost & Accessibility vs. Support & Reliability**

**Open-Source Advantages:**

Most OSS is free, reducing IT budgets. Source code modification enables tailored solutions. Users can audit the code for security or compliance. Large developer communities provide updates, bug fixes, and documentation.


**Proprietary Advantages:**

Proprietary systems tend to be more turnkey. Proprietary software offers dedicated support for customers, so you aren't at the mercy of community discussion boards and YouTube videos.
 
Proprietary products often come with a high level of support, which means you can rely on the vendor for help when needed.


**Proprietary Disadvantages:**

Users of proprietary software rely heavily on the vendor for updates and security patches. This dependence means trusting that the vendor will address issues promptly and effectively. Proprietary software can also lead to vendor lock-in, where users are bound by their contracts and unable to switch to other solutions until these contracts expire.


### 6. **Innovation Speed & Quality**


The development of proprietary software is centralized, with a dedicated team of developers employed by the owning company. While this can ensure a high level of consistency and quality, it also often leads to slower innovation compared to the rapid, community-driven advancements seen in open-source projects.


### 7. **Business Model Viability**


Some companies have realized that free software can also be financially smart when offered. IBM, for instance, still gets billions of dollars from Linux, which is completely free for the user.
 
More than 90 percent of companies use open-source software as a component of their proprietary software. The decision to use open-source software, or even engage with open-source projects to improve existing open-source software, is typically a pragmatic business decision.


### 8. **User Freedom vs. Convenience**


The "free" in "free software" refers to freedom, not price; specifically, it means that you have the freedom to study, change, and redistribute the software you use. These freedoms permit citizens to help themselves and help each other, and thus participate in a community. This contrasts with the more common proprietary software, which keeps users helpless and divided: the inner workings are secret, and you are prohibited from sharing the program with your neighbor.

## SUMMARY OF TENSIONS

The debate fundamentally hinges on competing values:

- **Ideological/Rights-based** (Free Software): Software freedom as a human right and social good
- **Pragmatic/Market-based** (Open Source): Software openness as a practical development methodology
- **Control** (Proprietary): Vendor control, revenue protection, and liability management
- **Transparency** (Open Source): Community oversight, rapid innovation, and security through visibility
- **Support** (Proprietary): Guaranteed vendor support and accountability
- **Autonomy** (Free/Open): User independence and customization freedom

These tensions remain unresolved because they reflect fundamentally different assumptions about the purpose of software, the nature of innovation, and the relationship between creators, users, and society.
