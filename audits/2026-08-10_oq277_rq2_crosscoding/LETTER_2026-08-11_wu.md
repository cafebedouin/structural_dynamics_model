# Note on arXiv:2606.14589 — an internal disagreement between your catalog and your labeled dataset

To: Wei Wu
Re: *When Errors Become Narratives: A Longitudinal Taxonomy of Silent Failures in a Production LLM Agent Runtime* (arXiv:2606.14589v1)
Date: 2026-08-11
From: the operator of an unrelated single-operator research repository (contact below)

---

## Why you're getting this

We're writing a methodology paper on a structurally similar setup — one operator, LLM
instances producing essentially all the work, an incident record accumulated over months, and
a six-pattern taxonomy of what we call *success-shaped absence* derived from those incidents.
When we found your paper in a literature check, our first question was whether we were simply
restating your result. To answer it properly we pre-registered a two-directional blind
cross-coding experiment: your five classes coded against our six patterns and ours against
yours, blind in both directions, with churn measurement and planted controls.

Building the direction-(i) units meant reconstructing your 22 incidents from your published
artifacts rather than from the paper's prose. That reconstruction turned up something we think
you want to know, and that nobody but an outsider rebuilding your data would have hit.

None of what follows is a criticism of the taxonomy. We think the five classes are good, we
are using them, and the most interesting thing we found is arguably a *result* about incident
taxonomies in general rather than a defect in yours.

## The finding

Your failure-modes catalog and your labeled dataset carry the same 22 incident IDs and assign
**different classes to 10 of them**. Agreement is 12 of 22, 55%.

Class distributions, recomputed from the frozen files rather than from the paper:

| source | A | B | C | D | E | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| catalog | 1 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 8 | 22 |
| labeled dataset | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 22 |

The catalog reproduces the paper's Table 1 exactly, which is why we took it as canonical and
the dataset as the robustness index. That reading is ours, from §3.2, and you should correct it
if it's wrong — the direction of the discrepancy matters less than which artifact a reader
should trust.

This is a single author, coding their own incidents, against their own five classes, with full
knowledge of the system. Those are the most favourable conditions a taxonomy will ever be
applied under. 55% self-agreement under those conditions is, we think, a more interesting
number than any cross-taxonomy comparison we were planning to run.

## The disagreement is structured, not scattered

We expected noise. It isn't noise.

Of the 10 disagreeing incidents, **8 involve class E** on one side or the other,
bidirectionally — E absorbs and sheds roughly equally. Only 5 of the 10 possible class pairs
are occupied at all. **D is the most stable class**, appearing in only 1 of the 10. The C/D
pair, which we predicted would be the seam on the theory that error-swallowing and chained
hallucination blur where a swallowed error gets narrated, is the *rarest* tie at 1 of 10. That
prediction was wrong and we're recording it as wrong.

Our reading of why, offered as inference rather than finding:

**E is defined on a different axis from the other four.** A, B, C and D sort by mechanism —
what went wrong. E (operational omission and forensic blind spots) sorts by *declared versus
runtime*: whether the system's account of itself matched what it did. That axis cross-cuts the
mechanism axis rather than partitioning alongside it. Every incident with a declaration
mismatch also has a mechanism, so it can be classified either way depending on which axis the
coder reaches for first — and a single coder, on two occasions, reached differently. D is
stable for the mirror reason: it names a mechanism nothing else names, so nothing competes for
it.

If that's right, it isn't a coding error. It's a taxonomy carrying two orthogonal cuts in one
five-member enumeration, and the instability is where they cross.

## The single sharpest row

`movespeed_tcc_sandbox` — the 60-day incident that is your §5.1 headline — is assigned **A** by
the labeled dataset, while that same row's `paper_class_ref` field cites **§4.5, class E, and
only E**.

The row disagrees with itself. The catalog sides with the row's own citation over the row's own
label.

We flag it separately because it's the case where no statistics are needed and because it's
your most-cited incident.

## Two mechanical predictors

We looked for a predictor of disagreement using only parseable fields, deliberately avoiding
any reading of your root-cause prose — judging which class a `真根因` paragraph "pulls toward,"
with the answer already in hand, is a biased instrument and we didn't trust ourselves with it.

- Rows carrying a `次类` (secondary class) note: **6 of 8 disagree.**
- Rows whose `paper_class_ref` cites a class other than the one assigned: **5 of 6 disagree.**
- Base rate: 10 of 22.

Nominal p-values 0.048 and 0.043. Three defeaters stated before the numbers rather than after:
uncorrected for multiple comparisons, post-hoc predictor selection, and small n. Treat these as
descriptive.

What they suggest, if they survive: the disagreement isn't coding drift but the trace of
**genuine multi-class membership** — incidents that legitimately belong to two classes, where
the artifact recording a secondary class is the one that noticed. Your own records are marking
the multi-membership; the single-label field just can't carry it.

We have specified a blind test of that and are explicitly **not** running it in the current
experiment, because it's a third coding direction and our pre-registration forbids adding one
without a new freeze.

## Three readings we cannot distinguish from outside

1. **Coding instability** — the same incidents coded twice, months apart, with normal drift.
2. **Multi-class membership** — both labels correct, one enumeration slot.
3. **Artifact provenance** — the two files were produced at different times for different
   purposes and were never expected to agree.

Only you can settle which. If it's (3), the fix is a line in the paper saying which artifact is
authoritative for reuse, and this note costs you five minutes. If it's (2), it's a finding worth
a subsection, and it strengthens rather than weakens the taxonomy — a five-class scheme that
detects its own multi-membership is doing more work than one that doesn't.

## One thing we hit that affects anyone reusing your data

The paper reports 22 incidents in which the meta-pattern manifested **at least 28 times**. For
cross-coding, the unit is ambiguous: 22 incidents or 28 manifestations. We pinned the incident
as the unit and recorded manifestation count as metadata, with the rule frozen in the
pre-registration, because the choice sets every per-class effect-size floor downstream. A
sentence in a revision stating your intended unit would save the next person that decision.

## Reciprocity, since this is one-sided otherwise

The same class of defect is present in our own work, found in the same week, and we'd rather
say so than send you a one-directional audit.

Our six patterns are published in one document and specified in another, and the two documents
**disagree on two of the six**. Same index, different pattern, all four disjoint members
carrying real dated exemplars. Nobody noticed until this experiment forced the question "which
set do we code against?" On inspection it isn't even a fork — it's an index collision, two
distinct patterns that were assigned the same number. And the branch that won publication turns
out to have the thinnest exemplar record of the four, which suggests the index was reused
precisely because nothing was firmly attached to it.

Separately, the incidence statistic our paper leans on has been corrected twice on its
denominator, rests on a positional shell parse whose field index depends on invocation shape,
and has three independent blind spots in its selection filter. It has survived every correction
at the same headline value, and we had been reading that survival as reassurance. It isn't. It's
a number whose instrument was never positive-controlled.

Which is the general point, and the reason we think your 55% is worth a subsection rather than
a footnote: in three separate cases now — yours, ours, and a benchmark paper one of us reviewed
last year — a published number turned out to rest on an instrument that had never been shown
capable of returning the answer it wasn't returning. The defect isn't in any of the three
analyses. It's that internal consistency checks are nobody's deliverable.

## What we're asking, and what we're offering

**Asking:** confirmation that we have the right pair of artifacts and the right canonical
reading, and your view on which of the three readings above is correct. If we've
misidentified which file is authoritative, everything above changes shape.

**Offering:** the reconciliation table — all 22 incident IDs with both labels, the 10
disagreements, and the pair structure — in whatever format is useful. And when our
cross-coding runs, the confusion matrices in both directions, including whatever they say
about our six. We pre-registered that disagreement is the result rather than a problem, and
that commitment holds symmetrically.

We'd also like to know if you object to your five classes being used as an external comparison
set in a pre-registered experiment. We'll cite the paper either way; we'd rather know your
position than assume it.
