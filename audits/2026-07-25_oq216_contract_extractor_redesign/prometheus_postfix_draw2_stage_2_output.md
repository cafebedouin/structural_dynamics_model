# SECTION 0: INVARIANT CONTRACT

**The floor invariant (primary):**
The world contains no neutral ground beneath its ruling order. The order that governs the reservoir-terraces of the Kesh Valley — the caste of Water-Wardens who decide whose fields drink and whose don't — did not descend from any shared founding principle. It was seized, generations back, by the ancestors of those who now hold it, in an act the present order has spent every year since converting into the appearance of a natural, ancient, and legitimate right. **Falsifier:** if the finished story allows a character (or the narration) to locate a moment when the Wardens' rule *was* legitimate — a golden charter, a fair founding vote, a divine grant witnessed and consented to — and treats the present problem as a deviation from that legitimate origin, the floor invariant has been lost. The seizure must remain seizure, all the way down, with no bedrock of consent beneath it.

**The grain (present, subordinate, non-primary):**
There exists, underneath and apart from the Wardens' order, a real and separate fact: a person or a channel, once given the capacity to grow and choose on its own terms, cannot afterward be un-given that capacity by whoever granted it. This is not the primary invariant of this telling. It appears, does its work in one register of the story, and is folded beneath the floor's larger unresolved question by the story's end — its resolution recedes rather than closes, while the floor's question about the legitimacy of rule continues to stand.

**The substrate that holds the floor:**
The Wardens' authority is carried, physically, in the **Reckoning-Stones** — a chain of grey markers sunk along the terrace walls at the valley's founding, each stone said to "remember" the water-right assigned to the land beneath it. No living person can read a Reckoning-Stone directly; the Wardens alone hold the chant-sequence that "asks" a stone what it remembers, and the stone's answer arrives only as the Warden's spoken verdict — never as a mark, number, or line anyone else can independently check. The stones are true instruments: pressed and asked honestly, they render an honest answer about what was recorded there at the founding. What they cannot do, what no instrument sunk in that ground could ever do, is tell you whether the founding itself — the first laying of stones, the first claim of chant — was rightfully made. The stones remember the seizure perfectly. They cannot remember that it was one.

**Substrate inhabitation sentence:**
*You kneel at a stone that has kept the same cold verdict since before your grandmother's grandmother, and you know — the way you know your own pulse — that the stone has never once lied to a Warden, and that this is exactly why no one, Warden or otherwise, has ever been able to ask it who gave the first Warden the right to ask.*

# SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

**Setting.** The Kesh Valley, a high terraced watershed threaded by a single perennial channel fed from glacial melt above the snowline. No date is fixed to any calendar a reader would recognize; the valley counts years by "Reckonings" — the ceremonial re-affirmations of water-right performed every third harvest. Three hundred and eleven Reckonings have passed since the valley's founding, by the count carved at the base of the oldest stone. The terraces descend in a shallow bowl from the high spring-house to the lowland marsh-fields; water that falls at the top must, by gravity and by law, pass through every terrace below it, and the order in which it is *released* — not merely the fact of its flowing — is the entire architecture of power in the valley.

At the top of the bowl sit the spring-house and the Warden compound: stone-built, roofed in slate, warm even in the wet months because the Wardens control not only water but the timber allotment that depends on water-rights being current. Here live perhaps forty families, the hereditary Warden lineages, who wear undyed wool as a mark of caste rather than poverty — the *absence* of ornament is their ornament, a visible claim that they need no display because their position needs no defending. They rise with the sun not from necessity but from ritual: the first chant-sequence at a Reckoning-Stone must be spoken before the shadow of the spring-house touches the second terrace, a rule with no discoverable purpose except that it has always been so.

Below them, arranged terrace by terrace down the slope, are the tenant-holders — families who work the land, own the roofs over their heads but not the water beneath their feet, and negotiate every third year for the release-order that will determine whether their particular terrace floods in time for planting or waits, sometimes fatally, behind six others. Their houses are timber and packed clay, cold in the wet season, built low against the wind that the stone compound above them is tall enough to ignore. A tenant-holder's whole planning horizon is the release-order: which terrace goes first this Reckoning, and whether theirs has been bumped down again.

At the very bottom, past the last maintained terrace, is the marsh-fringe — land the valley's law does not formally forbid anyone from working but does not protect either, because no Reckoning-Stone was ever sunk there. Families here live by what runoff escapes the system entirely: water no Warden bothered to claim because claiming it cost more chant-effort than it was worth. They are outside the terrace order, and therefore outside its protections, entirely.

**Constraint naturalizations.**

*C1 — the Reckoning itself (the release-order law).* This is the valley's founding order: the rule that water must be released terrace by terrace in a sequence the Wardens alone set and re-set, ostensibly to prevent flooding, actually to determine who thrives and who barely survives each cycle. Locals simply call it **the Order** or, more precisely, **whose turn it is**. A Warden experiences the Order as the thing that makes the valley livable at all — without a release sequence, everyone would dam and hoard and the whole terrace system would collapse into war. A tenant-holder near the top experiences the Order as a genuine, if unequal, bargain: their terrace usually gets early water, so they resent the Wardens less and comply readily, extracting real coordination value even as they concede real subordination. A tenant-holder trapped on a chronically late terrace, generation after generation, experiences the Order as an unbreakable law of the valley itself — as fixed as the mountain that shadows their fields — because in their whole family's memory, their terrace has *never once* gone first, and no one now living remembers it being otherwise.

*C2 — the Hush (mandated ignorance of chant-sequence).* Only Wardens may learn the chant that "asks" a Reckoning-Stone its verdict; teaching it to a tenant-holder is called **stone-theft** and punished as gravely as theft of water itself. Locals call this restriction **the Hush**. The Hush is maintained less by patrol than by shape of life: a tenant-holder's whole upbringing is arranged so the chant is simply never nearby to be overheard — Wardens hold their ceremonies at dawn behind compound walls, and children of tenant families are kept to the lower terraces at that hour by custom so old it isn't even instructed anymore, only inherited. A Warden experiences the Hush as pure, necessary order-keeping: unregulated stone-reading would mean chaos, false verdicts, forged rights. A sharp-eyed tenant-holder who has puzzled out fragments of the chant from a lifetime of half-heard dawn ceremony experiences the Hush differently again — sees, analytically, that the Hush *does* prevent certain real abuses, even while it locks her out of the one thing that could verify whether her terrace's low standing was ever fairly recorded.

*C3 — the Drowning (punishment for stone-theft).* Should a tenant-holder be caught learning or using the chant without Warden sanction, the sentence is the Drowning: exile to the marsh-fringe, water-right permanently struck from the family's terrace, kin barred from the Reckoning ceremony forever. It is called this not because anyone is literally drowned but because it is understood as being cast down into the water that has no order at all — the marsh, where nothing is guaranteed. From the sentenced tenant's position this is the single most total catastrophe the valley can inflict, worse than death by starvation because it is inflicted *with* the whole Order's weight behind it, and once fallen there is no path back up the terraces. From a Warden's position, applying the Drowning barely registers as violence at all — it is spoken of in the same flat tone as sealing a cracked stone or re-thatching a roof; it is *maintenance*.

**Character positions (variable mapping)**

- **Agent_A → Warden Ilsevet Cray**, senior of the compound, keeper of the founding chant-sequence, third in an unbroken line of chant-holders. Middle-aged, plainspoken, genuinely believes the Order is the only thing standing between the valley and ruin, and has never once had to test that belief against a stone that could tell him where the first right came from.

- **Agent_B → Meret Doun**, a mid-terrace holder — her family has bargained within the Order for six Reckonings, prosperous enough to have learned fragments of the dawn chant by half-listening as a girl, subordinate enough that her prosperity depends entirely on staying inside Warden favor. She is the valley's fulcrum: enough standing to act, not enough exit to act safely.

- **Agent_C → Old Pell**, elder of the lowest maintained terrace, has spent her whole life on land that has gone last in the release-order every Reckoning she can remember or her mother could remember. She calls the Order "the mountain's cousin" — not made by hands, in her telling, though it plainly was.

- **Agent_D → Sennik Pell**, Old Pell's grandson, a young man of the same terrace, whose horizon is the whole span of the valley's remembered history rather than his own lifetime — he thinks in terms of what his line has endured across all three hundred and eleven Reckonings, not merely his own hardship, and this longer view still cannot loosen the trap, because the terrace itself, not the calendar, is what holds him.

**Linguistic strategy: Direct Translation (with light Naming layer).**

Base register: plain, agrarian English carrying calqued terms straight out of the valley's own conceptual grammar, rather than English abstractions standing in for them. Key untranslatable-into-single-word terms, carried as compounds:

- **stone-theft** — not "theft" alone; the crime is specifically the theft of a stone's *voice*, distinct from theft of water or grain, and locals never conflate the categories.
- **turn-luck** — the fortune (good or bad) of a terrace's position in a given Reckoning's release order; used almost as a weather-word ("bad turn-luck again this Reckoning").
- **the long field** — tenant idiom for a family's entire remembered history of turn-luck, as in "we have been in the long field's shadow since before my mother."
- **chant-blind** — what a tenant-holder is, by design, with respect to the Reckoning-Stones; not "ignorant" (which implies a lack that could be filled by effort) but a state deliberately arranged, the way a room is made dark.

Grammatical feature carried into English: tenant-holder speech marks *duration* obsessively, favoring constructions like "has gone third for eleven Reckonings running" over simple past tense — the language itself encodes the generational weight the Order imposes. Warden speech, by contrast, favors the timeless present ("the Order holds," "the stone remembers") — grammar as a quiet claim of permanence.

Example exchange:
> "Your terrace has gone last nine Reckonings running, Pell. That's not the Order's doing. That's turn-luck."
> "Turn-luck doesn't set itself the same way nine times, Warden Cray. Something is doing the setting."

**Inhabitation sentences**

- **Ilsevet Cray:** *You are the keeper of a chant that keeps a whole valley from drowning itself, and you have never once had to ask what the chant would say about the hand that first taught it.*

- **Meret Doun:** *You have learned enough of the dawn-chant by accident to know exactly which rule is protecting people and which rule is only protecting Wardens, and the moment you act on that knowledge you will fall further than anyone who never learned it at all.*

- **Old Pell:** *You have watched your terrace go last for so many Reckonings that you no longer ask whether it could go otherwise, the way you no longer ask whether the mountain could move.*

- **Sennik Pell:** *You can name every Reckoning your family has lost across three centuries, and knowing the whole shape of the loss has never once been the same thing as being free of it.*

- **Substrate (repeated for continuity):** *You kneel at a stone that has kept the same cold verdict since before your grandmother's grandmother, and you know the stone has never once lied to a Warden — and that this is exactly why no one has ever been able to ask it who gave the first Warden the right to ask.*

# SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

**RESOLVED**

- **Ω_E01:** Does a terraced, gravity-fed irrigation hierarchy plausibly sustain a hereditary caste over 300+ cycles without written record? → Resolved: yes, provided the "record" is deliberately oral/ritual (the chant-sequence) rather than documentary — this is *the point* of C2 (the Hush), not a plausibility gap. Impact: strengthens rather than strains the naturalization.

- **Ω_E02:** Would a "release-order" system realistically produce chronic, multi-generational losers (needed for Agent_C/D's Snare status) rather than rotating fairness? → Resolved: yes — the spec requires exactly this (χ=1.20 unchanged across biographical and civilizational time horizons because exit is trapped). Built the release-order as Warden-discretionary rather than fixed-rotation, so chronic disadvantage is structurally possible and, per the floor invariant, not accidental.

- **Ω_C03:** Should the Drowning be lethal or exile-based? → Resolved: exile (to the marsh-fringe), not execution. This preserves C3's Naturalized/Snare divergence as a *status* collapse rather than a death, which better serves TR4/TR5 (Meret's later leverage and negotiated re-entry require her to still be alive and locatable).

- **Ω_C04:** Does the grain (untranslatable_real, non-primary) need its own named substrate distinct from the Reckoning-Stones? → Resolved: yes, lightly — carried through Sennik's later-story capacity (implied, not detailed here) to act on inherited knowledge in ways Cray cannot later revoke; kept minimal per instruction that it remain subordinate and recede rather than resolve.
