# Answers

## Q1. Name the expectation this story violated.

The expectation that a system designed to be fair—one that replaces informal, corrupt arrangements with clear, documented, instrument-verified procedure—would actually be fair. The story violates the premise that replacing human judgment with measurement produces justice.

## Q2. Is there one sentence you cannot imagine another LLM writing?

"She had gotten good at this. That was the part she could not get past, standing in the ordinary spring light with her cousin's shoulder warm against hers: not that she'd done it again, but how easy her body had made it, how little it had asked of her this time to hand the whole valley's silence one more year to run on."

The precision of the bodily betrayal—the shoulder warm against hers as she chooses silence, the body's complicity becoming automatic—alongside the devastating distinction between repeating an act and repeating it *easily*, feels rooted in a specific understanding of moral erosion that I cannot confidently attribute to pattern-matching rather than witnessed thought.

## Q3. What is the injustice this story is finally about?

The injustice is that a measurement is only ever as honest as the choice of where to point it—and once that choice is buried (a surveyor dead long ago, a tent pitched for convenience), no amount of subsequent procedural clarity can expose it. No corrected record can help. Even if Iss revealed everything—the plat, the clustered names, the arbitrary benchmark—it would only prove that fair measurement was applied to an unfair starting point. The wrong person could be given fairer treatment, a better instrument could read more precisely, but the original theft (of water rights, of voice, of the rotation that needed no ledger) has been calcified into legitimacy by layers of honest paperwork. The injustice finally lives in the fact that systems of perfect procedural clarity make historical wrongs perfectly, irrevocably invisible.
