## I. Meret

The frost came late that year, which meant the low path held its ice longer in the shadow places, and Meret Sanne knew every shadow place by the ache it put in her ankle before her eye ever found the shine. The flat stone below the split birch. The crook past the old fence post, rotted to a black stub, where the ground stayed soft long after the fields above had dried. She had learned these places the way she had learned her mother's hands — not as facts but as weather.

She left before light, the yoke settling wrong on her shoulder, and walked the low path down past the leased hut, down to where Tarn Water forked and one side ran cold and clean out of a spring the color of a knife.

That spring was not hers. The clerk who'd read her the figure years back had said one number; the survey papers Iss had shown her since said another, close but not the same. Neither number had ever been information to her, only verdict, and you do not ask a verdict to explain itself.

So she walked past it — the good water running loud on her right hand — down to the seep her family had watered from since her grandmother's time, a slower, browner flow that silted the buckets and put a taste in the milk the market buyers complained about without knowing why.

The walk back was the true cost. The climb with the buckets full, the yoke biting new grooves into shoulders that had, over the years, actually changed shape to hold it, a low permanent ridge her daughter was already growing to match. The ache arrived earlier this year. Not much. The kind of earlier you only know by the place on the path where you first feel it — the black crook instead of the flat stone above it.

She did not do the sum of what that meant across a life. She only noted, the way she noted a lamb born with its legs wrong, that the world had shifted very slightly against her, again, and that no shift of this kind had ever shifted back. The ache was hers, at least. It had never once belonged to anyone else, had never been read off a survey or certified by a stranger with an instrument.

At the fork she passed the stone shelf on the valley's western wall, called the Notch, though from the low path you could not see the mark cut into it, only the pale scar of old stone standing gray against the darker rock. She did not look at it. She had stopped looking at it the way you stop looking at a house you'll never live in.

Her cousin-by-marriage Iss was coming home this week, down from the capital where she did paper work for people with standing to need paper worked. Meret had a petition drafted — not by her own hand, her letters were rough — asking, again, that the water-court reconsider the boundary of what they still called the temporary allocation, though nothing about it had ever once moved.

She did not expect it to work. But Sarn Vey had told her, once, that you filed the petition anyway, every few years, not because paper changed anything but because filing it kept your name in the room. *If your name stops appearing,* Sarn had said, *they forget you were ever asking.*

Meret set the buckets down at the door of the hut and went in to put the kettle on before the second haul, because one trip never carried enough — not in her grandmother's time, not in her mother's, not now — past this exact spring that was, by the honest and uncorrupted reading of a survey instrument, simply not theirs.

## II. Ossit

Ossit Fenn liked the licensing office best in the hour just after the stove caught, when the room warmed unevenly from the corner out and he could sit with the morning's queue not yet arrived and put the previous day's files back into order.

He was good at his work. Disputes resolved, up from the year before. Complaints of favoritism, essentially none, because there was nothing in the system for a clerk to favor. Everyone stood exactly where the certified elevation put them. It was, he thought, *clean*. Before the Compact formalized the Ledger, water disputes had apparently gone on for whole seasons, resolved by shouting matches and grudges and who could out-wait whom at a spring in the dark. Now a dispute took an afternoon. You read the certified elevation. You read the recorded right. The numbers met or they didn't, and either way the room emptied with everyone knowing exactly where they stood. Better a clear no than an endless maybe.

The morning's first file was a routine one: a low-pasture woman, name of Sanne, petitioning again for reconsideration of an allocation set generations back. He pulled the certified survey and matched the figures against the file. They agreed with each other, if not quite with the number the woman herself had cited — off by a small margin she must have misremembered somewhere along the way, since only the certified figure had standing.

He paused there, pen lifted. A margin was not nothing, if you were the sort of person for whom that margin was the whole difference between asking and not asking. The itch passed the way an ember passes when there's no more air to feed it — the file did not require him to wonder where her number had come from, only to confirm that his did not match it, and the confirming was the whole of the job.

He wrote the denial in the clean procedural hand he had trained into himself, the hand that made even bad news look tidy: *the elevation stands as certified; no change in recorded right; petition without new grounds.* He liked that the language was gentle even when the outcome wasn't. It did not blame the woman for asking, which she was entitled to do — the water-court's doors were open to any petition, at no cost but the asking.

He stamped the file and did not think about the woman again, not because he was unkind but because there was nothing left in the file to think about. The numbers had settled it.

Later that morning Dael Korrin came through with the new Charter draft under his arm, and Ossit made coffee for them both and listened with real interest, because it was good work — finally, someone was going to give the low families' rotation legal shape. Licensed turns, recognized in water-court, so that when disputes arose there would be a document to consult instead of an old woman's say-so.

"It protects them," Dael said, turning a page. "Right now if families disagree about whose turn it is, there's no forum. They have to work it out themselves, and God help the one with less standing."

"And now there's a forum," Ossit said.

"Now there's a forum." Dael smiled, pleased with the phrase, and Ossit felt the small clean pleasure of watching a good system get better — the last ragged informal corner of the valley folded into the same fair clarity that governed everything else.

Her file passed through his hands again over the following months, each time a little thinner, the numbers by then so settled that even the clerk processing them scarcely needed to check.

## III. Iss

The capital had taught Iss Braddal to read a plat the way a physician reads a chest — the eye going first to what's wrong. She'd been home only days and had already read documents she hadn't been asked to read: the Charter draft Sarn Vey kept folded, unopened, on the mantel like something that might go off; the water-court's denial of Meret's petition, the same figure it had always been, near enough; the surveyor's fresh certification of the Notch's elevation, precise to a fraction no one in this valley could use for anything except confirming what everyone already believed; and, that morning, the plat itself — the original survey, brittle at the folds, that she had asked the archive clerk in the capital to pull before she left, telling herself it was professional curiosity and knowing, the whole train ride home, that it wasn't.

She'd spread it on Sarn's kitchen table, the only surface wide enough, and Sarn had watched her without asking what she was looking for — patient, unhelpful, waiting to see whether the watching would matter.

The plat showed the western wall, the notation for the benchmark, a small cramped hand noting elevation readings, tent positions, grazing claims already staked by families whose names — Braddal among them, her own name — appeared clustered thick around the mark, while further down the valley the same hand had simply written *unclaimed*, without further comment.

There was no note about why the surveyor had chosen that shelf specifically. She knew, from cross-referenced archive records, that royal survey teams of that period customarily benchmarked from their camp for simple convenience — easy to find again, easy to defend from weather. She had no proof this was what happened here. She had, instead, a very old habit of noticing that the families camped nearest the shelf were exactly the families who ended up with permanent heritable right, and that this was either an extraordinary coincidence or not a coincidence at all, and that no document anywhere could tell her which.

"You're looking at it like it owes you something," Sarn said, from the stove.

"I'm looking at it like paper," Iss said. "It doesn't owe anyone anything. That's what it's good at."

Sarn made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Your cousin says the same kind of thing. Different words. He says the Notch is just where the water is." She turned from the stove. "You know he doesn't mean anything by it."

"I know."

"He's not cruel."

"I know that too."

She rolled the plat closed, carefully, the old paper wanting to crack at the fold, and did not say the thing she had thought on the train home and had gone on thinking every day since: that the Notch had never been a measurement of anything except where a tent had once stood, and that every fair, honest reading of it since had been fair and honest in exactly the way that made it impossible to ever call it wrong. You could not accuse an instrument of lying when the instrument had never lied. You could only ask who had chosen where to point it, and that person had been dead a very long time, and had left no motive on record.

She had kept her hand strictly to the practical when helping Meret draft the petition — the citation of prior appeals, the correct clerk's name, the correct filing window — giving the petition its very best chance of a hearing, which was still no chance at all.

Coyt found her on the porch that evening, smelling of lambing pens, cheerful in the particular unbothered way he'd always had.

"Iss Braddal, home again," he said. "Come to fix the water-court from the inside?"

"Come to see the family," she said.

He sat on the step below her, stretched his legs out, looked up toward the western wall where the pale scar of the Notch caught the last of the light. "You know," he said, easily, "I used to think that mark was just where the water starts being counted. Never thought about it being *put* there." He said this the way he might have remarked on the sun coming up in the east.

The answer rose in her before he'd finished speaking — the tent, the convenience of a benchmark, the names clustered thick as a fist around it — and sat in her mouth, wanting badly to be spoken, costing nothing at all to speak except whatever it would cost afterward to watch her cousin's easy face try to hold it.

"It's late," she said instead. "You should get back before Aunt Ren holds supper."

He went, whistling, unbothered, and she stayed on the step a long while with the folded plat still in her coat pocket, its brittle fold pressing a thin line against her ribs, until it had gone from an ache to simply a shape she was used to carrying.

## IV. Meret

The Charter clerk came to the low pastures in the last good week before the frost, a young man Meret didn't know, polite, carrying a satchel of forms and a tone she recognized instantly as the tone used on people you were doing a favor for whether they'd asked for it or not.

He gathered them — Meret, Sarn, and the other low families — in Sarn's front room, and explained, with real warmth, that the Compact wanted to give the old rotation legal standing. Filed. Licensed. Recognized. "Right now, if there's ever a dispute between low families about a turn, there's no forum. This protects you."

Meret watched Sarn's hands in her lap. They did not move, which for Sarn was its own kind of speech — Sarn's hands moved constantly, mending, sorting, worrying a hem — and their sudden stillness now was louder than anything the clerk said.

"We'd need the schedule," the clerk went on, kindly. "Whoever holds it in memory. Just to get it down properly, once, and then it's protected forever."

Nobody answered. Then Sarn said, in a voice Meret had never heard her use, flat and careful, the voice of someone reading aloud something she has decided, in advance, not to feel while she says it: "I can give you the turn." And she began — spring water first, then summer, the order of families, the years each had held which spring, further back than anyone now living — and Meret watched her hands the entire time, still motionless, while her voice went on giving away the one thing in this valley that had never needed a clerk's satchel to survive.

The clerk wrote quickly, pleased, thanking her twice, telling her she was doing a generous thing for the families who'd come after her.

He left before dark, satisfied, and the front room did not empty the way rooms usually emptied. Sarn sat a long while with her hands still in her lap, not moving to the stove, and the other women did not leave either, though there was nothing left to be done and no reason to stay except that leaving felt, just then, like one more thing being taken from a room that had already given up enough for one day.

It was full dark before Sarn rose. She stood at the window a while, looking at nothing Meret could see out there in the black, and when she finally crossed to the stove her hands, freed at last to move, fumbled the match against the strike-strip, once, twice, before it caught — small clumsy failures Meret had never once seen from those hands in all her years of watching them do everything asked without a wasted motion.

Meret stayed the night, sending word home with Sarn's youngest. She lay on the floor by the banked fire and heard Sarn, in the black hour before dawn, get up and go to the drawer where the filed schedule now lived, and heard the drawer open, and heard nothing further — just the drawer open a long while and then, eventually, closed again, softly, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to enter.

In the morning Sarn made tea and mended a hem and moved through the house exactly as she always had, and if a stranger had walked in they would have seen nothing to remark on. Meret understood, watching her, that this was perhaps the exact nature of the thing that had happened: it left no mark you could point to. It had never needed a mark to be real.

The denial of Meret's own petition came that same week, delivered by a different clerk in a different tone — brisk, unkind by omission. *No new grounds.* She had known before she read it. She folded it and put it in the drawer with the others, and did not draft another, not this year, though she knew Sarn would tell her to, next spring, out of a stubbornness Meret privately thought had less to do with hope than with refusing to let the room forget her name.

That evening she made the second haul alone, the buckets heavier, the ache arriving at the black crook exactly where it had arrived all that season — no earlier, no later, simply there, waiting, the way it always would be now, every year a little sooner, for as long as she or her daughter kept walking that path.

## V. Ossit

The spring re-certification required little of Ossit beyond witnessing competence at work. The Compact's surveyor, a meticulous woman named Trest who had held the post many years, climbed the western wall with her transit and her chain, while Ossit stood below with the ledger open on a folding table.

He watched her set the tripod's feet exactly, sight and re-sight before she'd trust a reading — a woman who had never once, in all the years he'd stood beneath her, been wrong. She called the figure down, and it matched, as it matched every year, the figure struck into the stone long before either of them was born.

"Steady as ever," Trest said, climbing down, and then, brushing chalk dust from her palms, added, almost to herself: "Funny thing about that shelf. Best sightlines in the whole valley from up there. Whoever picked it knew what he was doing."

Ossit had never thought about it that way before — not the number, which he thought about constantly, but the *shelf*, the actual stone, chosen for something as ordinary as a clear sightline, and not for any quality of the water below it — and for one unguarded moment the thought sat wrong in him, some small object underfoot he couldn't quite place. He almost asked her what she meant. Whether *knew what he was doing* meant knew the water, or knew the view.

Trest was already packing the transit, unbothered, and the ledger sat open waiting for its number, and there was nothing on the form for a sightline, only a line for the elevation. He wrote the elevation. The wrong-sitting thing did not settle the way it usually did — it stayed with him through the packing of the table, through the walk back to the office, sitting under his ribs the whole way like a swallowed stone, so that by the time he sat down to close the ledger his hand would not go easy to the stamp. He held it a moment longer than the file required before he brought it down.

"Steady as ever," he'd agreed, out loud, to no one, and the agreement had felt, for the first time he could remember, like something he was insisting on rather than something he simply knew.

He processed the Charter's completion paperwork that same week — Sarn Vey's rotation, now filed, now licensed, now subject to the standard administrative fee, prorated for the season already underway. It was a modest fee, and he thought the Compact had been generous in setting it — a fair price for a genuine service, legal standing where none had existed before.

He closed the file, set it in the outbound stack, and moved on to the next. The stone under his ribs was still there, smaller now, worn down some by the ordinary weight of the afternoon's remaining work, but not gone — and he found, closing the office for the night, that he had left the lamp burning longer than usual over that one file, for no reason he could have given anybody who asked.

## VI. Iss

She almost told Coyt the following spring, standing at the base of the western wall while Trest's transit clicked and settled above them and they waited, the way half the valley waited every year, for the number to come down and confirm what everyone already knew.

"Same as always?" Coyt said, easy, not really asking.

"Same as always," Iss said.

He looked pleased by this in the uncomplicated way he was pleased by most steady things. "Funny old thing," he said, nodding up at the pale scar in the stone. "Whole valley runs off that little cut. You ever wonder who made it?"

The plat was still folded in her coat, its crease gone soft from a year of being carried and never shown. She had the archive dates, the pattern of names clustered thick around the mark, and every professional habit that would have let her lay it all out for him plainly, the way she'd have laid it out for a client who'd paid her to.

She thought of Sarn's hands stilled in her lap, giving away in one sitting what memory alone had kept safe longer than any ledger. She thought of the match failing against the strike-strip in hands that had never once fumbled anything else. She had told herself, a year ago on this same step, that it wasn't hers to say — that pointing out a trap after someone has already stepped in it serves no one. Standing here now with Coyt's face turned up bright and untroubled toward the stone, she found that story would not hold its shape today. It had never been a story about Sarn. It had only ever needed her own silence to be blameless.

This was not that. This was before. Coyt had not yet stepped in anything. She could still, standing here, be the reason he didn't.

"Someone had to," she said.

Coyt nodded, satisfied, and looked back up at Trest, who was calling the figure down, clear and unchanged. Down in the valley Ossit's clerk would already be writing it into the ledger in his clean procedural hand; somewhere on the low path Meret Sanne would be deep into her walk, the yoke settling into its permanent grooves; in Sarn Vey's front room the filed schedule sat in a drawer where a whole unwritten order used to live, needing no drawer at all.

Iss felt the plat's fold pressing its thin line against her ribs, the same place it always pressed, and let the number come down clean, and said nothing further. The silence took its familiar shape in her mouth — small, dense, already cooling, like something she'd swallowed too fast to taste. She had gotten good at this. That was the part she could not get past, standing in the ordinary spring light with her cousin's shoulder warm against hers: not that she'd done it again, but how easy her body had made it, how little it had asked of her this time to hand the whole valley's silence one more year to run on.
