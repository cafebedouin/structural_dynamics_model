# Answers

**Q1. Name the expectation this story violated.**

The expectation that institutional reform—documentation, standardization, clarity—produces justice. The story violates the assumption that making something "official" or "written down" corrects prior wrongs. Instead, it shows how formalization can enshrine injustice while appearing to transcend it.

**Q2. Is there one sentence you cannot imagine another LLM writing?**

"She stood there afterward with the rope slack in her fist, not gripping it, not releasing it, just holding the shape of it the way you hold something you're no longer sure is yours to hold."

The sentence's specificity—the distinction between the rope's material state and Maren's epistemological uncertainty, the metaphorical reach of "holding the shape of it"—feels earned by the story's moral precision rather than generated from pattern-matching.

**Q3. In two or three sentences: what is the injustice this story is finally about?**

The injustice is the theft of epistemic authority—the theft of the ridge-dwellers' right to know and navigate their own world through embodied practice (the dog's restlessness, the wind felt in the knees, the silence that meant *hold*). The Charter does not correct the original theft of land; instead, it systematizes it by replacing felt knowledge with administrative categories, making the original wrong invisible and therefore permanent. Nothing can put it right, because the very instruments designed to remedy injustice are the ones that erase its history and make correction impossible—Maren wins her clause but loses the ability to trust her own hands.
