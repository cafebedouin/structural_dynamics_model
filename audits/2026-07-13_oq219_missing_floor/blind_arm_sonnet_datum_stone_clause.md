Q1. **Expectation violated:** The story sets up the frame of a bureaucratic-injustice narrative — the sympathetic petitioner denied by an indifferent system, the clerk who might have a crisis of conscience, the insider (Iss) who possesses the damning evidence — and primes the reader to expect a reckoning: exposure of the survey's origin, a confrontation, some turn toward correction or at least open accusation. Instead the story repeatedly arrives at the threshold of disclosure (Ossit's unease with the stamp, Iss on the porch with Coyt, Iss again at the transit) and each time backs away into silence. The expectation of *narrative payoff through revelation* is violated on purpose, more than once, as if to make the withholding itself the subject.

Q2. Yes — this one:
*"She did not do the sum of what that meant across a life."*
This sentence's flatness, its refusal to let the character (or the narration) perform grief, and the way it names an absence of calculation as itself a kind of knowledge — that combination of restraint and precision feels like a specific authorial hand rather than a generic move.

Q3. The injustice is that the original wrong — a survey mark placed for a surveyor's convenience, not for any truth about water or right — has become permanently unfalsifiable: it was never a lie, so it can never be caught in one, and everyone downstream (Ossit stamping, Trest re-measuring, Iss staying silent, the Charter clerk formalizing memory into a fee-bearing document) reproduces it faithfully and honestly, which is exactly what makes it irreversible. Nothing in the text could put it right — not a fairer official (Ossit's conscience achieves only a longer-burning lamp), not a better instrument (Trest is precise and always was), not a corrected record (the plat exists and changes nothing) — because the wrong lives in the *origin of the benchmark itself*, a choice made by someone "dead a very long time" who "left no motive on record," and every subsequent act of fairness only re-certifies it.
