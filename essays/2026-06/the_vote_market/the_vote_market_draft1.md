# The Vote Market: What Democracies Actually Trade

*Draft 1 — UKE_W v2.1 discipline applied. Mode B (invisible scaffolding). Metadata block at end, not for publication.*

---

For forty years, the best empirical work on American policy responsiveness has produced two findings that do not fit together. The first: when the preferences of low- and middle-income Americans diverge from those of the affluent, the relationship between what the less advantaged want and what government does is statistically near zero — a flat line that holds within every policy domain examined, economic, foreign, social welfare, and moral alike (Gilens 2012; Gilens & Page 2014). The second: outcomes that the consolidated donor class and the consolidated commentariat bitterly opposed keep happening anyway — Brexit, the 2016 American election, a string of European populist insurgencies, and, on a forty-year fuse, the overturning of *Roe v. Wade*. A theory of democracy has to explain both: a system that ignores the median voter on divergence, and a system the elite periodically loses control of.

"Democracy" doesn't explain this pattern. Neither does "oligarchy." The argument here is that both labels misdescribe the mechanism, because both treat the question as one of who rules. The better description is a market: votes are bought and sold, the tradable unit is not the individual ballot but the organized bloc, narrative production is the technology that sets prices, and elections are less an instrument of popular sovereignty than an insurance policy — the cheapest known method, measured in variance rather than dollars, for rotating elites without bloodshed. On this reading the live political question is never "do the people rule?" It is: what is the current price schedule of consent, and who can afford it?

## The Wrong Question

Start with why the familiar dichotomy fails. The accusation that democracy is "really" oligarchy stings only against a baseline in which the popular will translates into policy. That baseline has been in trouble on its own terms for seventy years: Arrow showed there is no coherent aggregate "will" to be thwarted; Converse showed most voters do not hold the structured, stable preferences a faithful agent could betray; Downs explained why they rationally wouldn't. Schumpeter drew the conclusion in 1942: democracy is not the rule of the people but a competitive method for choosing among rival elites, with the demos ratifying rather than ruling. If that is what democracy *is*, then calling it oligarchy by proxy is not an exposé. It is a definition.

But Schumpeter's framing concedes too much in the other direction, because it makes the demos pure audience. The data say otherwise — sometimes, under specific conditions, mass preferences move policy. Gilens himself documents that responsiveness to lower-income preferences rises measurably when political pressure peaks: impending presidential elections, evenly divided control of Congress. The interesting question is therefore not whether elites govern (they do, everywhere, always — Michels' iron law has no documented exceptions at scale) but what determines *when* the governed get what they want. That is a question about prices.

## The Market and Its Floor

The market model says: politicians are firms assembling minimum-cost winning coalitions; donors supply capital; voters sell a commodity whose price varies by organization, salience, and information. Thomas Ferguson formalized a version of this in 1995 as the investment theory of party competition — parties as blocs of investors, with mass preferences winning on issues where no investor bloc has a stake, because conceding them is cheap. The model is not a metaphor. It generates predictions, and the predictions can be checked against the responsiveness literature. Three findings carry the weight, and each is an independent line — refute one and the others stand.

**First: the flat line has a floor condition.** Gilens' one domain of partial exception is social welfare, where policy is somewhat more responsive to the poor and middle class. But his own analysis attributes the exception to a coincidence: on healthcare, education, and Social Security, the preferences of the less well-off happen to align with those of powerful organized interest groups. The influence flows through the organizations, not from the mass. Read as market structure rather than as a footnote, this is the model's central fact: *mass preferences win only when coupled to an organized purchasing bloc.* Below some threshold of organization, your preferences are not cheap — they are not for sale at all, because there is no counterparty who can warrant delivery. Call this the organization floor. It explains why the moral/religious domain shows the *largest* responsiveness penalty for the poor in Gilens' data — unorganized moral majorities have no one to transact through — while the next section's worked example shows what happens when a moral bloc organizes.

**Second: the inequality is mostly veto.** Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien's 2017 reanalysis of the Gilens data found that the rich and middle agree about 90 percent of the time, and when they disagree, the rich win only 53 percent of the time — but the inequality that does exist operates mostly through negative power: the rich and middle blocking policies the poor favor. In market terms this is exactly what a cost function predicts, because blocking is structurally cheaper than enacting. A veto requires capturing one chokepoint in a system deliberately built of chokepoints; enactment requires buying the whole chain. The status quo is the cheapest product on the shelf, which is why the buyers' side, whoever it is, accumulates there.

**Third: the pattern is general and it moved on schedule.** A 2023 pooled analysis of Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, and Sweden (Lupu & Pontusson, eds., *Unequal Democracies*) found policy corresponding far better to affluent preferences than to low- and middle-income preferences in all four countries — and found that the one historical exception, the relative responsiveness of left governments to lower-income citizens in the economic and welfare domain, was present before 1998 and has since disappeared. The direction of skew is now uniform across rich democracies. What varies, and what used to vary more, is magnitude — and the variance tracked the price of organized labor's votes. Where unions, mass parties, and churches functioned as seller-side cartels, votes were expensive and policy bent toward their members; where labor was atomized, votes were cheap and capital's purchasing power dominated. The post-1998 convergence is the cartels' decay showing up in the regressions.

Falsification conditions, stated plainly: the market model predicts (a) unorganized mass preferences should lose at high rates even on low-stakes issues, while organized-bloc preferences win even against affluent opposition; (b) responsiveness should rise with electoral proximity and competitiveness, when the price of votes spikes; (c) measures of seller-side organization — union density, bloc cohesion, party-membership depth — should predict responsiveness gradients across countries and across time. If unorganized majorities were found winning at rates comparable to organized blocs, or if organization measures had no predictive power on responsiveness, the model fails. The existing evidence runs the predicted direction on all three, but (c) in particular deserves to be called what it is: consistent, not yet conclusive.

## The Price-Suppression Technology

A market with chronically low prices on one side needs an explanation, and the standard one — propaganda, manufactured consent — explains too much. If elite media manufactures mass preferences, then every mass outcome is either elite-permitted or elite-fabricated, and no observation can count against the theory. It also fits the facts badly: in 2016 the consolidated prestige media opposed the winning candidate nearly uniformly and lost, which is hard to square with a unified consent factory.

The defensible version requires no coordination at all. Event-driven journalism — bound to the story unit, dependent on access, optimized for speed and engagement — systematically omits the structural context that would let a reader understand what an outcome is worth. Not by order; by selection. Each outlet's incentive structure reproduces the omission independently, which is why the pattern survives elite fragmentation: rival elite factions manufacturing rival narratives still share a single information regime, one that prices in events and prices out structure. The market consequence is information asymmetry of a specific kind — sellers who cannot price what they are selling. A voter who has been given accurate facts and no structure does not know what their vote purchases or what it forecloses, and systematically undercharges.

The demand side of the cheap-vote market has a documented geography. PRRI's 2021 survey work on QAnon belief found its strongest predictors clustering exactly where the model says prices should be lowest: no college, rural residence, lower income, declining group prospects, and — critically — a media diet narrowed to one channel-family and not much else. That last factor is the mechanism in miniature. A single-channel information environment is the limit case of the asymmetry, and the QAnon purchase is the limit case of the transaction: a voting bloc acquired with pure narrative, no material concession whatsoever, the story not even required to be true. The scapegoat is the cheapest coalition glue ever discovered — it costs the buyer nothing and the designated out-group everything — which is why ruptures and insurgencies reach for it first.

This section's central claim — that media structure functions as price suppression in the vote market — should be marked for what it is: a structural hypothesis, coherent with the documented media incentives and the documented demographics of low-information belief, but not yet demonstrated as a causal mechanism. What would move it up a tier: content analysis showing structural-context omission correlates with measurable voter mispricing of policy stakes, or natural experiments where information environment changes shift the price of a bloc's votes. What would falsify it: voters in structurally richer information environments selling their votes no dearer than voters in event-only environments.

## The Scale Ceiling

Why don't the sellers cartelize? They did, once, and the conditions are instructive. The deep constraint is coordination cost as a function of polity size — and there is an inversion of Madison hiding in it. Federalist No. 10's celebrated cure for faction is to extend the sphere: multiply interests across a large republic until no majority faction can cohere. The argument is usually taught as a safeguard against majority tyranny. Read against the market model, it is a vote-price suppression mechanism by design — the extended republic protects the propertied minority precisely by raising the coordination costs of everyone else, and Madison says nearly as much. Denmark organizes six million people in one labor market under one bargaining regime. A seller cartel in the United States would need to organize 330 million people across a continent, against employers who can arbitrage fifty state regulatory regimes and, since the late twentieth century, an open capital account. Scale is not the only variable — Germany sustains sectoral bargaining at 84 million — but the cross-national pattern of union density, polity size, and responsiveness is the right test bed, and the hypothesis is stated here as exactly that: a hypothesis, with the cross-national regression as the evidence that would settle it.

## Repricing Events

The model's strongest test is the cases that look like its refutation: the historical moments when the demos seemed to win outright. Examine the two largest, and both turn out to be price events, not regime changes.

The New Deal and Bretton Woods settlement — capital controls, confiscatory top marginal rates, the postwar labor accord — is routinely cited as the demos voting its way to a different capitalism. The architecture says otherwise. The settlement was drafted by elites (Keynes, Harry Dexter White), sold by an elite (Roosevelt, explicitly, as saving capitalism from itself), and implemented through existing institutions. But it was not a gift, either. It was a purchase at a spiked price: union membership had roughly quintupled in a decade, the 1934 strike wave had demonstrated coordination capacity, and a competitor regime was offering the world's workers an alternative settlement entirely. The elite repriced because the cost of not buying mass acquiescence now carried a revolutionary tail. And the reversal is timestamped: when the competitor collapsed and capital mobility returned, the price collapsed with it — which is what the post-1998 disappearance of left-government responsiveness in the European data records. The demos's greatest victory, examined closely, was a renegotiated rate. The rate has since been renegotiated back.

The ruptures point the same direction, darker. Brexit, 2016, and their cousins are routinely read as the masses breaking elite control. Look at the capitalization tables. Brexit's leadership was a hedge-fund and press-baron faction arrayed against the CBI consensus; 2016 was a counter-elite with its own media stack against an incumbent elite with the legacy one. On the market model these are hostile takeovers: a rival elite spots mass grievance the incumbent coalition has been ignoring — because ignoring it was, until that moment, free — and arbitrages it. The demos appears in these events as the undervalued asset, not the acquirer. The civil rights movement, the strongest apparent counterexample, fits uncomfortably well: a genuinely organized seller bloc (the floor condition, met) whose purchase was closed partly because Cold War state elites needed the product for legitimacy abroad. Mass organization was necessary. It was not sufficient until a buyer appeared.

## The Insurance Policy

Which brings the argument to what elections actually are. Not cheap — the most recent American federal cycle cost on the order of fifteen billion dollars — but low-variance. Consider the removal problem as a risk profile. Removing an American president requires an election: enormously expensive, bounded variance, and even its worst recent stress test stayed within bounds. Removing the ruler of Russia or China requires a coup, a succession crisis, or a collapse: low routine cost, unbounded tail. Karl Popper's minimalist defense of democracy — its value is not that it expresses the popular will but that it removes rulers without bloodshed — is exactly this point, restated as variance reduction. Democracy converts the catastrophic tail risk of succession into a continuous premium payment.

And note who the policyholder is. The faction that loses an election survives to compete in the next one; under the alternative mechanisms, losing factions historically do not. Rotation insurance is a product elites buy for themselves, which is the unsentimental answer to why elites tolerate elections at all, and why the system's most dangerous moments are precisely the ones where a faction concludes it cannot afford the next premium.

## A Worked Example: The Forty-Year Purchase

One case runs the whole machine end to end. In 1979 white evangelical Protestants were a large, electorally diffuse population whose policy preferences — per the Gilens domain data, the kind of unorganized moral preferences that lose worst of anyone. The founding of the Moral Majority that year, and the broader religious-right institution-building around it, was the construction of a seller cartel: leadership that could warrant delivery, a punishment capability (primary challenges), and a clear asking price (judicial appointments and abortion policy) in exchange for turnout. The purchase agreement held across four decades and both parties' realignments. Delivery came in June 2022, in *Dobbs* — an outcome that majorities in national polling consistently opposed, achieved through the one channel (lifetime judicial appointments) most insulated from electoral repricing.

Every component of the model is visible in the arc: the organization floor (diffuse preferences became tradable only after cartelization), the cost function (the bloc was paid in policy nearly costless to capital — no investor bloc's returns depended on abortion law), the veto asymmetry (the purchase was executed through blocking and appointment chokepoints, not legislation), and the insurance logic (the bloc stayed loyal through candidates it had every doctrinal reason to reject, because the agreement, not the agent, was the asset). The model also makes a forward prediction, stated here as a hypothesis with a clock on it: a bloc's leverage should collapse after delivery — paid sellers have nothing left to withhold — and the post-*Dobbs* electoral pattern of abortion-referendum losses and suburban realignment is the early data. If evangelical-bloc leverage over Republican candidate selection is undiminished through the next two presidential cycles, the prediction fails and the model loses its dynamic component.

## Alternative Explanations Considered

**The coincidence reading.** Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien's own interpretation of their reanalysis is deflationary: the income groups agree 90 percent of the time, the rich win disagreements barely more than a coin flip, so perhaps there is no oligarchy to explain — just broadly shared preferences. This is the simplest explanation and it deserves the strongest engagement. It is insufficient on three documented grounds. The negative-power finding is itself a structured inequality: the asymmetry concentrates in blocking, exactly where a cost function predicts and where a coincidence story predicts nothing. The domain analysis shows the skew is uniform across all four policy areas, which coincidence would not produce. And the cross-national 1998 break — responsiveness to lower incomes disappearing on a schedule that tracks capital liberalization — is a dose-response relationship, not a coincidence pattern. A fourth objection exists — that the 90 percent agreement is itself partly produced by the information environment — but it is noted and *not relied upon*, because it is the unfalsifiable move this essay's media section explicitly declines to make.

**The capture reading.** The strong Gilens-Page interpretation — economic elites dominate, full stop — fails against the same evidence from the other side: the social-welfare responsiveness channel, the election-proximity effect, the ruptures, and the *Dobbs* case, in which an organized non-elite bloc extracted forty years of performance from both the agent and, eventually, the policy. Capture predicts none of these; a market with an organization floor predicts all of them.

**The institutional-design reading.** Perhaps this is all just an American pathology of money-saturated, weak-party majoritarianism, and "democracy" is fine elsewhere. This was the strongest objection available until recently; the 2023 four-country European data substantially closed it. Institutional design still moves the magnitude — that is the seller-cartel variable — but the direction is now uniform across rich democracies, and the historical exception decayed on the schedule the market model predicts.

## Evidence Framework

**Documented in Public Records (Tier 1):**

- When low- and middle-income preferences diverge from affluent preferences, the preference-policy link for the less advantaged is virtually zero; the pattern holds within economic, foreign policy, social welfare, and moral/religious domains: Gilens, *Affluence and Influence* (Princeton, 2012); Gilens, *Swiss Political Science Review* (2015).
- The 10th percentile's responsiveness penalty is smallest in social welfare and largest in religious-values issues; the social-welfare exception is attributed to alignment with organized interest groups, not influence of the poor: Gilens (2012).
- Responsiveness to lower-income preferences rises with impending presidential elections and even partisan division; parties behave as policy maximizers oriented to activists and interest groups: Gilens (2012).
- Rich and middle income groups agree ~90% of the time; the rich win disagreements 53% of the time; observed inequality operates mostly through negative power (blocking policies favored by the poor): Branham, Soroka & Wlezien, "When Do the Rich Win?", *Political Science Quarterly* 132:1 (2017).
- In Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, and Sweden, policy corresponds better to affluent than to low/middle-income preferences; left-government responsiveness to lower incomes in the economic/welfare domain existed before 1998 and has since disappeared: Lupu & Pontusson (eds.), *Unequal Democracies* (Cambridge, 2023), ch. 2.
- The investment theory of party competition (parties as investor blocs; mass preferences prevail where investor blocs are indifferent): Ferguson, *Golden Rule* (Chicago, 1995). Cited as published theory, not as a finding.
- QAnon belief predictors: no college, rural, lower income, narrow (Fox/far-right-only) media diet, Republican identification: PRRI, "Understanding QAnon's Connection to American Politics, Religion, and Media Consumption" (May 2021).
- Moral Majority founded 1979; *Dobbs v. Jackson* decided June 2022; national polling majorities consistently opposed overturning *Roe*: contemporaneous polling archives (Gallup, Pew).
- US union membership roughly quintupled between the early 1930s and mid-1940s; the 1934 strike wave (Toledo, Minneapolis, San Francisco) demonstrated mass coordination capacity: BLS historical series; standard labor histories.
- 2024 US federal election cycle spending on the order of $15 billion: OpenSecrets estimates. (Figure approximate; verify before publication.)

**Pattern Inferences from Tier 1 Facts (Tier 2):**

- The organization floor: mass preferences become tradable only when coupled to an organized bloc that can warrant delivery (inference from: the social-welfare interest-group exception, the worst-of-all outcome for unorganized moral preferences, the Olson logic of concentrated vs. diffuse coordination costs, and the *Dobbs* arc).
- Veto asymmetry: blocking is structurally cheaper than enacting in a chokepoint-rich system, so observed inequality concentrates in negative power (inference from: Branham et al.'s blocking finding plus the institutional structure of the US lawmaking process).
- The New Deal/Bretton Woods settlement as an elite-drafted repricing under organized-labor pressure and regime competition, not a demos victory (inference from: documented elite authorship, documented union growth and strike capacity, documented framing of the settlement as preserving capitalism).
- The post-1998 European reversal tracks the post-Cold War, post-capital-liberalization collapse in the price of labor's votes (inference from: the documented timing in Lupu & Pontusson against the documented liberalization timeline).
- Elections as low-variance elite rotation (inference from: documented cost and outcome-boundedness of electoral removal vs. documented historical variance of non-electoral succession).

**Structural Hypotheses Requiring Additional Evidence (Tier 3):**

- Media structure functions as vote-price suppression: event-driven, access-dependent journalism omits structural context, producing sellers who systematically misprice their votes. (Moves to Tier 2 with: content-analysis evidence linking structural omission to measurable stake-mispricing, or natural experiments on information-environment change. Falsified by: equal vote-pricing across structurally rich vs. event-only information environments.)
- The scale ceiling: seller-side cartelization cost rises with polity size and internal regulatory arbitrage, bounding responsiveness in continental polities regardless of institutional design. (Moves to Tier 2 with: cross-national regression of responsiveness on union density × polity scale with controls. Falsified by: large polities achieving Nordic-gradient responsiveness at low organization cost.)
- Post-delivery bloc devaluation: the evangelical bloc's leverage declines materially within two presidential cycles of *Dobbs*. (Falsified by: undiminished bloc influence over candidate selection and platform through 2028/2032.)

## What This Changes

If the analysis is right, the standard reform program is aimed at the wrong side of the market. "Getting money out of politics" attacks the buyers' purchasing power, which a century of campaign-finance whack-a-mole suggests is the most adaptive variable in the system. The binding variable on the evidence above is seller-side organization — the floor below which preferences are not tradable at all. The European data's grim addendum is that even strong seller cartels decayed once capital could exit the jurisdiction; any organizational answer that ignores the exit option is re-running 1998.

## Unresolved Questions

First, is the 90 percent preference agreement between rich and middle exogenous, or partly a product of the information regime? This essay declines to assume the latter, because the assumption is unfalsifiable as usually deployed — but the question is empirically pregnant: preference movement following documented information-environment shifts (cord-cutting, platform migration) is measurable in principle, and nobody has measured it well.

Second, can seller cartels form at continental scale under current media structure, or is the combination of polity size, capital mobility, and fragmented information a hard ceiling? The answer determines whether the post-1998 convergence is an equilibrium or a trough.

Third, the question existing institutions could answer but have not: every responsiveness study cited here was possible only because Gilens spent years assembling preference-policy data that no government collects. A state that wanted to know whose preferences its policy tracks could instrument the question — the survey infrastructure exists, the policy coding exists, the method is twenty years old. No democracy audits its own responsiveness. The refusal is not mysterious under the model above: the audit would publish the price schedule. But it means the central empirical fact of democratic life — who actually gets what they want, and when — is known only by academic accident, on a lag of decades. Whatever else changes, that is the measurement a system confident in its own description would already be making.
