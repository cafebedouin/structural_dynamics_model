Book IV
Prelude
Books I and II built an autonomous, clear-seeing self. That self is necessary but not sufficient, because a deliberately lived life takes place in a social world that must, in part, be made. The making requires other people. The question is how to act with others without surrendering what the first two books built — and without being conscripted into performing coordination for arrangements that are extracting from you.
The difficulty is structural, not personal. Extraction always has a cover story. The arrangements that will cost you most present themselves as the ones most requiring your loyalty. Book IV is about how to see the difference before you are inside the arrangement long enough to have adapted to the cost.

I. On Reading People
The original outline had no chapter here. It needs one, and it needs to come first.
The Book III catalog gives you Tells for institutional arrangements. This chapter gives you the equivalent for individuals — not as character verdicts but as functional pattern recognition. The three observables from the character essay (how they handle favors, how they treat powerless people, how they end relationships) are Tells in exactly the Book III sense: observable on a specific afternoon, not requiring diagnosis or certainty, revisable in both directions.
The chapter's philosophical hinge is the distinction the narcissism essay makes precise: some people have a functioning reciprocity loop and some have substituted other people's capacity for their own. This is not a moral assessment. It is a functional description, observable over time. The person who externalizes all problem-solving doesn't have a different character from you — they have a different architecture. Accurate classification of that architecture is the prerequisite to everything that follows.
The chapter also needs to address why this classification is systematically avoided. The narcissism essay names the mechanism: the arrangement transforms giving into moral obligation, so that accurate naming feels like betrayal rather than diagnosis. The Cluster B environment is the extreme case, but the mechanism operates at lower intensities throughout ordinary social life. Book II established that the instrument of examination is compromised; this chapter establishes that the social environment actively works to compromise it further, specifically on questions of who is extracting from you.

II. On Trust
The original version of this chapter still mostly holds, but needs one significant addition.
Earned trust versus positional trust remains the right distinction. But the narcissism essay adds a complication: sophisticated extractors will perform earned trust. They will appear to update, will use the language of accountability, will produce what looks like revision conditions being met. The theater ratio climbs to maintain the coordination narrative. Behavioral criteria for trust are gameable by the people it is most important to screen for.
The addition: trust is not just accumulated evidence but accumulated evidence under conditions where gaming was costly. Someone who has been wrong, has lost something by being wrong, and has revised — that is different from someone who has performed revision without bearing cost. The chapter needs to distinguish trust built through costly signaling from trust built through performance. The tell is whether the revision conditions, when activated, actually changed something the person had investment in maintaining.
What trust makes possible that pure self-direction cannot remains the same: division of epistemic labor. You can know things I cannot observe. The vulnerability is also the same: Book II established how compromised any individual knower is. Trust extends your exposure to another person's compromised instrument. The architecture has to account for that honestly.

III. On Solidarity
The original version's distinction between identity-based and interest-based solidarity still holds, but the DR framework sharpens it.
Identity-based solidarity is borrowed furniture at scale. The mechanism is identical to what Book I warned against in individuals — the group's position substitutes for your own judgment, and when the group shifts, you must shift too. The narcissism essay extends this: movements and institutions can run the same theater ratio dynamics as intimate relationships. The performance of shared purpose can maintain extraction from members just as effectively as it maintains extraction from individuals.
Interest-based solidarity is different because it is indexed. DR's indexical realism applies directly: you share a specific structural position — same power level, same exposure to a specific constraint — and that shared position is the basis of action. This is compatible with maintained autonomy because the solidarity is about the constraint, not about identity. When the constraint changes, the solidarity appropriately changes with it. There is no borrowed furniture because there is no identity claim.
The chapter also needs the theater ratio insight applied to movements themselves. The diagnostic questions from the narcissism essay translate directly: Is the moral language intensifying over time? Does the performance serve coordination or justification? What happens when someone tries to exit — are they released with minimal friction or met with moral condemnation? A movement that cannot be left without identity destruction has drifted from coordination to extraction regardless of its stated purpose.

IV. On Voice
The original version is mostly right but missing its hardest problem.
Voice backed by genuine exit capacity has a different structure from complaint. The implicit threat is real. When enough people maintain that capacity collectively, voice becomes structurally different in kind. All of this holds.
What the original version didn't address: the narcissism essay establishes that trying to fix a fundamentally extractive structure through better communication, deeper commitment, or more embodied values only maintains the system. Voice in an extractive arrangement is not advocacy — it is performance that the arrangement requires to sustain its coordination narrative. This is why "communicating better" consistently fails in these environments. The problem is not misunderstanding.
The chapter therefore needs to distinguish voice directed at genuinely coordinating arrangements from voice directed at extractive ones. In the first case, voice backed by exit is the correct tool. In the second case, voice is often the wrong tool entirely — it provides the performance the arrangement needs to continue. The correct tool in the second case is accurate naming followed by a decision: exit, conscious acceptance with clear understanding of costs, or structural reform. What the narcissism essay eliminates is the exhausting middle position of trying to repair something designed to extract.
The chapter's closing should complete the Hirschman argument: voice without exit is complaint, exit without voice is abandonment, but the prior question is whether the arrangement is actually a coordination problem that voice can address. Many are not. Knowing which kind you are in is what the whole of Book IV has been building toward.

On the original tension I flagged
The tension between "avoid the stupid and the unlucky" and collective action dissolves on inspection. "Stupid" in context is not an assessment of intelligence — it is a functional description of someone whose problem-solving architecture externalizes all costs onto others. That is a Tell, not a verdict. It is observable, revisable, and applies the same structural logic as the Book III catalog. The series' non-moralism survives intact.
"Avoid the unlucky" remains worth examining separately. The narcissism essay's power analysis points toward a refinement: the question is not whether someone has been unlucky but whether the constraint they're inside is a Mountain they're navigating or a Snare they're adapted to. Someone navigating genuine misfortune who maintains a functioning reciprocity loop is a different case from someone who has built misfortune into a permanent conscription mechanism. The first warrants solidarity. The second is the communal narcissist, and the theater ratio tells them apart.
