# The Table of Winters

The Mutual Assurance Office of Skarholm occupies two rooms above the fish-buyers' exchange, and the smaller room holds the Table. It is not a table you sit at. It is nine columns ruled on linen-backed paper, renewed each year by the actuary at Visby, and it prices the sea. Hull-age down the left side, month across the top, waters and crew counted in the margins, and where your boat's row meets your sailing's column there is a figure, and the figure is your premium, and past a certain corner of the paper there is no figure at all, only a ruled line, and below the line the Office writes nothing, because below the line a boat is past class, and the Office does not price what the Table cannot see.

I have clerked in the smaller room for eleven years. My father was a master; I know what I am writing down.

You could sail uninsured, in my grandfather's day. Men did; it was called sailing on your own bones. You cannot now, not because any law forbids it, but because the fish-buyers downstairs will not take an unassured boat's catch at better than half price — a wreck, they say, leaves them short and they must cover the shortage somewhere — and half price does not feed a crew. So every hull in Skarholm comes up our stairs once a year to find out what its winter will cost, and the Table decides, and I want to be careful here, the way you are careful lifting a net you suspect is fouled: the Table is not a swindle. Its figures are honest. Boats of that age, in those waters, in that month, are lost at the rate the Table says they are lost; Visby counts every wreck from Riga to the Skaw, and the counting is true. Ask whether the Table lies and you will search your whole life and find nothing. That is not where it is.

Here is where it is. The Table prices a class. A class is every boat like yours. But no man ever sailed every boat like his; he sails his, this hull, tonight, in this sea, and what he knows about her is not the Table's kind of knowing at all. My father could stand on a deck he had worked for twenty years and feel through his boot-soles, in a following sea, the half-beat of hesitation the old *Svanen* kept before she answered her helm, and he trimmed his whole sailing around that half-beat without ever once saying it aloud, because there was nothing to say and no one to say it to; the knowing lived in the trimming, and nowhere else. Ask him to write it in a column and you would get either a lie or a shrug. It was not a fact about a class of boats. It was barely a fact at all; it stopped, the moment you tried to hand it to anyone, being the thing that kept him alive. The Table cannot read it. I do not say this as an accusation. I say it as you would say that a scale cannot weigh the wind. But the fish-buyers read the Table, and the Table cannot read my father, and so, one column at a time, Skarholm has learned to sail as its class instead of as itself.

Kolbein Aas was the best master of my working life, and the winter his *Tereza* aged past the ruled line, he did what the paper leaves a man to do: he let her go for firewood-price and took assurance on a new hull, table-perfect, first row of the paper, premium light as sea-foam. I watched him sign for her. And I heard what he said on the stairs after, not to me: *she is quick and she is stiff and she does not talk.* His hands had twenty winters of the old hull's grammar in them, and the new one was a page in a language of nobody's, and what the Table told him — what its beautiful first-row figure told him, in the only tongue it has — was that this silence was the safest thing he had ever stood on.

He sailed her by the paper. That is the thing I need someone besides me to know. Not recklessly — by the paper: in the assured months, in the classed waters, the full crew counted, every column satisfied, and in the third February a following sea came up off the Ryn ground, the kind the old *Tereza* would have told his boots about a half-beat early, and the new hull said nothing, because she had nothing yet to say to him, and she went over with Kolbein and two of his crew, in class, in season, fully paid.

You will want the Office to have blinked. It did not blink. It functioned. The claim was flawless: the loss stood in the assured months, the watch at the harbor mouth attested it — I should say that the watching, which the mouth-families once kept for each other unasked, is itself a paid attestation now; a claim requires a witnessed loss, so the Office pays per witnessing, and what was a thing neighbors were has become a fee neighbors collect — and the payout went out whole to the owner of record, a Visby merchant who has never stood on a deck, within the month. And in the spring the actuary renewed the Table, and Kolbein's *Tereza* — the new one — entered the count that trues the first row's figure, one loss among the expected losses, and the column closed over him like water. The Table was not wrong about him. Wrong is not a thing the Table can be. A death that has been priced correctly refutes nothing; it balances.

His son sails now. He came up our stairs in October, and I found his row and his column and read him his figure, and he paid it, and at the door he stopped and asked me — he is twenty, he asks things his father had stopped asking — whether the Office had ever once, in my eleven years, seen a boat.

I have thought about it since. Wrecks, yes; manifests, attestations, rows, and columns. I said: no. The Office has never seen a boat. He nodded, the way you nod at a fair price, and went down the stairs to the exchange, and I turned back to the smaller room and entered his premium in the ledger, in the ninth column, where it belonged, and it did belong. That is my trade. I keep the paper that keeps the port fed, and it has cost what it has cost, and the sea, which is the only thing in this account that never signed anything, goes on not being a class, all night, under everyone.
