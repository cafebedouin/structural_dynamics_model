# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## SECTION 1: THE REVISED NARRATIVE

# THE SEPARATION

## I.

The drill bit screams through ice older than human civilization, and Kira feels it in her teeth. Sixty meters down in Europa's crust, the vibration travels up through the rig, through her suit, through bone. She's been on surface for four months now. Two more until rotation back to Ganymede.

Her radiation counter ticks. Always ticking. 540 rem per deployment, the Consortium says. Safe enough if you don't plan to reproduce. Which you don't. Which you can't. Which the implant in your arm makes sure of.

"Flow rate dropping," Lena says through the comm. "Adjust angle five degrees."

Kira makes the correction. The drill bites deeper. Ice becomes water becomes commodity becomes the reason they're all here, 628 million kilometers from Earth, trapped in a rotation that never ends: six months surface, six months station, twenty years until payout. She's twelve years in.

The ice gives. Water floods the collection system. Lena marks it in the log. Another quota met.

"Good work," Lena says. "Shift change in six hours."

Six hours. Then the transport back to Ganymede, back to the drums, back to 1.2g that'll make her legs ache for the first three days. Back to the Compact.

Back to enforcement.

---

The thing they don't tell you about high gravity is that standing becomes a decision. Kira's been back in Drum Section 7 for eight hours, and her back already remembers why she hates rotation week.

She finds Lena in the common area, staring at the viewport. The hub floats beyond the glass: gardens and open space and 0.3g luxury. You can see it from anywhere in the drums. You can never reach it.

"We need to talk," Kira says.

Lena doesn't turn. "I know."

"You were looking at him during shift change. Marcus saw."

"I was just—"

"Doesn't matter what you were doing. Matters what it looked like." Kira sits. "How long?"

"Three weeks. He asked if we could talk. Just talk. I said no. But I wanted to say yes."

"Who is he?"

"Yuri. From Section 4. I worked with him before the Compact. Before everything." Lena finally turns. Her eyes are red. "It's been three years, Kira."

1,095 days. Forty-seven women. Zero defections.

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because it's working. Three years ago, we had nothing. Now? Tanaka's people are asking questions. Running audits. Looking for ways to 'resolve the operational disruption.' That's us. We're the disruption. And the only reason they're talking is because we've held."

"For how much longer?"

"Until they change the policy." Kira stands. "Yuri's been trapped for the same 1,095 days. I'm not saying it doesn't hurt him. I'm saying it hurts all of us, and we're carrying it together. That's the difference between a trap and a tool. We chose this. We maintain it. We end it when it gets us what we need."

"And if it never does?"

Kira doesn't answer. Because she doesn't know. Because three years is a long time to climb a rope that might not have an end.

"It will," Kira says, and leaves before Lena can see the lie.

---

## II.

Dmitri's hands won't stop shaking.

It started during the shift—just a tremor in his right hand, nothing that affected the work. Ice processing is mostly monitoring: watch the temperature gradients, adjust the flow rates, make sure nothing crystallizes in the wrong pipes. He's been doing it for fourteen years. His body knows the rhythm.

But today his body forgot.

He's in his quarters now, shared space with fifteen others, curtains for privacy. He sits on his bunk and stares at his hands. The tremor's worse.

This is the third year.

His wrist counter reads 47 rem accumulated this rotation. Well below danger threshold. The implant scan came back clean last month. Everything by the numbers. Everything compliant.

Everything trapped.

"You okay?" Sergei asks from the next bunk. Older guy, six months left on his contract. Almost out.

"Fine," Dmitri says.

"You look like shit."

"I'm fine."

Sergei doesn't push. Nobody pushes anymore. There's nothing to say that hasn't been said a thousand times in the past three years. The Compact exists. It holds. The women who formed it won't break it. The men who want it gone can't break it. And the Consortium won't intervene because intervention would mean acknowledging that the whole system is built on control.

The tremor spreads to his left hand.

Dmitri thinks about the first year. The rage. The certainty that it couldn't last, that someone would defect, that the whole thing would collapse under its own weight. He tried negotiating. Went to Irina—one of the forty-seven, someone he'd worked with for years—and asked if they could just talk. She said no. Not unkind, but absolute.

He tried going through official channels. Requested psychological support services. Got a form letter suggesting "meditation and exercise protocols." Requested transfer to a different drum section. Denied—crew cohesion requirements. Requested early contract completion. Denied—no provision for voluntary termination without payout forfeiture.

The second year was despair.

The third year is numbness. Mostly. Except for moments like this, when the Pulse hits and his body remembers what it wants and can't have, and the tremor starts, and he sits on his bunk staring at his hands like they belong to someone else.

Six years left. 2,190 days. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then freedom.

He just has to last.

The tremor doesn't stop.

---

Shift change. Dmitri moves through the transit tube with a hundred other Extractors, heading from drums to industrial modules. The tube is narrow, one-way flow, security scanners every fifty meters. His ID badge opens the checkpoints. It won't open the hub tubes. He's tried.

He processes ice for twelve hours. Temperature gradients. Flow rates. Crystal formation prevention. His hands don't shake while he's working.

During break, he sees her.

Irina. One of the forty-seven. She's in the next module over, checking readouts, making notes. She doesn't look at him. She hasn't looked at him in three years.

He remembers: they used to play chess. Bad chess, neither of them very good, but it passed the time. They used to share rations when supply shipments ran late. They used to talk about Earth—neither of them had ever been, but both their contracts promised citizenship at completion. Moscow for him. Lagos for her. Places that existed in their heads as escape routes from this station, this rotation, this life.

Then the Compact formed. Then she stopped looking at him.

He wants to cross the module gap. He wants to ask: *Is it working? Is the leverage real? Are you getting what you need?* He wants to ask: *Does it hurt you the way it hurts me?*

He doesn't move.

The break ends. Irina leaves without looking at him. Dmitri goes back to the ice.

His hands don't shake until the shift ends.

---

## III.

The first meeting happens in Cargo Bay 3, after a double shift when everyone's too tired to think clearly. Kira doesn't plan it that way. It just happens.

Eighteen women, scattered among equipment crates, sitting because standing costs too much. Someone—Kira doesn't remember who—says: "We should organize."

"Organize what?" someone else asks.

"A refusal. Collective. All of us together."

"Refuse what? Work? They'll just terminate our contracts."

"Not work. The other thing."

Silence. Everyone knows what thing. Nobody wants to say it out loud.

Kira's the one who finally speaks. "The Pulse. We refuse to accommodate it. Any of it. Complete withdrawal."

"That's—" Lena stops. Starts again. "That traps the men."

"Yes," Kira says. "It does."

More silence. The cargo bay hums with ventilation.

"They'll wait us out," Irina says. "The Consortium. They'll just wait until we break."

"Maybe. But what if we don't? What if we hold? Forty-seven of us, all standing in the same place, all pulling the same direction. That's leverage. That's the only power we have."

"It's cruel," someone says.

"Yes," Kira agrees. "It is."

She doesn't say: *The Separation is cruel. The prohibition is cruel. The architecture that sorts us into drums and hubs is cruel. We're just using the same physics they used on us.*

She doesn't have to. Everyone in the cargo bay already knows.

"I'm not doing this," Maya says. She's sitting against the far wall, arms crossed. "I can't. I can't be responsible for that."

"Then don't," Kira says. "Nobody's forcing you."

"But if I don't, and everyone else does—then I'm the defection. I'm the crack."

"You're not a crack," Lena says. "You're just—"

"I'm just what? Too weak? Too soft?" Maya's hands are shaking. "You want to know what I think? I think this is exactly what they want. They trap us, and we trap each other, and everyone's trapped, and they don't have to do anything."

"So what do we do instead?" Kira asks. "Keep asking nicely? Keep filing requests that get denied? Keep hoping someone up in the hub notices we're suffering and decides to care?"

Maya doesn't answer.

"I'm tired," Rina says. Older, fifteen years in. "I'm so fucking tired. I don't know if I have the energy to hold something like this. I don't know if I can enforce it on myself, let alone anyone else."

"Then we hold each other," Kira says. "That's what collective means. We don't each carry it alone. We distribute the weight."

"Until someone drops it," Rina says. "Then what?"

"Then we pick it up. Together."

Rina looks at her. Exhaustion in every line of her face. "You make it sound simple."

"It's not simple. It's just the only thing we haven't tried."

The vote happens without formal procedure. Hands raised. Eighteen women, sitting among equipment crates, too tired to stand.

Twelve hands go up.

Six stay down.

"Majority," someone says.

"Not enough," Kira says. "If six of us don't believe in this, it collapses in a week. We need—not unanimous, but close. We need to know we're all in this together."

"So what do we do?" Lena asks.

"We talk. We figure out what the six need to say yes. Or we figure out that this won't work, and we try something else."

They talk for two more hours. Maya's afraid of being responsible for someone else's suffering. Rina's exhausted before they even start. Three others worry about retaliation. One woman says she doesn't believe it will work, but she'll participate anyway because isolation is worse than participating in something you doubt.

By the end, the vote is different.

Sixteen hands go up.

Two stay down.

"That's enough," Kira says. "Sixteen is enough to start."

The two who voted no leave. They don't argue. They just leave.

Kira watches them go and knows: this is how it starts. Not with everyone believing. With enough people willing to try, and everyone else too exhausted or too isolated to refuse.

Within a week, it's twenty-three women.

Within a month, it's forty-seven.

---

The first enforcement decision comes three weeks in. One of the forty-seven—Maya, the woman who voted no at the first meeting but joined anyway—is seen talking to someone. Just talking. But talking is how it starts.

The meeting happens in the same cargo bay. Forty-seven women, exhausted, sitting among equipment crates.

"She was just talking," someone says.

"Talking is a crack," Kira says. "Cracks spread."

"So what do we do?"

Kira looks around the bay. Forty-seven faces, all waiting for an answer she doesn't want to give.

"Enforcement. Maya loses access to shift coverage networks. Resource-sharing systems. Social support. Complete isolation within her drum section."

"That's—"

"I know what it is. But if we don't enforce, the Compact means nothing. And if the Compact means nothing, we have nothing."

"I'll do it," Irina says. "The enforcement. I'll be the one who tells her."

"We all do it," Kira says. "That's the point. We all carry the weight."

The vote passes: thirty-two for enforcement, fifteen against.

Maya doesn't defect. She holds. The isolation lasts two weeks, then she's reinstated.

Kira volunteers for every enforcement decision after that. She figures: if she's asking others to carry this weight, she should carry it first.

---

Three months into the Compact, Kira stops sleeping well. The exhaustion is cumulative. Physical—the 1.2g, the shifts, the surface rotations. Psychological—the enforcement meetings, the votes that pass by smaller margins.

She lies in her bunk at night and thinks about Dmitri. About Yuri. About all the men trapped by a tool she helped build.

She thinks: *We're using the same physics they used on us.*

She thinks: *That doesn't make it right. It makes it effective.*

She thinks: *How long can we hold?*

The votes start to split. Thirty-two for holding, fifteen against. Then thirty for holding, seventeen against. Then twenty-eight for holding, nineteen against.

The margin shrinks.

Kira watches it shrink and knows: they're running out of time. Either the leverage works soon, or the Compact collapses from internal exhaustion.

She doesn't know which outcome she wants anymore.

## IV.

**CONSORTIUM COMPLIANCE AUDIT REPORT**  
**Ganymede Station, Jupiter Orbit**  
**Reporting Officer: Amira Osei**  
**Date: 2089.08.15**

**SUMMARY:**

The operational disruption designated "Compact" continues to maintain structural cohesion. Current compliance among participating Extractor population: 94% (44 of 47 original members; 3 departed station via contract completion, zero defections to date).

Psychological support service requests from male Extractor population increased 340% over baseline. Director Tanaka has requested operational options for resolution.

**DETAILED FINDINGS:**

1. **Compact Maintenance Mechanisms**

Internal enforcement operates through withdrawal of cooperative support structures. Observed mechanisms include: exclusion from shift coverage networks, removal from resource-sharing systems, social isolation within crew modules. In drum section environment, where survival depends on mutual aid, social exclusion presents genuine threat to operational capacity and personal safety.

Extractors interviewed consistently described enforcement as "necessary" and "collectively maintained." No reports of physical coercion. No requests for intervention.

2. **Impact on Male Extractor Population**

Psychological support utilization patterns indicate sustained distress. Common reported symptoms: sleep disruption, difficulty concentrating, emotional volatility, somatic complaints (tremor, chest tightness, digestive issues). Medical scans show no physiological basis for reported symptoms in 87% of cases.

Requests for contract modification, transfer, or early termination increased 230% over baseline. All requests denied per standard Consortium policy.

Three attempted self-harm incidents this quarter (comparison: zero incidents same quarter previous three years). All individuals received mandatory psychological evaluation and returned to duty.

3. **Coordinator Observation**

Five of six Coordinators interviewed described the disruption as "temporary labor issue" requiring "management intervention." One Coordinator noted: "The architecture works perfectly if you're in the hub. From the drums, I imagine it looks different."

Director Tanaka declined interview for this report cycle.

**OPERATIONAL NOTES:**

The compliance officer has access to both drum and hub sections. Transit between sections requires physical adaptation (0.3g to 1.2g transition produces noticeable physiological response: increased heart rate, muscle strain, balance adjustment). Coordinators who never cross the boundary do not experience this transition.

Drum section common areas maintain direct viewport access to hub section. Visual access to gardens, open spaces, and low-gravity recreational facilities is continuous. Architectural decision rationale: "maintains crew awareness of station totality." Practical effect: daily reminder of inaccessible space.

**CONCLUSION:**

The Compact maintains structural integrity after 1,095 days. Internal cohesion shows no signs of imminent collapse. External pressure on target population continues to escalate. Consortium policy remains unchanged. Operational disruption costs continue to accumulate.

**END REPORT**

---

Amira submits the report and sits back in her office—low gravity, private quarters, air that doesn't taste like metal. She's been on Ganymede for eighteen months. Sixteen more until her rotation ends.

She thinks about the transit tube crossing this morning. The way her body registered the shift from 0.3g to 1.2g. The way her legs remembered what weight felt like.

She thinks about the interview with Dmitri Volkov last week. He sat across from her in the compliance office, hands folded in his lap to hide the shaking, and answered every question with perfect compliance. *Yes, I'm managing the stress. No, I don't require additional support. Yes, I understand the contract terms.*

His radiation counter read 47 rem. His medical scans were clean. His contract compliance was perfect.

His hands wouldn't stop shaking.

She'd asked: "Do you feel the situation is sustainable?"

He'd looked at her—really looked, like he was trying to see if she understood what she was asking. Then he'd said: "For six more years? I don't know. Ask me in six years."

She'd noted it in the file. *Subject demonstrates awareness of temporal constraint. No immediate intervention required.*

What she didn't note: *Subject is trapped by a tool built by people who are also trapped. Subject's suffering serves no purpose except as leverage in a negotiation he has no part in. I am documenting it. Documentation enables it.*

Because that would be analysis. And analysis is outside compliance officer scope.

She thinks about Kira Okonkwo. Different interview, same week. Kira had walked into the compliance office like she was walking into a negotiation. Sat down. Made eye contact. Waited.

Amira had asked the standard questions. Compact cohesion. Enforcement mechanisms. Sustainability assessment.

Kira had answered in Consortium Standard—perfect, precise, exactly what the audit required. Then, at the end, she'd switched. The syntax changed. The register dropped. She'd said: "We're holding. Forty-seven hands on the rope. You want to know if we'll break? We won't. Not before they do."

Amira had noted it in the file. *Subject demonstrates sustained commitment to collective action. High confidence in strategy sustainability.*

What she didn't note: *Subject is exhausted. Subject is climbing a rope that might not have an end. Subject believes the leverage is working, but belief and truth aren't the same thing. I am documenting this belief. My documentation will be used to determine when the Consortium intervenes. I am part of the mechanism.*

Because that would be interpretation. And interpretation is outside compliance officer scope.

Amira looks at the viewport. The drums rotate below—massive cylindrical sections, cramped quarters, 1.2g constant load. She can see them from here. She can reach them with her ID badge. She crosses the boundary and her body registers the difference, and then she crosses back and the difference disappears.

She's been crossing that boundary seventeen times per quarter for eighteen months. Every crossing, her body tells her: *This is different. This costs something. You feel it for thirty seconds and then you're back in 0.3g and the feeling stops.*

She lives in the position where the feeling stops.

She writes reports about the position where it doesn't.

She doesn't put that in the report.

---

Director Tanaka reads the compliance report in her office—executive section, lowest gravity on the station, floor-to-ceiling viewports showing Jupiter's banded face. She's been with the Consortium for twenty-two years. She didn't design the Separation—it was in place when she arrived—but she's maintained it.

The Compact has been disrupting that efficiency for three years.

She reads Amira's data. 94% cohesion. 340% increase in psychological support requests. Three self-harm incidents. Zero defections.

The numbers tell her: this is not collapsing on its own.

She opens the policy file. Reproduction prohibition, established 2067, rationale: radiation exposure from Europa surface operations creates genetic damage risk. The medical data supports this. 540 rem per deployment exceeds safe conception thresholds.

But.

Coordinators never deploy to Europa surface. Coordinators face negligible radiation exposure. Coordinators are also subject to reproduction prohibition.

Tanaka knows why. Labor control. If Extractors can't reproduce, they can't form family units, can't develop long-term station attachments, can't build the kind of social infrastructure that would make them harder to rotate out. The prohibition keeps the labor force mobile, replaceable, compliant.

The radiation rationale is real. It's also convenient.

Tanaka has never questioned this logic. She's a Coordinator. She lives in the hub. The Separation works from her position. The drums are visible through her viewport, but she hasn't crossed down in eight years. There's no reason to. The architecture sorts people efficiently.

The Compact forced her to notice the sorting.

Not because it changed the architecture—the Separation remains intact. But because it revealed the cost. Three years of operational disruption. Three self-harm incidents. Three hundred and forty percent increase in psychological support requests.

She opens a comm channel to Legal. "I need policy modification options for the reproduction prohibition. Specifically: pathways to conception approval for Extractor population, contingent on radiation exposure review and medical clearance."

Legal responds within an hour. The pathway exists—it's the same one Coordinators use. It just requires extending access.

Tanaka reads the proposal. It's elegant. It maintains the prohibition's structure while appearing to remove it. Extractors can apply. The application requires medical review. The review will document radiation exposure. The exposure will exceed safe thresholds in most cases. Denial will be medically justified.

The prohibition becomes bureaucratic rather than absolute. The control remains. The appearance changes.

She opens another channel, this one to Kira Okonkwo's work assignment. "Compliance audit follow-up. Report to Director's office at 1800 hours."

She sits back. Jupiter rotates beyond the glass. The drums rotate below.

She's about to offer Kira Okonkwo equal access to approval she won't receive.

She thinks this resolves the disruption.

---

Kira arrives at 1800 hours exactly. She's been in the drums for three weeks since surface rotation. Her legs have stopped aching. Her back hasn't.

Tanaka's office is everything the drums aren't: space, light, air that doesn't taste recycled. Kira stands in 0.3g and feels her body remember what floating feels like.

"Sit," Tanaka says.

Kira sits. The chair barely registers her weight.

"The Compact. Three years. No signs of collapse. Impressive organizational coherence."

Kira waits.

"I'm offering policy modification. Extractor population gains access to conception approval pathway, contingent on radiation exposure review and medical clearance. Same process Coordinators use. Equal access."

Kira's heart rate spikes. She keeps her face neutral. "In exchange for?"

"Compact dissolution. Immediate."

"And if we refuse?"

"The operational disruption continues. The costs accumulate. Eventually, the Consortium intervenes with less favorable terms." Tanaka leans forward. "This is the best offer you'll get. Take it now, or wait until the leverage runs out."

Kira thinks about 1,095 days. Forty-seven women. Zero defections. She thinks about Lena, wavering. She thinks about the enforcement meetings, the votes to hold passing by smaller margins.

She thinks about Dmitri's hands shaking. About Yuri, who Lena looks at during shift changes.

"Equal access to what?" Kira asks. "To an approval process that will still deny us based on radiation exposure we accumulated doing the work you required?"

Tanaka doesn't flinch. "The radiation exposure is real. The medical risk is documented. The approval pathway accounts for individual variation. Some applications will be approved."

"How many?"

"That depends on individual exposure levels."

"How many?" Kira repeats.

Tanaka is quiet for a moment. Then: "Approximately fifteen percent, based on current exposure data."

Fifteen percent. Seven women out of forty-seven.

"I need to bring this to the group. We vote collectively."

"You have forty-eight hours."

Kira stands. The 0.3g makes her feel like she's floating away. She turns to leave.

"Okonkwo," Tanaka says.

Kira stops.

"The Separation remains. The architecture remains. You understand that?"

"Yes."

"Then you understand this resolves the disruption. Not the structure."

Kira looks at her. Director Tanaka, who lives in the hub, who moves through a station where every door opens because her badge makes them open, who hasn't crossed down to the drums in eight years.

"Yes," Kira says. "I understand."

She leaves. The transit tube takes her back to the drums. Her body registers the shift: 0.3g to 1.2g, floating to weight, ease to effort. By the time she reaches Drum Section 7, her legs are already tired.

She has forty-eight hours to convince forty-six women that three years of climbing was worth this: equal access to approval most of them won't receive.

## V.

The meeting happens in Cargo Bay 3, off-shift, forty-seven women in a space designed for equipment storage. Kira stands in the center. Her legs ache. Her back aches. She's been standing for twenty minutes, explaining Tanaka's offer, and the 1.2g makes standing a decision she has to keep making.

"Equal access," she says. "Same approval pathway Coordinators use. Radiation review, medical clearance, standard processing. We apply, they evaluate, they approve or deny based on documented criteria."

"Fifteen percent approval rate," Irina says. "That's seven of us. What about the other forty?"

"Denied based on radiation exposure. Medical justification. Documented."

"So we dissolve the Compact, and forty of us still get nothing."

"We get access to the process. We get documentation of why we're denied."

"I don't care about documentation," Rina says. She's sitting against the wall, same spot as the first meeting three years ago. "I'm tired. I'm so fucking tired. I don't care if it worked or if it didn't. I can't hold anymore."

Silence. The cargo bay hums.

"I can't either," someone else says. "I thought I could. I voted to hold last month. But I can't. My body—I can't."

"Then we accept?" Lena asks.

"I'm not saying that," Rina says. "I'm saying I'm out of strength. Whether we accept or refuse, I'm out. You want to keep holding, you hold without me."

Kira looks at her. Rina, who's been in for fifteen years. Who voted yes at the first meeting. Who enforced compliance on Maya. Who held for 1,095 days.

"Anyone else?" Kira asks.

Four more hands go up.

"So that's five who can't hold. That's not failure. That's bodies giving out. Anyone want to argue they should keep going?"

No one argues.

"What about the men?" someone asks. "We've been—we trapped them. For three years. And now we just—what? Say sorry and take the deal?"

"I don't know what we say. I know we can't hold anymore. Five people just said so. How many more are close?"

Twelve hands go up.

Seventeen out of forty-seven. More than a third.

"The Compact held because we held it together," Kira says. "If seventeen of us are at the breaking point, we're not holding anymore. We're just pretending. And pretending costs more than accepting."

"What about the fifteen percent?" Irina asks. "Seven of us get approved. Forty get denied. Is that victory?"

"No," Kira says. "It's accommodation. It's what we could get. It's not what we organized for, but it's more than we had."

"Is it?" Maya asks. She's been quiet this whole meeting. Now she speaks. "We organized to force them to change the prohibition. They're not changing it. They're just making it bureaucratic. We're trading an absolute no for a documented no. How is that more?"

"Because documentation matters. Because every denial has to be explained. Because the radiation exposure we accumulated becomes visible. Because—"

"Because we're exhausted," Rina interrupts. "That's the real reason. We're accepting because we can't hold anymore. Not because the deal is good. Because we're out of strength."

Kira doesn't argue. Because Rina's right.

"Vote," someone says.

They vote. Hands raised. Forty-seven women, same as always.

Thirty-one hands go up for dissolution.

Sixteen stay down.

The sixteen who vote no aren't wrong. They're the ones who can still hold. They're watching the collective fracture because other bodies gave out first.

"I'm sorry," Rina says to the sixteen. "I know you can keep going. I can't."

One of the sixteen—Irina—says: "You don't have to apologize. We held as long as we could. That's all anyone can do."

The Compact ends.

Not because the leverage failed. Not because the strategy was wrong. Because bodies gave out. Because exhaustion is a force that acts on people regardless of principle, regardless of collective commitment, regardless of how much they believe in what they're holding.

Kira watches the vote and knows: this is what collective action looks like. Not the formation—that was easier than anyone expected. Not the maintenance—that was harder. This. The dissolution. Bodies giving out at different rates. Some people still able to hold, watching others let go, understanding it's not betrayal. Just exhaustion.

She thinks about Dmitri. About Yuri. About all the men who've been trapped for 1,095 days.

She thinks: *We're letting them go because we have to. Not because it's right. Because we can't hold anymore.*

She doesn't say it out loud.

---

Dmitri hears about it during shift change. The news travels through the drums faster than official channels. Someone tells someone who tells someone, and within an hour, everyone knows.

The Compact is dissolving. The policy is changing. Equal access to conception approval.

His hands stop shaking.

It takes him a moment to notice. He's in the transit tube, heading to the industrial modules, and his hands are steady. The tremor that's been with him for months—gone. Just like that.

He doesn't feel relief. He doesn't feel anything. Just absence. The trap opened, and the first thing he notices is numbness.

The shift is normal. Temperature gradients, flow rates, crystal formation prevention. He processes ice for twelve hours. During break, he sees Irina in the next module over.

She looks at him.

For three years, she hasn't looked at him. Now she does. It's not an apology. It's not reconciliation. It's just acknowledgment. *You're here. I'm here. We both survived.*

He nods. She nods back. Then they both return to work.

After shift, he goes back to his quarters. Sits on his bunk. Looks at his hands. Steady.

He thinks about 1,095 days. About the Pulse that ambushed him, about the trap that tightened, about the numbness that became his default state.

He thinks about the new policy. Equal access. Approval pathway. Radiation review.

He thinks: *I can apply. They'll evaluate. They'll probably deny—the radiation exposure is real. But I can apply.*

He thinks about Irina. About the forty-seven women who organized because they had no other option.

He thinks: *They weren't trying to trap me. They were trying to escape.*

He thinks: *Both things are true. Understanding why doesn't erase what it cost.*

His hands stay steady, but the steadiness feels wrong. Like his body doesn't know what to do without the tremor. Like the trap was the only thing holding him together, and now that it's gone, he's just—empty.

He thinks about six more years. 2,190 days. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then Moscow.

He thinks: *I just have to last.*

But he doesn't know what he's lasting for anymore. The trap opened, but the Separation remains. The architecture remains. He's still in the drums. The hub is still inaccessible. The 1.2g still makes standing a decision.

He lies back on his bunk. Listens to the station rotate. Closes his eyes.

His hands don't shake.

He doesn't feel relief.

## VI.

Kira applies for conception approval on the third day after the Compact dissolves. The process requires: medical documentation, radiation exposure history, psychological evaluation, and a formal written statement explaining the request.

She compiles the documentation over two weeks. The medical scans take three days. The genetic screening takes another week. The radiation exposure history is already in her file—540 rem per deployment, accumulated dose well above standard safety margins.

The psychological evaluation happens in Amira's office. Same questions as always: *Are you managing the stress? Do you require additional support? Do you understand the contract terms?*

Kira answers in Consortium Standard. Perfect. Precise. Exactly what the audit requires.

The written statement is harder. The form asks: *Why are you requesting conception approval at this time?*

Kira stares at the blank field. She thinks about writing: *Because I want to.* But that's not what the form is asking.

She writes: *I am requesting conception approval to exercise reproductive autonomy consistent with Consortium policy modifications implemented 2089.09.01. I understand that approval is contingent on medical review and radiation exposure assessment.*

She submits the application. The processing time is fourteen days.

She waits.

---

*Your application for conception approval has been reviewed. Based on documented radiation exposure levels (cumulative dose: 6,480 rem over twelve-year deployment period), approval cannot be granted. Radiation exposure exceeds safe conception thresholds by factor of 3.2. Genetic damage risk: 87% probability. Approval denied per medical protocol.*

Kira reads it twice. Then she closes the comm and sits in her quarters—shared space, curtain for privacy, acoustic only—and stares at the wall.

Six thousand four hundred and eighty rem. That's what twelve years of processing ice on Europa costs. That's what her body carries.

The denial is medically justified. The risk is real. They're not wrong.

They required the work that caused the exposure. They're denying her based on damage they caused. Both things are true.

She doesn't cry. She's too tired to cry. She just sits and feels the weight of 1.2g pressing down, feels the ache in her legs and back, feels the accumulated cost of twelve years in the drums.

Eight more years until contract completion. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then Lagos.

Then maybe—eight to twelve years after that—radiation levels drop enough to reapply.

She'll be fifty-two years old.

The seven approved applications share a pattern: administrative roles, processing work, minimal surface deployment. Low radiation exposure. Bodies that weren't damaged by the work the Consortium required.

The forty denied applications share a different pattern: surface deployment, ice extraction, twelve years in the drums. High radiation exposure. Bodies that carry the cost.

Kira sees Maya three weeks after the approvals come through. Maya's in the common area, sitting alone, staring at her hands.

"Congratulations," Kira says.

Maya looks up. Her eyes are red. "Don't."

"You got approved. That's—"

"I know what I got." Maya's voice is sharp. "I got approved because I never went to the surface. Because I processed paperwork instead of ice. Because my body wasn't damaged enough to deny."

Kira sits. The chair takes her weight.

"I'm the exception that proves the rule," Maya says. "They approved seven of us. Just enough to say the system works. Just enough to justify denying the other forty."

"That's not your fault."

"Isn't it?" Maya looks at her. "I applied knowing most of you would be denied. I applied anyway. I wanted it. I still want it. And I hate that wanting it makes me—" She stops. Starts again. "I hate that I'm grateful. I hate that I got what I wanted and it feels like proof that the system is fair when I know it isn't. I hate that I'm relieved and guilty at the same time and I can't untangle which feeling is real."

Kira doesn't know what to say. Because Maya's right. Because her approval is both genuine—she qualified, she met the criteria—and structural proof that the sorting works. Because both things are true.

Maya starts crying. Not loud—just quiet tears, the kind that come from exhaustion rather than grief.

Kira sits with her. They don't talk. They just sit in the common area, in 1.2g, in the drums, while the station rotates and the hub floats beyond the viewport, inaccessible and visible and perfect from a distance.
