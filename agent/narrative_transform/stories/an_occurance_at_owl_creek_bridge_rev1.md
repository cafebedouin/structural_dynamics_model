# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## REVISED NARRATIVE

# The Weight You Carry

The drill bit screams against stone and my shoulders lock. Twelve hours down here and the lithium veins glow like diseased teeth in the rock face. I lean into the machine's kick, let the vibration travel up my arms into my chest where it sits next to everything else I carry.

"Miguel, your cart's full."

Dante's voice behind me. I kill the drill and the silence rushes in, thick with dust and the smell of overheated metal. My hands won't stop shaking. They never do anymore, even in sleep. Rosa mentioned it last week on the video call—said I was twitching, asked if I was okay. I told her I was fine. The house looks good, I said. The walls are really coming together.

"You good?" Dante asks.

"Yeah. Just thinking about home."

He helps me haul the cart to the sorting station. We don't talk much down here—the air's too thin for conversation. Every word costs breath you need for the next lift, the next drill, the next twelve hours.

At the station, I watch the lithium chunks tumble onto the conveyor. Each one is money. Each one is my brother's tuition, my mother's medicine, the tin roof that doesn't leak when the monsoons come. I chose this. That's what I tell myself when my lungs burn and my hands shake and the rock dust coats my throat.

"Shift change in twenty," Dante says. "You want to grab food?"

"Sure."

We ride the lift up together. The hab-modules come into view—concentric rings of prefab boxes stacked three high. Ours are in the outer ring, 7-G-33, where the walls are thin enough to hear Dante's breathing through the partition at night. The middle ring looks better from here—larger modules, actual windows. The central core gleams in the distance. I've never been inside.

In the mess hall, we eat rice and reconstituted vegetables that taste like the packet they came in. Dante pushes his food around.

"You hear about the new quotas?" he asks.

"No. What about them?"

"Up fifteen percent next quarter. Same wages."

I keep eating. The rice is gummy but I'm too tired to care. "We'll make it work."

"That's what you always say."

"Because it's true. We're almost done, Dante. Eight more months and we're out."

He doesn't smile. "You ever think about what happens if we don't?"

"Don't what?"

"Finish. What if something happens? What if we get hurt, or sick, or—"

"Then we get medical care. It's in the contract."

"Miguel, the contract says a lot of things."

I put down my spoon. "What are you saying?"

"Nothing. Forget it." He stands, dumps his tray. "I'm going to sleep. See you at shift."

I watch him go. Dante's been weird lately—asking questions, talking to other workers during breaks instead of resting. I don't like it. Questions don't help. Work does. Finishing does.

Back in the hab, I strip off my work clothes and stand in the communal shower. The water is rationed—three minutes, lukewarm. I watch the rock dust swirl down the drain and think about the shower in the new house. Rosa sent pictures last week. Real tile, she said. Blue tile, like the ocean.

In my bunk, I pull up the video messages on my tablet. Rosa's face fills the screen, pixelated but smiling.

"Miguel, *mahal*, the walls are done! Look—" The camera swings around and I see concrete, solid and real. "Your brother helped with the wiring. He's so good at this, Miguel. The engineering program is perfect for him."

I will make him proud. I am making him proud. Every shift, every cart of lithium, every time I don't ask questions.

"I miss you," Rosa says. "Eight more months feels like forever. But I know you're working hard. I know you're doing this for us. *Mahal kita*."

I love you too, I mouth at the screen. The message is three days old—that's how long signals take from here to Earth.

I record my reply. "Rosa, the house looks amazing. Tell my brother I'm proud of him. Eight more months and I'll be home. We'll fill that house with noise and life and everything good. *Mahal kita*."

I send it and lie back on my bunk. Through the thin wall, I hear Dante's breathing, slow and even. My hands are still shaking but I fold them on my chest and close my eyes.

Tomorrow is another shift. Another twelve hours in the shaft. Another cart of lithium that becomes another brick in the wall, another month of tuition, another dose of medicine. The weight in my chest—the exhaustion, the debt, the *utang na loob*—it's heavy, but it's also purpose. I can carry it. I have to carry it.

---

Three weeks later, I'm in the deep shaft when the drill hits something wrong. The bit catches and the machine bucks hard enough to throw me backward. I land on my tailbone and the pain shoots up my spine.

"*Putang ina*," I hiss.

Dante helps me up. "You okay?"

"Yeah. Just surprised me." I check the drill—the bit is bent, useless. "I need to swap this out."

"I'll finish the vein. You go to equipment."

I limp to the equipment station. The supervisor on duty is a woman I've seen before but never spoken to. She's middle-aged, Filipino, with a badge that says REYES, L. - OPERATIONS SUPERVISOR.

"Drill bit bent," I tell her in English. "I need a replacement."

She looks at the mangled bit, then at me. "*Ano'ng nangyari?*" she asks.

The switch to Filipino surprises me. Supervisors usually stick to English—it's protocol.

"Hit something in the rock. Kicked back hard."

"You hurt?"

"Just my back. I'll be fine."

She pulls a new bit from storage and hands it over, but she doesn't let go immediately when I reach for it. Her eyes are on mine, searching.

"You're Miguel Santos, right? Hab 7-G-33?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"How long have you been here?"

"Almost thirty-two months. Eight more to go."

She nods slowly. "That's a long time in the shaft. You must be tired."

"I'm fine."

"I'm sure you are." She releases the bit. "Be careful down there. These quotas—they push people too hard. But you didn't hear that from me."

I take the bit and head back to the shaft. The conversation sits strange in my chest. Supervisors don't acknowledge quota pressure. They enforce it.

But I don't have time to think about it. The drill is waiting.

---

Two days later, Supervisor Reyes finds me at the water dispenser during break.

"Miguel," she says in Filipino. "How's your back?"

"Better, ma'am. Thank you."

"Good. You know—" She fills her own canteen, casual. "I started in the outer ring too. Hab 7-G-41, twenty years ago. Same thin walls, same twelve-hour shifts. I know what it's like."

I don't know what to say to that.

"You made it out," I say finally.

"I did. Finished my term, got promoted, worked my way up. It's possible, Miguel. The company rewards hard work if you work with it." She caps her canteen. "But it's hard. Especially with the quotas changing all the time. Especially when you're carrying so much."

The way she says it—*carrying so much*—makes my chest tight.

"I manage," I say.

"I'm sure you do. But if you ever need to talk—about work, about conditions, about anything—my module is 4-C-17. Middle ring. You can find me there."

She walks away before I can respond. I stand there with my canteen, watching her disappear into the crowd of workers.

That night, I tell Dante about it.

"She wants you to come talk to her?" he asks through the partition wall.

"I guess. I don't know why."

"Maybe she's recruiting."

"For what?"

Silence. Then: "You really don't know what's happening, do you?"

"What are you talking about?"

More silence. Then his breathing changes, gets slower. He's going to sleep without answering.

---

The quotas go up again four days later. Another twelve percent. No explanation, just a notice posted at the equipment station: PRODUCTIVITY TARGETS ADJUSTED PER OPERATIONAL REQUIREMENTS.

In the mess hall, the workers are quiet. Not angry—angry costs energy. Just quiet, eating their rice, calculating in their heads how many more hours, how many more carts, how many more months.

Dante sits across from me and doesn't eat.

"You okay?" I ask.

"No."

"Dante—"

"They keep squeezing us, Miguel. Every quarter, every month. And we just take it because what else can we do?"

"Seven and a half months," I say. "That's all. Then we're done."

"Are we? Or do the quotas go up again next month? And the month after?"

"The contract has an end date. They can't change that."

"They can't change a lot of things. But they do."

I want to argue but I'm too tired. The rice is gummy. My back still hurts.

"What do you want me to say?" I ask finally.

"Nothing. I'm just—" He pushes his tray away. "Forget it."

But he doesn't go to sleep. That night, through the partition wall, I hear him moving around. Hear the tablet chime. Hear his voice, low and careful, speaking to someone.

I don't ask who.

---

A week later, Supervisor Reyes finds me again. This time at the end of shift, when I'm walking back to the hab. She falls into step beside me.

"Miguel. How are you holding up?"

"Fine, ma'am."

"The new quotas must be hard."

"We manage."

"I'm sure you do. But managing isn't the same as thriving, is it?" She glances at me. "You know, there are people here who remember what it's like in the outer ring. People who want things to be better. Not just for themselves—for everyone."

My chest tightens. "Ma'am, I just want to finish my term."

"Of course. And you will. But you could finish with things improved for the people who come after you. Wouldn't that be worth something?"

We've reached the outer ring. The corridor narrows, the air gets thicker. She stops walking.

"Think about it," she says. "If you ever want to talk—really talk—you know where to find me."

She leaves. I stand there in the narrow corridor, workers pushing past me. Someone bumps my shoulder. Someone else coughs, wet and thick.

I think about seven and a half months. I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about the weight in my chest and how it's getting heavier.

I think about what Dante said: *What if we never finish because the target keeps moving?*

That night, I can't sleep. Through the partition wall, Dante's breathing is steady, but I know he's awake. I can tell by the rhythm.

"Dante," I say quietly.

"Yeah?"

"What did you mean about recruiting?"

Long silence. Then: "Come over here."

I get up, pull on my work pants, step into the corridor. His curtain is open. He's sitting on his bunk, tablet in his lap, face lit by the screen.

"Close the curtain," he says.

I do. The space is tiny—barely room for both of us. He turns the tablet so I can see. It's a message thread, encrypted, names I don't recognize.

"There are people organizing," he says quietly. "Not a union—that's prohibited. But a network. Workers talking to each other, sharing information, trying to push back against the quota changes."

"Dante, that's dangerous."

"What's dangerous is doing nothing."

"The contract—"

"The contract says a lot of things, Miguel. But it doesn't say we can't talk to each other. It doesn't say we can't share concerns through proper channels. That's all we're doing."

I look at the message thread. The names are coded—Worker 7-G-33-A, Worker 5-F-22-B. But the messages are clear: quota increases, shift extensions, equipment failures. Pattern documentation. Evidence.

"Who's running this?" I ask.

"People who care. People who remember what it's like." He closes the tablet. "Supervisor Reyes is one of them."

My stomach drops. "What?"

"She's been helping us. Connecting workers across sectors, providing information about company policies, showing us how to work within the framework to make things better. She's on our side, Miguel."

"She's a supervisor."

"She's someone who finished her term and moved up and remembers what it cost. She wants to help."

I stand up. The space is too small. I need air.

"I have to go."

"Miguel—"

"I'll see you at shift."

I push through the curtain, back to my hab, back to my bunk. I lie there in the dark and think about Supervisor Reyes saying *the company rewards hard work if you work with it*. I think about Dante's encrypted messages. I think about seven and a half months and whether I'll actually make it.

The weight in my chest shifts. Not lighter—just different.

---

I avoid Supervisor Reyes for three weeks. When I need equipment, I wait until someone else is on duty. When I see her in the corridors, I take a different route. But the quotas keep going up—another eight percent, then another ten—and the weight keeps getting heavier.

Finally, I go to Module 4-C-17.

The middle ring is different. Wider corridors, cleaner air. The modules have actual doors. I stand outside 4-C-17 for a full minute before I knock.

Supervisor Reyes opens the door. She's not in uniform—just regular clothes. Behind her, I can see a small living space with a real bed, a table, a window.

"Miguel," she says, and her smile is warm. "I'm glad you came. Come in."

I step inside. The floor is solid. There's a coffee maker on the counter—real coffee, not instant.

"Sit," she says, gesturing to the table. "I'll make us something to drink."

I sit. The chair is comfortable. Through the window, I can see the Valles Depression stretching to the horizon, the extraction shafts like small scars in the distance.

She brings two mugs of coffee. The ceramic is warm against my palms. The smell is rich, real.

"I know this is hard for you," she says, sitting across from me. "Coming here. Talking to me. It goes against everything you've told yourself about keeping your head down and finishing your term."

"I just want to understand what's happening."

"What's happening is that workers are starting to see the pattern. The quotas that keep changing. The wages that don't. The equipment that breaks and doesn't get fixed. The medical care that's 'in the contract' but somehow never available when you need it." She sips her coffee. "We're not organizing—that's prohibited. But we're talking. Sharing information. Building awareness."

"And then what?"

"And then we present concerns to management through proper channels. We show them the documentation—the quota changes, the safety violations, the contract terms that aren't being honored. We ask for adjustments. Not demands—requests. All within the framework."

"You think they'll listen?"

"I think they'll have to. If enough workers present the same concerns, if we have documentation, if we go through proper channels—they can't ignore that."

I drink the coffee. It's good. Real. The warmth spreads through my chest.

"Why are you helping us?" I ask.

"Because I remember, Miguel. I remember the outer ring. I remember thinking I had no power, no voice. And then I finished my term and I moved up and I realized—there are ways to work within the framework. There are ways to make things better without breaking the rules." She leans forward. "I want to help you. I want to help all of you. But I need to know who's with us. Who understands what we're trying to do."

The question hangs in the air. Through the window, the extraction shafts look smaller from here.

"Dante's with you," I say.

"He is. He's been very helpful—connecting us with workers in other sectors, sharing information. He's smart, Miguel. He sees what's happening."

"What do you need from me?"

"Names. Other workers in your sector who are frustrated, who see the pattern. People you trust. People who would understand what we're doing if they knew it was safe."

I think about the workers in my sector. Carlos, who's been here four years and still has two to go. Jin, who sends every peso home to his parents. Amara, who talks about her daughter's school fees during breaks.

I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about seven and a half months. I think about the weight in my chest and how it's been getting heavier every day.

"I need time to think," I say.

"Of course. But Miguel—" She reaches across the table. Her hand is warm on mine. "I know you're scared. I know you think this could jeopardize everything you've worked for. But I promise you, this is safe. This is smart. This is how you finish your term with your dignity intact."

I finish the coffee. I stand up.

"Thank you for the coffee."

"Anytime. You know where to find me."

---

I don't give her names right away. I spend three days thinking about it, watching Carlos and Jin and Amara during shifts, during breaks, during the times when they talk about home and family and how many months they have left.

Carlos is angry. Not loud—quiet anger, the kind that sits in your jaw. He's been here four years and the quotas have gone up seven times.

Jin is careful. He counts everything—carts, hours, wages. He has a notebook where he tracks the quota changes, the wage stagnations, the equipment failures.

Amara is tired. Not physically—though she's that too. But tired in a way that goes deeper. She talks about her daughter's school fees the way I talk about Rosa's house.

They're all frustrated. They all see the pattern.

On the fourth day, I go back to Module 4-C-17.

"I have names," I tell her.

She gets out a tablet. "I'm listening."

"Carlos Mendez. Hab 7-G-29. Jin Wei. Hab 7-F-18. Amara Hassan. Hab 7-G-41."

Supervisor Reyes finishes writing and looks up at me. Her smile is warm, genuine.

"Thank you, Miguel. You're helping build something important. Something that could actually change things for everyone."

"What happens now?"

"Now I reach out to them. Carefully, through proper channels. I'll let them know there are people who care, people who want to help." She closes the tablet. "We're going to start distributing people—moving workers around so the network is spread across sectors. That way we can share information more efficiently. You might see some of your sector mates reassigned. Don't worry—it's strategic."

"Okay."

"And Miguel—" She stands, walks me to the door. "You've demonstrated real judgment today. The kind we need more of. I'm going to recommend you for advancement. Supervisor trainee program. You've earned it."

The words hit strange. Advancement. Supervisor trainee. That's middle ring. That's solid floors and real coffee and windows.

"I just want to finish my term," I say.

"And you will. But you'll finish with something more than just your contract fulfilled. You'll finish knowing you helped make things better." She opens the door. "Think about it. The recommendation will take a few weeks to process. In the meantime, keep doing what you're doing. Keep your eyes open."

I walk back to the outer ring with my hands in my pockets. The corridors feel narrower than before. The air feels thicker.

---

The reassignments happen within a week.

Carlos moves to a different sector—early shift instead of late. I see him in the corridor once and raise my hand to wave, but he doesn't see me.

Jin gets transferred to surface operations. Different work, different hours. I don't see him at all anymore.

Amara moves to a different hab-module, far enough away that our shifts don't overlap. She's just gone.

Dante notices.

"You see what's happening?" he asks through the partition wall one night.

"They're distributing people. Supervisor Reyes said it's strategic."

"Strategic for what?"

"For building the network. So people are spread across sectors."

Silence. Then: "Miguel, when did you talk to Supervisor Reyes?"

"Last week. I gave her names. Carlos, Jin, Amara. She said she'd reach out to them."

More silence. Longer this time.

"What names did you give her?" Dante asks finally.

"I just said. Carlos, Jin, Amara. Why?"

"Miguel—" His voice is different now. Careful. "Those aren't network people. I never mentioned them. How did you know they were frustrated?"

"I didn't. I mean, I could tell. Carlos is angry, Jin tracks everything, Amara's tired. They fit the pattern."

"The pattern of what?"

"Of people who would understand. People who would want things to be better."

Through the wall, I hear Dante moving. Then his curtain pulls back. Then he's in my space, standing over my bunk in the dark.

"Miguel, listen to me. Supervisor Reyes isn't running a network. She's running intelligence. She's identifying workers who might organize and neutralizing them before they can. The reassignments aren't strategic—they're isolating. She's breaking up connections."

"That's not true. She said—"

"What did she say? That she wants to help? That she remembers what it's like? That she's on our side?" His voice is harsh now. "She's a compliance officer, Miguel. That's her job. She identifies threats and she neutralizes them. And you just gave her three more names."

My chest goes cold.

"No. She said we were building something. She said it was safe."

"Nothing is safe. That's the point. That's what she uses—our hope that things could be better." He sits down on my bunk. "I thought you understood. I thought when you came over that night, when I showed you the messages, you saw what was really happening."

"I thought you said she was helping us. You said she was on our side."

"I said she was one of the people organizing. I didn't say she was on our side."

"What's the difference?"

"She's organizing us so they can document us. So they can identify who's frustrated, who's talking, who's a risk. And then they reassign us, isolate us, make sure we can't connect."

I sit up. The space is too small. I need air.

"You're wrong. She gave me coffee. She talked about finishing with dignity. She recommended me for advancement."

"Advancement to what?"

"Supervisor trainee. Middle ring."

Dante laughs. It's not a happy sound.

"Of course she did. That's perfect. They identify someone who's scared, someone who's carrying weight, someone who'll do anything to finish. And then they offer them a way up. All they have to do is give names."

"I was trying to help."

"You were trying to survive. I understand that. But Miguel—the people whose names you gave, they're not going to understand. They're going to think you betrayed them. And they're right."

He stands up. Leaves. I hear his curtain close.

---

The next day, I get a notice. Reassignment to Module 4-C-22. Supervisor trainee position. Immediate transfer.

I pack my things—the tablet with Rosa's messages, my work clothes, the few personal items I've accumulated. Through the partition wall, Dante's breathing is steady. I want to knock, to say something, to explain. But I don't know what to say.

In Module 4-C-22, I have a real bed. A solid floor. A window that shows the Valles Depression. The air doesn't smell like sweat and metal.

I have a badge now: SANTOS, M. - SUPERVISOR TRAINEE.

The training is simple. How to monitor equipment requests. How to document worker concerns. How to identify patterns of behavior that might indicate organizing activity.

"You're looking for clusters," the trainer says. "Workers who talk during breaks instead of resting. Workers who ask questions about contract terms. Workers who seem frustrated with quota changes. Document everything."

I nod. I take notes. I wear my badge.

During my first equipment shift, Carlos comes to the station. He needs a drill bit. He looks at my badge, at my face, and something changes in his expression.

"Drill bit bent," he says in English. Formal. Distant.

"I'll get you one."

I pull the bit from storage and hand it over. He takes it without meeting my eyes and leaves.

The next day, Jin comes for a respirator filter. He doesn't speak at all. Just shows me the broken filter, takes the new one, walks away.

Amara doesn't come to my station. I see her once in the corridor, far away, and she turns and goes a different direction.

At night, I lie in my real bed with my solid floor and my window view. The silence is complete. No breathing through walls. No Dante moving around. Just me and the weight in my chest.

I send messages to Rosa but I don't tell her about the promotion. I don't tell her about the names I gave, about Carlos and Jin and Amara, about the way they won't look at me anymore.

I tell her the house looks beautiful. I tell her I'm proud of my brother. I tell her seven more months and I'll be home.

---

Six weeks after the promotion, Supervisor Reyes calls me to her module.

"Miguel," she says when I arrive. "Come in. Sit."

I sit. She doesn't offer coffee this time.

"I want to talk to you about your performance," she says. "You've been doing well in the trainee program. Documenting concerns, identifying patterns. You're good at this."

"Thank you, ma'am."

"But I've noticed something. You're not reporting as many concerns as I expected. Given your position in the outer ring, your connections with other workers, I thought you'd have more to share."

My chest tightens. "I'm reporting what I see."

"I'm sure you are. But I wonder if you're seeing everything. Or if you're still holding back, still trying to protect people." She leans forward. "Miguel, I need you to understand something. The workers you're protecting—they don't see you as one of them anymore. You're in the middle ring now. You have a badge. You have a future. They see you as part of management."

"I know."

"Do you? Because your reports suggest otherwise. You're documenting surface concerns—equipment requests, shift complaints. But you're not documenting the deeper patterns. The conversations during breaks. The questions about contract terms."

"I haven't seen organizing."

"Then you're not looking hard enough." She opens her tablet. "Let me show you something."

She turns the screen toward me. It's a message thread—encrypted, coded names. Worker 7-G-33-A. Worker 7-F-22-B. The same thread Dante showed me months ago.

"This is an active organizing network," she says. "It's been operating for six months. We've been monitoring it, documenting participants, building a case. And you know who Worker 7-G-33-A is?"

My stomach drops.

"Dante," I say quietly.

"Dante Cruz. Your bunkmate for thirty-two months. Your friend. The person who recruited you into this network." She closes the tablet. "Miguel, you participated in organizing activity. You attended meetings. You discussed forbidden topics. You're in these messages."

The room feels smaller. The air feels thinner.

"I didn't know what it was. I thought—"

"What you thought doesn't matter. What matters is what you did. And what you did is participate in prohibited organizing activity under Section 7.3 of your contract."

"I gave you names," I say. "I helped you. I did everything you asked."

"You did. And that's why we're having this conversation instead of a different one. You demonstrated cooperation. You showed willingness to work within the framework. That counts for something." She pauses. "But it doesn't erase your participation. The messages are documented. The meetings are recorded. You're in violation of contract terms."

"What happens now?"

"That depends on you. You can continue cooperating—fully cooperating, not holding back. You can help us identify other participants, document the network's full extent, build a complete case. If you do that, we can recommend leniency. A warning instead of termination. Probation instead of immediate departure."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you're in breach of contract. Termination without wages. Return transport to Earth. Family liable for signing bonus repayment plus interest." She looks at me steadily. "Forty-three thousand dollars, Miguel. Plus interest. How long would it take your family to repay that? How many years would your brother have to leave school? How long before they lose the house?"

The weight in my chest is water now, cold and rising. I can feel it in my throat.

"What do you need?" I ask.

"Names. Everyone in the network. Everyone Dante recruited, everyone you talked to, everyone who participated in any way. Full documentation. Complete cooperation."

I think about Dante's breathing through the partition wall. I think about his encrypted messages, his careful voice, the way he tried to warn me.

I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about my brother in his engineering program. I think about forty-three thousand dollars plus interest.

"I'll need time," I say.

"You have twenty-four hours. After that, we proceed with termination." She stands. "I know this is hard, Miguel. But you're doing the right thing. You're protecting your family. That's what matters."

---

I give her everything.

Dante Cruz, primary organizer. Worker 7-G-33-A.

Marcus Webb, recruiter for the surface operations sector. Worker 5-F-22-B.

Sarah Kim, documentation specialist. Worker 6-H-14-C.

The message threads, the encrypted channels, the meeting times. The notebook Jin kept, the patterns Carlos tracked, the conversations Amara had during breaks.

I give her everything because forty-three thousand dollars plus interest is more than my family can pay. Because the house has blue tile and my brother is in school and Rosa is waiting for me to come home.

When I'm done, I send the file to Supervisor Reyes and I lie down on my real bed with my solid floor and my window view, and I wait.

---

The arrests happen within forty-eight hours.

I don't see them. I'm in my module when it happens, monitoring equipment requests, documenting worker concerns. But I hear about it later—how security came to the outer ring at shift change, how they pulled Dante and Marcus and Sarah and six others from their habs, how they were escorted to the central core for processing.

I hear about it from another supervisor trainee, casual, over coffee in the middle ring break room.

"Big organizing bust in the outer ring," he says. "Compliance finally caught them. Been building the case for months apparently."

"What happens to them?" I ask.

"Standard procedure. Tribunal, termination, return transport. Family liable for contract repayment."

"How much?"

"Depends on how long they've been here. Some of them have been here four, five years. That's a lot of signing bonus to repay. Plus interest." He shrugs. "Shouldn't have organized. Contract's clear about that."

I drink my coffee. The ceramic is warm against my palms.

"Yeah," I say. "Contract's clear."

---

Three days later, I'm called to the central core.

I've never been inside before. The corridors are carpeted—actual carpet, soft under my boots. The air is cool, climate-controlled. There are plants in pots, real plants with green leaves.

A security officer escorts me to a conference room. The door is solid wood, not metal. Inside, there's a long table with comfortable chairs. At the head of the table sits a man in a clean uniform, his hands folded on a tablet.

"Miguel Santos?" he asks in English.

"Yes, sir."

"I'm Captain Torres, Contract Compliance Division. Please sit."

I sit. The chair is comfortable. My boots leave dust prints on the carpet and I try to tuck them under the chair, out of sight.

Captain Torres looks at his tablet. "You've been with TPMC for thirty-three months. Seven months remaining on your contract."

"Yes, sir."

"And you were recently promoted to supervisor trainee after demonstrating cooperation with a compliance investigation."

"Yes, sir."

He looks up at me. "Mr. Santos, we've completed our review of the organizing network you helped us document. Nine workers have been processed for contract violations under Section 7.3. They've been terminated and will be returning to Earth on the next supply transport."

"Yes, sir."

"Your cooperation was valuable. You provided names, documentation, message threads. You helped us build a complete case." He pauses. "However, our review also identified your own participation in organizing activity. You attended meetings. You discussed forbidden topics. You're documented in the message threads."

My stomach drops. My hands go cold.

"Sir, I cooperated. I gave you everything. Supervisor Reyes said—"

"Supervisor Reyes's recommendations are noted. But the contract is clear. Section 7.3 prohibits organizing activity. Participation is participation, regardless of subsequent cooperation." He taps his tablet. "The tribunal has reviewed your case. The hearing will begin now."

"Hearing? Sir, I don't understand—"

Two more people enter the room. Another security officer. A woman with a supervisor badge who won't meet my eyes. They sit at the table.

Captain Torres reads from his tablet. "Miguel Santos, you are charged with willful breach of contract terms under Section 7.3: Prohibited Organizing Activity. The evidence includes recorded conversations, documented meetings, and message thread participation. You have the right to speak on your own behalf. Do you have anything to say?"

My mouth is dry. "I cooperated. I gave names. I helped build the case. Supervisor Reyes said I'd get leniency."

"Leniency was considered. However, the evidence of your participation is clear. The contract waives claims of coercion or misunderstanding."

"But she told me to participate. She told me to give names. She said it was safe."

"Supervisor Reyes's role was to identify and document organizing activity. Your participation in that activity, regardless of her instructions, constitutes a contract violation." He looks at me steadily. "Mr. Santos, do you deny participating in organizing discussions?"

"I participated because she told me to."

"Do you deny attending meetings with other workers to discuss forbidden topics?"

"She said it was safe. She said we were working within the framework."

"Do you deny providing names of other workers for organizing purposes?"

The names. Carlos. Jin. Amara. Dante. Marcus. Sarah.

"I gave names because she told me to. Because I was cooperating."

"Then the facts are established." Captain Torres looks at the other two. They nod. He looks back at me. "The tribunal finds you guilty of willful breach of contract terms, Section 7.3. The sentence is immediate: termination without wages, return transport to Earth on the next supply ship, family liable for signing bonus repayment plus interest. Additionally, you will undergo seventy-two hours of processing before departure."

The words hit me but I can't process them. Termination. No wages. Family liable. Seventy-two hours.

"Sir, please—my family—the house—my brother's in school—"

"Your family will be contacted regarding repayment terms. The signing bonus plus accumulated interest comes to approximately forty-three thousand dollars. They will have sixty days to begin repayment or assets will be seized as collateral."

"Please, sir, I didn't know—I was cooperating—I did everything she asked—"

"The hearing is concluded. Security will escort you to processing."

The security officer stands. His hand is on my arm, pulling me up. The chair is comfortable but I'm not sitting in it anymore. My boots leave dust prints on the carpet as he leads me out.

In the corridor, I try to speak. "I need to call my wife. I need to explain—"

"No communications during processing," the officer says.

"Please, just let me send one message—"

"No communications."

We walk through the carpeted corridors, past the plants in pots, past the clean air and the solid doors. We go down, down, into a section of the central core I've never seen. The corridor narrows. The carpet ends. The walls are bare metal again, like the outer ring, but different. Institutional. Cold.

We stop at a door marked PROCESSING - AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

The officer opens it. Inside is a small room with a metal chair bolted to the floor and a drain in the corner. No windows. No plants. The air smells like disinfectant and something else, something chemical.

"Seventy-two hours," the officer says. "Someone will be with you shortly."

He pushes me inside and closes the door. The lock clicks.

I stand in the middle of the room. The chair is bolted to the floor. The walls are bare metal, scratched and dented. The drain in the corner is dark, circular, perfect.

I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about my brother in his engineering program. I think about forty-three thousand dollars plus interest. I think about sixty days before they seize the house.

I think about the names I gave. Carlos. Jin. Amara. Dante. Marcus. Sarah. Nine workers total.

I think about Supervisor Reyes and her coffee and the way she touched my hand.

I think about Captain Torres reading from his tablet, his voice steady. Not cruel. Just following procedure. Following the contract.

I sit down on the floor, my back against the wall, and I stare at the drain.

---

The door opens. Someone enters—a medical technician in a clean uniform, carrying a tray with instruments I don't recognize.

"Mr. Santos," the technician says. "I'm here to begin your processing. This will take seventy-two hours. You'll be asked questions. You'll be given medications to help with the transition."

"What kind of questions?"

"Questions about your organizing activity. Questions about your cooperation. Questions about your understanding of contract terms." The technician sets the tray on a small table I didn't notice before. "The medications will help you process the information clearly. They'll help you accept what happened."

"Accept what?"

"That you violated your contract. That you endangered your family. That you made choices that led to this outcome." The technician prepares a syringe. "The processing is designed to help you leave here with a clear understanding of your role in what happened. So you can move forward without confusion or resentment."

The technician approaches with the syringe. I don't resist. What would be the point? The contract is clear. The tribunal has decided. Seventy-two hours of processing, then transport to Earth, then my family gets the bill.

The needle goes in. The medication is cold, spreading through my arm, up to my shoulder, into my chest where it meets the weight that's been there for months.

"The first session will begin in fifteen minutes," the technician says. "Try to relax. The medication will help."

The technician leaves. The door closes. The lock clicks.

I sit on the floor with my back against the wall and I stare at the drain. The medication is spreading through my body, making everything feel distant, soft. The edges of the room blur. The drain becomes the center of everything—dark, circular, perfect.

I think about how everything flows down. How the rock dust swirls in the shower drain. How the lithium flows from the veins to the carts to the ships. How everything flows down, down.

I think about Rosa receiving the notification. Forty-three thousand dollars plus interest. Sixty days to begin repayment. The house with blue tile becomes collateral.

I think about my brother leaving his engineering program. About my mother's medicine running out. About Rosa standing in the house we built, looking at the blue tile.

The medication makes it hard to think clearly but one thing feels clear: I understand now. I understand how it works.

Or maybe that's what the medication wants me to think.

I can't tell anymore. The room is getting softer. The drain is getting darker. My thoughts are slowing down, spreading out like the medication in my blood.

---

The door opens again. A different person this time—someone in a suit, carrying a tablet.

"Mr. Santos," the person says. "I'm here for your first processing session. We're going to talk about your organizing activity, your cooperation, and your understanding of what happened. The medication should help you answer clearly."

I look up at the person. The medication makes everything soft. The person's face is blurry.

"I understand," I say. Or maybe I just think I say it. I can't tell.

"Good. Let's begin. Tell me about your first meeting with Supervisor Reyes."

And I do. I tell them about the coffee, the warm ceramic mug, the way she touched my hand. About the names I gave. About how I thought I was helping. About how I thought I was protecting my family.

I tell them everything because the medication makes it easy. Makes the words flow like water. Like rock dust in the drain.

The person takes notes. Nods occasionally. Asks follow-up questions. The session lasts two hours, maybe three. Time is hard to track.

When it's done, the person leaves. The door closes. The lock clicks.

I'm alone again with the drain.

I don't know how long I sit there. The medication makes time strange. But something is happening in my chest. The weight is changing again. Not lighter. Just different.

Or maybe it's not changing. Maybe the medication is just making me think it's changing.

I can't tell anymore.

---

More sessions. More medications. The technician comes back with different syringes. The person in the suit comes back with different questions.

"Do you understand that you violated your contract?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that you endangered your family?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that you made choices that led to this outcome?"

"Yes."

But do I understand? Or am I just saying yes because the medication makes everything feel true?

The drain is always there. Dark, circular, perfect. I stare at it between sessions. I stare at it during sessions. Sometimes I think I can see things in it—shapes, patterns, meanings.

But maybe that's just the medication too.

---

On the third day—or maybe it's the second day, or the fourth, time is strange now—the person in the suit asks me a different question.

"Mr. Santos, do you understand why you gave those names?"

I think about Carlos. Jin. Amara. Dante. Marcus. Sarah.

"To protect my family," I say.

"And do you understand that protecting your family required cooperation with company procedures?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that cooperation with company procedures is how the framework maintains safety and productivity for everyone?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that workers who don't cooperate with company procedures endanger not just themselves but everyone around them?"

"Yes."

But I'm not sure if I'm understanding or if I'm just repeating. The medication makes everything feel like understanding. Like clarity. Like recognition.

Or maybe it is recognition. Maybe I really do understand now.

I can't tell anymore.

---

On the last day—I think it's the last day, the technician said seventy-two hours and it feels like seventy-two hours but I can't be sure—the person in the suit comes back one more time.

"Mr. Santos, we're almost done. I just need to confirm a few things. Do you understand that you violated your contract?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that you endangered your family?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that you made choices that led to this outcome?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that the company followed proper procedures in your case?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that the company's procedures exist to protect workers and maintain productivity?"

"Yes."

"Do you accept responsibility for your actions?"

"Yes."

"Good." The person makes a final note on the tablet. "Your processing is complete. You'll be transferred to the transport facility in six hours. Your family will be contacted regarding repayment terms. Do you have any questions?"

I think about Rosa. About the house with blue tile. About my brother in his engineering program. About forty-three thousand dollars plus interest.

"No," I say.

"Then we're done here. The medication will wear off over the next few days. You may experience some confusion or disorientation, but that's normal. If you have concerns, you can contact the company's worker assistance program when you return to Earth."

The person leaves. The door closes. The lock doesn't click this time.

I sit on the floor with my back against the wall and I stare at the drain. The medication is still in my system, making everything soft, making everything feel like understanding.

I understand that I violated my contract.

I understand that I endangered my family.

I understand that I made choices that led to this outcome.

I understand that the company followed proper procedures.

I understand that the company's procedures exist to protect workers and maintain productivity.

I accept responsibility for my actions.

But underneath all that understanding, underneath all that acceptance, there's something else. Something the medication can't quite reach. Something that sits in my chest next to the weight, next to the cold water, next to everything else I carry.

A question.

Not a big question. Not an angry question. Just a small question, quiet and persistent.

Do I really understand? Or do I just think I understand because the medication made me think it?

And if I can't tell the difference, does it matter?

The drain in the corner is dark, circular, perfect. Everything flows down into it. Everything flows down.

I close my eyes.

Maybe I understand.

Maybe I don't.

Maybe the medication worked exactly as designed.

Maybe it didn't.

I can't tell anymore.

And maybe that's the point.
