# The Catalyst

The hydrogen sulfide alarm shrieked through two floors of concrete. Rashid took the stairs three at a time. Through the chemical haze he saw Yusof bent over the emergency panel, one hand covering his mouth.

"Keluar!" Rashid shouted, but Yusof was already moving.

They stood in the corridor while the ventilation system cleared the room. The smell was thick enough to taste.

"What happened?" Yusof asked in Malay.

Rashid switched to English. "Thermal decomposition. The catalyst wasn't stable at that pressure."

"The gauge reading was normal. I checked it twice."

"I know." Rashid had bypassed the pressure limiter that morning. He had calculated the risk. He had been wrong.

The incident report took forty minutes. *Unexpected exothermic reaction. Equipment malfunction. No injuries.* He filed it and returned to document the failure in his research journal. Nowhere did he write that he had known the catalyst was unstable. Nowhere did he write that he had ignored the safety protocols because five years of funding were running out.

That night, he told Zainab there had been a small equipment failure. She nodded. Aminah looked up from her book, met his eyes for half a second, then looked away.

---

Three floors below, Aminah filed incident reports alphabetically by department code. Today's batch included one from the research division: *Dr. Rashid bin Abdullah, Equipment Malfunction*. She placed it in the appropriate folder.

At lunch, she sat at her usual table by the wall, back to the room. Her father entered, scanned the room, checked his watch, returned to the stairwell.

Aminah counted to twelve and finished her meal.

---

Hassan's wife was discussing their eldest daughter's marriage prospects when Aminah arrived for Friday dinner. The conversation flowed around the low table: which families had sons of appropriate age, which had good reputations.

Aminah sat at the edge of the gathering, slightly behind Zainab. She had learned this position years ago—present but peripheral. Hassan's three children played in the corner. Rashid and Hassan discussed the offshore platforms.

"Salmah is nineteen now," Siti said, glancing at Aminah, then away. "A good age."

"Very good," Zainab agreed.

Aminah watched her father's hands. They were steady on his teacup, but his shoulders had tightened.

Hassan leaned back. "The system works if you work the system. Good family, good character, good prospects."

"And good health," Siti added. "Strong children."

After dinner, Hassan found Rashid on the balcony.

"You look tired, abang. The work is eating you."

Rashid lit a cigarette. "Research is slow-slow. Can't be rushed."

"Aminah is looking thin also."

Rashid drew smoke, held it, released it. "She's fine."

Hassan was quiet. Then: "Siti has a cousin. Widower, older man. He's not particular about appearance."

The cigarette burned between Rashid's fingers. "I'll think about it."

"Don't think too long."

After Hassan's family left, Rashid returned to the laboratory. He told himself he needed to check the ventilation system. The whole truth was that he could not sit in his house with Zainab's careful silence and Aminah's careful absence.

---

Dr. Karim's office overlooked the harbor. On his wall hung photographs: the Sultan at the petroleum ministry's opening, Karim receiving an award from the Edinburgh Alumni Association, his four sons at graduation.

The quarterly review began at nine. Rashid's project was fifth on the agenda.

"Enhanced extraction methods for heavy crude," Karim read. "Five years, three hundred thousand dollars, two published papers, zero commercial applications." He looked up. "Dr. Rashid is brilliant. Trained at Imperial College. But brilliance without results is expensive thinking."

"The work is technically sound," Dr. Ibrahim offered.

"I don't question the methodology. I question the timeline." Karim tapped the folder. "Platform Seven needs new extraction technology now, not in another five years."

"There was a safety incident last week. Equipment malfunction."

Karim found the report, scanned it. "Add safety compliance review to next quarter's oversight."

The board moved on. Outside, a tanker moved toward the offshore platforms. The harbor was busy, the platforms productive. The work continued.

---

Aminah learned to read her father's patterns the way sailors read the tide. On Monday morning, she heard his footsteps hesitate in the corridor above her filing room. She continued working, head down. The footsteps retreated.

On Wednesday, she saw him at the far entrance of the courtyard. He turned back inside.

On Friday, Zainab mentioned Hassan's wife's cousin. Aminah said she would think about it. Her father's shoulders relaxed slightly.

That night, she performed the calculation she had been performing since she was fourteen: salary, expenses, savings. How long until she could afford a room of her own. The numbers never quite added up. The filing clerk position paid enough to contribute to the household but not enough to leave it.

In his laboratory, Rashid recalibrated the pressure monitoring system. The work was meticulous. He welcomed it. Precision was something he understood.

The catalyst synthesis required three more weeks. After that, another test series. After that—

He stopped. After that, what?

Through the window, he could see the lights of the offshore platforms. Hassan was out there now, managing a night shift, confident in the system that employed him.

Rashid returned to his calibration.

---

The next incident report crossed Aminah's desk three weeks later. Same laboratory, different equipment. *Pressure seal failure during catalyst testing. Minor chemical exposure. No injuries.*

She filed it.

That evening, Hassan came to dinner without his family. He spoke in Malay, his voice careful.

"The cousin is interested. He's seen Aminah at the mosque. He's willing to proceed."

Rashid set down his fork. "I haven't discussed this with her."

"Discuss what? A man is interested. That's good news."

"She should have a choice."

Hassan's expression shifted. "A choice? Abang, you're a scientist. You know how the world works. Some people have choices. Some people have circumstances. Aminah has circumstances."

Zainab stood and began clearing dishes.

"She has a job," Rashid said. "A salary."

"A filing clerk's salary. In a position you arranged. How long does that last if you're not there to maintain it?" Hassan leaned forward. "The cousin is a good man. He has a house, a business, respect. He's offering her security."

"I'll think about it."

"There's nothing to think about. There's only what is." Hassan stood. "Talk to her. Or don't. But don't pretend you're protecting her by doing nothing."

After Hassan left, Rashid sat at the table while Zainab finished in the kitchen. Aminah had gone to her room—he had heard the door close when Hassan's voice rose. He should go to her. He should ask what she wanted. He should have asked twenty years ago.

He went to his laboratory instead.

---

Dr. Karim's office door was open when Rashid arrived. The quarterly review folder sat on the desk.

"Sit. We need to discuss timeline expectations."

Rashid had prepared a presentation. He opened his briefcase.

Karim held up his hand. "I've read your reports. The technical work is excellent. But we're five years in with no commercial application, and I'm seeing safety incidents every few weeks."

"The incidents are minor. Standard research hazards."

"Standard research hazards that require incident reports." Karim opened the folder, showing a stack of yellow forms. "I respect your work. But respect doesn't change the fact that this project is consuming resources without producing results."

"I need more time."

"You've had time. What you need is a different approach." Karim closed the folder. "I'm not terminating the project. But I am scaling back funding and requiring monthly progress reviews. If we don't see commercial viability indicators within six months, we'll need to consider reallocation."

Rashid nodded at appropriate moments, collected his briefcase, returned to his laboratory. Six months wasn't enough time.

He could stop. Accept a teaching position, focus on his family. The thought sat in his mind like a foreign object—theoretically possible, practically impossible. Who was he if not the scientist pursuing this solution?

He began the catalyst synthesis procedure.

---

Aminah was filing quarterly reports when Dr. Karim appeared in the doorway.

"Miss Aminah. A moment, please."

She followed him to his office.

"Your work is excellent. The filing system has never been more efficient." He paused. "I'm restructuring the research division. I'd like to create a senior filing position. Increased responsibility, increased salary. I think you'd be well-suited for it."

The offer was generous. Too generous.

"Thank you, Dr. Karim. May I think about it?"

"Of course. But don't think too long. The restructuring begins next month."

She returned to her filing room. The quarterly reports documented her father's project, marked with yellow incident report tabs. She read the summary: *scaled funding, monthly reviews, six-month deadline*.

She filed the report and continued working.

---

That evening, Rashid came home at seven—earlier than usual.

"There's been a change at work," he said in Malay. "Budget adjustments."

Aminah watched her mother's hands go still.

"Dr. Karim is restructuring. Some projects are being scaled back. Mine included."

"Will you lose your position?" Zainab asked.

"No. Just reduced resources."

Aminah set down her fork. "Dr. Karim offered me a promotion today."

Rashid's gaze met hers. "A promotion?"

"Senior filing clerk. He said the restructuring will generate additional documentation."

Silence. Zainab resumed eating.

"You should take it," Rashid said in English. "It's a good opportunity."

"Is it?" Aminah asked in Malay. "Or is it compensation?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Hassan's wife has a cousin. Dr. Karim has a promotion. Everyone has solutions for circumstances they didn't create."

Zainab stood and began clearing dishes.

"What do you want me to say?" Rashid's voice was quiet.

Aminah looked at her father—at the exhaustion in his face, the guilt in his posture. She had learned to read these signs before she learned to read books. She knew they meant he was sorry. She knew they meant he didn't know how to fix what he had broken. She knew they meant he would go back to his laboratory because work was something he understood and she was something he didn't.

"Nothing," she said. "There's nothing to say."

She helped her mother with the dishes. Rashid sat at the table for ten minutes, then collected his briefcase and left.

---

The catalyst synthesis succeeded. Rashid tested it at low pressure, then moderate, documenting each result. The reaction was stable. The extraction efficiency was promising.

He prepared his monthly progress report, including data tables, methodology notes, projected timelines.

The review lasted thirty minutes. Karim read the report, asked questions, examined the data.

"This is good work. But good work at laboratory scale isn't commercial viability. We need industrial-scale results."

"Industrial scale requires different equipment. That infrastructure takes time to build."

"How much time?"

"Eighteen months. Possibly two years."

Karim was quiet. "You have six months to demonstrate scalability potential. Not full industrial implementation—just evidence that scaling is feasible. If you can show that, we'll discuss extended funding."

Rashid returned to his laboratory. Six months to demonstrate scalability with equipment designed for small-batch testing. Six months to justify the last five years.

He began designing the scaling tests.

---

Aminah accepted the promotion. The new position came with a small office. She could close the door. She could control who entered.

The salary increase was modest but meaningful. She recalculated: three years until she could afford a room somewhere else.

Hassan's wife stopped mentioning the cousin. The opportunity had passed, Siti said. The man had found someone else. Aminah nodded.

She filed the documents that detailed her father's scaled funding, his monthly reviews, his six-month deadline. She filed the incident reports that documented his failures. She filed everything in its proper place, and she did not think about what it meant that her job was to organize the evidence of her father's desperation.

---

The scaling test failed.

Rashid had calculated the pressure ratios, adjusted the catalyst concentration, modified the reaction vessel. The mathematics were sound. The methodology was rigorous.

The catalyst decomposed anyway.

The alarm shrieked. The ventilation roared. Yusof evacuated, and Rashid stood in the corridor, breathing air that tasted like rot, and understood that six months would not be enough because no amount of time would be enough because the fundamental problem was not solvable with current technology and he had spent five years refusing to accept that.

He wrote the incident report. *Thermal decomposition during scaling test. Catalyst instability at elevated pressure. No injuries. Recommend project reassessment.*

---

Dr. Karim's office was quiet when Rashid arrived for his unscheduled appointment.

"The scaling test failed," Rashid said. "The catalyst is not stable at industrial pressure."

Karim nodded slowly. "Can it be modified?"

"Possibly. With different materials, different reaction conditions. But that would require starting over."

"How long?"

"Five years. Maybe more."

Karim leaned back. "Dr. Rashid, I respect your honesty. Most researchers would have hidden the failure, requested additional funding, promised results they couldn't deliver."

Rashid waited.

"But the truth is that we can't invest another five years in a project that might not succeed. The ministry has limited resources." Karim paused. "I'm not terminating your employment. You're a valuable researcher. But this specific project is concluded. I'd like you to transition to the technical review committee."

The offer was generous. He would retain his position, his salary, his standing.

He would not pursue the solution he had spent five years chasing.

"Thank you, Dr. Karim. I accept."

---

Aminah processed the project termination documents three days later. Her father's five-year pursuit reduced to sixteen pages of administrative paperwork.

She filed them alphabetically by department code.

That evening, Rashid came home at six. He helped Zainab prepare dinner. He asked about Aminah's work.

After dinner, he asked if Aminah would like to take a walk.

They followed the harbor road, past the fishing boats, toward the offshore platforms visible in the distance. The evening call to prayer echoed from the mosque.

"The project is finished," Rashid said in Malay. "Dr. Karim has reassigned me to technical review."

Aminah nodded. She had filed the paperwork.

"I thought if I succeeded, it would matter. The work would be worth it."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what it was worth."

They walked in silence. Aminah watched the platforms in the distance, their lights steady against the darkening water.

"Hassan's wife has another cousin," Rashid said quietly. "Younger man, works in the petroleum ministry's accounting division. He asked Hassan to make inquiries."

Aminah stopped walking. "And what did you tell Hassan?"

"I told him I would ask you what you wanted."

Aminah looked at her father—at the exhaustion in his face, the guilt in his posture.

"I don't know what I want," she said finally. "But I know what I don't want. I don't want to be arranged. I don't want to be someone's solution to someone else's problem."

Rashid nodded slowly. "Then I'll tell Hassan no."

"He won't understand."

"He doesn't need to understand. He needs to accept it."

They resumed walking. The harbor stretched before them, the platforms steady in the distance.

"I'm sorry," Rashid said, his voice barely audible over the water. "For all of it. For not being there. For the job I arranged. For the circumstances I created."

Aminah watched the platforms, their lights reflecting on the dark water. She thought about her small office, her three-year timeline to a room of her own. She thought about her father's five-year pursuit of a solution that didn't exist.

"I know," she said.

They walked back in silence. Zainab had left the porch light on. Inside, everything was in its place.

Rashid went to his study to sit by the window and watch the harbor lights. Aminah went to her room and recalculated her savings timeline, the numbers stretching before her like a bridge she would cross slowly, step by careful step.

In his office, Dr. Karim filed the project termination documents and turned to the next quarter's budget allocations. The work continued.

On Platform Seven, Hassan supervised the night shift, confident in the system that employed him.

The harbor lights reflected on dark water. The offshore platforms maintained their steady rhythm.
