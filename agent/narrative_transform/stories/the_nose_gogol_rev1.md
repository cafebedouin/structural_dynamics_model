# The Weight of the Cord

## I.

The splice-knot sat at the back of Ostrun Vey's neck like a second spine. He found it now without looking, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. Still there. Still fused. Still warm from his own skin.

He climbed to the third gallery with the petition folded twice in his coat, though the paper hardly mattered anymore. Not refused — he knew better than to think refused. Refused implied someone had looked at the thing and said no. What happened in the Reading-chamber was quieter than that, and worse.

Notary Ceth Ambrail sat at the long table with the ledger open, hands flat on either side of the binding, and looked up with an expression of complete, professional welcome that held no memory of him at all, though he had stood in this doorway more times than either of them cared to name.

"By what tier do you address the Table," Ceth said.

"Fifth. Fused-fifth. Lock-Warden Ostrun Vey, cording reference —" and he gave the long string that identified the marriage-fusion entry, memorized the way a man memorizes a wound's exact dimensions, because the dimensions were the only part anyone else could be made to look at.

Ceth's hands moved to the correct tab without looking. "This is the matter of the separable identity."

"It is."

"You bring the same claim as before."

"I bring it again because it was never seated." He heard in his own voice the flatness of a man reciting liturgy he no longer believed would be answered, only performed. "My wife is gone these long years now. The fusion was hers and mine together. I am asking the Table to recognize that a thing joined can be shown to have once been separate."

"The Table does not dispute that it was once separate," Ceth said, kindly. "The Table requires a witnessed anomaly-event to process a claim of this class. You have brought testimony. Testimony is not witness."

"I was there when we were spliced. I remember the splice."

"Your memory is not doubted. It is not admissible in the shape this claim requires." Ceth's eyes went down the index, the way a woman's eyes might travel down a lock-mechanism, checking each tooth for the one that would catch. "There is no category presently seated for testimony of a fusion's original separateness, uncorroborated. I would enter it if I could find where it goes."

"Then where does it go."

Ceth closed the tab with the soft sound of a page returning to its fold. "The Table thanks you for bringing this forward. It has not been seated at this time."

Not denied. Not seated. Ostrun had believed, the first time this happened, that the difference meant there was still a door somewhere. He no longer believed that. He also had not stopped bringing the petition. These facts sat in him without resolving each other.

He left past the bench of other petitioners, out through the gallery's wet stone air. He did not notice that the small unfiled girl who sometimes sat against the gallery's outer wall — the one the residents called by an accounting instead of a name, born on a gallery deep enough to hold no tier at all — was gone from her usual place. He would say, if asked, that of course he'd seen the child around. He would not be able to say when he had last looked at her.

That evening, alone in his sleeping-berth, he checked the splice-knot again, as if the only variable left in his life worth managing was whether the rope his dead wife's rank was tied into still lay flat against his own.

It did. It always did. That was the whole of the problem.

## II.

Notary Ceth Ambrail's morning went the way Ceth's mornings generally went: correctly.

There was a satisfaction — Ceth did not call it that, in the private grammar Ceth used even alone, but it functioned as satisfaction — in watching a Reading-day open onto its bench and knowing, category by category, where each petitioner would land. A dispute over a contested water-right had a page. An engineer's request to certify an apprentice early had pages, an entire procedure with its own appendix. Even Ostrun Vey's petition had a page — the page simply described a category that could not receive what he brought it. An unseatable claim was not a claim the Table had failed. It was a claim shaped wrong for any table at all, and the kindest true thing anyone could do was say so plainly, rather than pretend a door existed where none had ever been cut.

Instructor-Engineer Prel Kastade arrived early, required by quorum rule to witness any lock-adjacent certification, and settled into the observer's chair with the ease of a man for whom quorum duty was a mild interruption to a full day. He asked whether the pressure-lock recertification on the western seal had been logged, because he wanted the apprentice cleared before the seep-season made the gallery too wet to train in.

"Not yet. I'll have it by midday."

"No rush." He was good at the locks, had been for years, had saved lives with that goodness in the way that mattered to the Cistern's survival more than almost anything else the Table adjudicated. Ceth respected this the way one respects weather that has always been fair.

The next petitioner was brought forward not by herself but by a woman named Hara Dost, unfiled, gaunt in the way of someone who had spent a winter stretching chits meant for herself across two mouths, a child's hand held loosely in hers — not gripping, the way you'd hold a hand you were presenting as evidence, but the way you'd hold a hand you were simply keeping near you in a cold room.

"By what tier do you address the Table," Ceth said, to Hara, who was filed, marginally, at the second tier.

"Second. I bring forward a matter for the child. Her cording was never entered. Her mother's tier was under dispute at the birth-hour, and the birth fell into no window the Table had open. She has gone every year of her life without a page."

Ceth looked, for a held breath, at the child — who did not meet Ceth's eyes, who had positioned herself half a step behind Hara's skirts, in the geometry of someone who had learned very young exactly how far a room's attention could reach — and found the tab without needing to search.

"This is the matter of the uncontested-but-unentered birth."

"It's not uncontested. That's the trouble of it. It was contested, between rival tier-claims. Both households have since —" she stopped, chose a different word — "stopped disputing it. There's no one contesting anymore. She's fallen through the gap between claims that both went quiet."

This was, Ceth recognized, a genuinely difficult shape, and Ceth's mind ran down the index, searching for the page that would receive it.

There was no such page.

"The Table requires the original contest to be formally withdrawn by a party of record before a birth-cording can be re-opened," Ceth said. "If the parties have ceased pursuing the matter without formally withdrawing —"

"They're dead," Hara said, flatly. "Flood-fever. There's no one left to withdraw anything."

Ceth held very still.

This was the crack, and it lasted a beat longer than the beat Ceth would have held for Ostrun Vey's petition — long enough that Ceth looked at the child again, at the bare unbraided throat above her collar, cold-reddened in the wet air, and thought — in the Table's own grammar, because there was no other grammar left to think in even for that single private beat — *there is no procedural mechanism by which a dead party's silence can stand in for a living party's withdrawal, and a Table with every page it could ever be given would still have no page for a thing that was never one thing to begin with — not mis-filed, but the leftover any filing makes.*

Procedure resumed.

"I am sorry," Ceth said, and meant it, in the limited way the Table's grammar permitted meaning things. "The claim cannot presently be seated. There is no page for a contest resolved by the death of both parties rather than formal withdrawal. I would enter it if a page existed to enter it into."

"She's a child still," Hara said, not pleading, simply stating the fact as though its bareness might do work the procedure couldn't. "She's some of the unfiled, and she's going to stay some of the unfiled. Dead households can't sign a withdrawal."

"I understand the weight of what you're describing," Ceth said, and did — and still could not make the ledger's architecture bend around a shape it had never been built to hold. "The Table thanks you for bringing this forward. It has not been seated at this time."

Hara did not argue further. There was nothing to argue with, no adversary in the room, only an absence of a slot. She took the child's hand again and turned from the bench.

"Where will you take her now," Ceth said. It was not required, not on any page. Ceth heard the question leave before deciding to ask it, and then it was asked, and could not be unasked.

Hara turned back, and something moved in her face that was not quite surprise and not quite contempt — closer to the fatigue of being seen by the wrong kind of attention, attention with no page to put her on. "Where I always take her," she said. "Where the ones without a place take the ones without a place. You don't have a form for that either." She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way you'd tell a man the hour, when he'd asked out of some need of his own that had nothing to do with the time.

"What happens to her," Ceth said, "if no page ever comes."

"Then she stays some of the unfiled her whole life, same as I did, same as her mother would've if the fever hadn't got there first. She'll eat what gets passed hand to hand. She'll sleep where the ledge stays dry. None of that needs your Table's permission. It's the only mercy in the arrangement — you can't be refused a thing you never asked for." Hara adjusted her grip on the child's hand. "You asked me a question with no page for it. I'll give you the only answer that has none either. We'll manage. We have been managing. Managing isn't the same as being seen, but nobody down where we live mistakes the two anymore."

She went, the child close along the chamber's wall rather than out across its open floor, through the door someone else had left ajar.

Prel, from the observer's chair, said, mildly, "Sad business, that. Shame there's no way round it," the way a man says something true and regrettable about weather, and asked whether the recertification might be ready before midday after all. Ceth told him it would. He gathered his coat and left in good humor, already thinking about locks.

Ceth sat with the ledger open to the page that described what could not receive Hara Dost's claim, thumb resting along its edge, unmoving. A question with no page for it had been answered by a woman who had every right to walk away without answering, and the answer sat now where no ledger-tab could file it — a splinter gone in crooked, to be dug for later, at some cost, by someone.

The next petitioner was called. The morning went on, correctly.

## III.

Ostrun went down some evenings later, though he could not afterward have said what specifically sent him — only the old wound settling further into its groove, the way a rope worn against the same stone edge stops fraying and simply flattens, taking the shape of the wear.

He passed gallery after gallery, the stone going darker and colder, past the lowest certified lock, past the point where any cord's tier meant anything to any door, until the spiral stair gave out onto the bare stone of the aquifer's original floor — granular, ungoverned, holding old cold in it like a held breath.

There was no gate. There had never been a gate. Nothing here asked him for a tier before it let him stand on it.

He had come here after the burial, though he'd never admitted as much to anyone at the Table, and had come back since, on the worse nights — not praying by the end of it, just present, in the one place the fused cord weighed no differently than an unfused one would have.

Someone was already there.

He saw her before he understood what he was seeing — a small shape settled against the base of the old stone where no lamp-bracket threw light, working her fingers along the knots of a length of frayed cordage, unhurried. She did not startle at his approach, though she went still, the way of someone accustomed to gauging whether stillness or movement made her less visible, and decided stillness would do.

He knew who she was, in the loose way everyone on the lower galleries knew — the girl they called by the accounting, who slept where the wet ledge stayed dry enough, who ate what the unfiled passed hand to hand in their own warm, unranked grammar the Table's ledger had no ear for.

He looked at her now, properly, for what he understood — dimly — was perhaps the first time.

Her throat was bare. No braid, no dye, no knot, no weight at the collarbone. Just skin, cold-reddened, catching no lamp-light because there was none down here to catch. Something in Ostrun's chest went cold at the sight of it. His hand moved to answer before his mind found a thought to send it, fingers going to his own splice-knot, the double-thickness of a cord that held too much.

She did not ask why he was staring. She went back to her knots.

He sat, some distance off, saying nothing at first. The Cistern-formal address had no shape for this room, and the only other register he owned — stripped, warm, rank-less — belonged to a dead woman's mouth and not his own.

Her fingers stopped. She held up a length of cord toward him, not offering exactly, just showing — a knot doubled back on itself that would not come loose no matter which end she pulled. "This one's stuck," she said. Her voice was younger than he expected and flatter, the voice of someone who had learned early that wanting things said plainly was a luxury and said them plainly anyway. "Nobody taught me the undoing. Just the tying."

It was the first thing he had ever heard her say. He held out his hand, and she gave him the cord, and he turned it over, feeling for where it had crossed itself, the way his hands still knew how to feel for a fault in a lock nobody had asked him to fix in years.

"Here," he said, working the loop free with a thumbnail. "You have to loosen the wrong end first. Feels backward. It isn't." He handed it back undone.

She took it, tested the give of it, said nothing — no thanks, just tucked the loosened cord into whatever she kept things in, the way you'd bank a small true thing against a use you couldn't yet name. Then she went back to a different length, a different knot, and did not offer this one to him.

He did not ask her name. She did not ask his.

The lamp-brackets did not reach this deep. The cold came up through the stone the same for both of them. They sat on a while longer in the dark — his throat too heavy, hers too bare — and neither of them moved to leave.
