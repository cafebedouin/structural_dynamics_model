# The Damp

The stove had been lit since matins and still her hands would not warm.

Marfa Osipovna held them to the black iron, turned them front and back, though the day was dry and the fire good. Foma Silovich kept a well-stocked woodpile — he reminded her of it often — and the house did not creak with cold the way her father's had, out on the drier ground where the wind came in under the door. Here the door was tight. Here the roof did not weep. It was, by every measure she'd been given to measure a house by, a good house, and she was ashamed to stand at the stove with her hands out like a beggar's, as if her own blood had simply grown too thin to hold what the house provided.

She had always been a poor keeper of blood. Her mother used to say so — *Marfushka runs cold, always did* — and so when the ache came into her wrists that winter, and the grey crept up under her nails like dusk coming early, she understood it the way she understood everything about her body: a fault of her own thinness, not a message from anywhere outside herself.

She did not write to her mother. To write would mean saying she was unwell, and to say she was unwell in her husband's house, to kin outside it, was to say the house did not tend her — and the house *did* tend her, this she would have sworn on the Gospel. Foma Silovich did not raise his hand. He brought in fish and marsh-fowl and, that autumn, a length of red wool traded for a fox-pelt, because red suited her coloring, he said — though by the time it came she had stopped believing anything suited her coloring, since her coloring had gone the grey of standing water.

She kept the threshold, as her mother had before her. When old Xenia from the next steading came asking after her, Marfa Osipovna went out to the yard herself rather than let the old woman past the gate, arms folded into her shawl so the shaking wouldn't show, and said, *I am well, Foma Silovich keeps a warm house* — and believed it as she said it, the way one believes the sky is up.

Her hands ached worst in the evening, after the water-carrying. She had begun, without deciding to, setting the buckets down partway to the door, hating herself for the weakness, glancing back at the house as though it might be watching her fail at the thing a wife was for.

---

The znakharka Praskovya Terentievna had delivered children into the marsh air for most of her adult life, and buried a good many of the mothers who bore them, and knew the damp in every mood it had — a slow damp and a quick damp, a damp of first frost and a damp of thaw-mud, a damp that took the old first and, more cruelly, a damp that took young mothers fastest of all.

She was called to Foma Silovich's house near the Feast of the Intercession, on account of the wife's cough, and found a young woman sitting very straight at the table with her hands folded into her sleeves. She unwrapped them herself, since Marfa Osipovna seemed reluctant, and looked a long moment at the nail-beds.

She had seen that grey — the blue-grey of a duck's egg left too long in the shell. She could have named other women whose nails had gone that color, and roughly how each season had gone for them, wet or dry, what herbs she'd given, in what order.

What she could not tell you — what nagged her, later, walking the dike with her basket on her arm — was why none of them quite fit this case in front of her.

She gave nettle and the bitter marsh-root that brought water off the lungs. She told Foma Silovich, arms crossed in the doorway, his face arranged into the correct shape of a worried husband, that his wife should rest and take broth and be kept from drafts. He nodded, thanked her formally, paid her in dried fish. She left thinking she had done what could be done.

It was on the walk home, the light going long over the reeds, that she said aloud, to no one, "Some fevers come from the pot, not the marsh." Her own teacher had said it once, meaning it as a warning against bad cooking, spoiled grain, a house let to ruin. Praskovya had laughed at it even as a girl. She laughed at it again now, alone on the dike, a little embarrassed at herself.

To carry the thought further would mean standing again in that clean kitchen, asking a man who had paid her promptly and thanked her with proper feeling what exactly his wife had eaten that week, from whose hand, and why. There was no way to ask it that would not sound, even to her own ear, like an accusation with nothing behind it. She remembered how fast a healer's usefulness could curdle into suspicion — how Fedosya Yakovlevna, two circuits over, had her herbs called poison by a doctor in front of a room full of witnesses, and never worked again. Praskovya was not young. Her standing was the only thing she owned outright. She put the thought away, and did not write it anywhere, because writing it would have cost her the right to be trusted at the next threshold, and the one after that.

---

By the first hard frost, Marfa Osipovna could not lift a full bucket. Foma Silovich took over the carrying himself, without being asked, and told anyone who inquired that his wife was under the damp and being nursed as well as any wife in the marsh. No one doubted it. It happened to be true.

His uncle Prov Andreyevich, who had married them himself, remarked to another elder that Foma Silovich was managing his household with proper order despite the affliction God had sent it — that a lesser man might have sent the wife back to her people. That he kept her, tended her, spoke of her with correct grave tenderness at council meetings: to every man in that room, this was evidence of a well-run house and nothing else.

There came an evening that season when Praskovya arrived to find him at the washing-trough behind the house, scrubbing a pot with more force than the pot required, sleeves pushed back, jaw set in a way she'd never seen it set in front of company. He looked up and saw her before he'd composed himself, and for the length of a breath his face held something that was not the grief-shape he wore indoors — something flatter, more tired, a man doing a chore rather than performing a devotion. Then he saw her seeing it, and the grief-shape closed back over it like a shutter, and he asked, pleasantly, would she like tea before she went in. Praskovya said she would not, she'd come straight to the patient, and went in, and did not think of the look again until much later, when it was too late to be anything but a thing she had once seen.

Marfa Osipovna, in the rare hours the fever loosened, thought of her mother's face and felt a grief with nothing to be sad *at.* Not the house, which had never once failed to be warm and provisioned. Not her husband, whom she addressed even in the privacy of her own skull as Foma Silovich, formal, patronymic — no small name worn soft by use in the dark. The grief sat in her chest with no direction to travel, the way water sits in a low place with no outlet, going stagnant for want of the sea.

She thought, some nights, of sending for her mother anyway. The thought came and went like a coal flaring in ash. To send for her mother would be to say: come look at what is wrong in my house. And there was nothing wrong in her house. There was only her, wrong, in it.

---

The district doctor came in the spring, as he did every spring and autumn when the roads held — a brisk sandy-bearded man named Kirill Denisovich Struve, trained in Dorpat, instruments in a case lined with green baize.

Praskovya walked out to be present. Struve did not resent a znakharka's presence in the room.

He was gentle with Marfa Osipovna, who could no longer sit upright without support. He looked at her tongue, her eyes gone the color of old linen, pressed at the swelling in her wrists and ankles, had her cough into a cloth and studied what came up, asked through Praskovya about her appetite, her sleep, whether she'd traveled anywhere the vapors might run stronger, whether her water came from the river or the well.

He ruled out the quick consumption that took the lungs alone. Ruled out childbed fever, she having borne no child. Set aside the joint-swelling of cold standing water, her ankles showing a different character. Asked whether the household kept animals near the sleeping-quarters, and was told they did not.

He wrote, in the ledger he carried beside his instruments:

*Wasting fever, of the marsh type, advanced stage. Attributable to prolonged exposure, damp climate, and constitutional weakness of the patient. Recommend continued rest, warmth, nourishing broth. Prognosis guarded.*

He was not wrong in any particular he examined. He did not ask what Foma Silovich fed his wife with his own hand — a husband feeding a wife too weak to feed herself was not a category of illness, only what a husband did, in a well-run house. Struve had, in fact, noted with private approval how carefully Foma Silovich had kept a broth going that week specifically because the doctor was expected.

He closed his case, said a few kind words the patient may or may not have heard, told Foma Silovich to send word if she worsened sharply, and rode on to the next steading.

Praskovya walked him partway to the crossing and asked whether he had ever seen a damp-case that didn't answer to any remedy at all — not worsen and improve the way damp cases did, but simply worsen, with no plateau.

He considered this seriously and said constitution varied greatly; some patients had less to give the illness to work against, and grief, poor diet in childhood, a naturally cold humor, could account for a steeper decline than the marsh alone. He said it kindly. He believed it.

Praskovya thanked him and turned back, and did not say the thing about the pot. A woman of her standing did not trade in superstition in front of a Dorpat-trained doctor — and to say it to a man with the authority to write things in ledgers was to risk him writing something else about her. She had watched that happen to Fedosya. She was not going to watch it happen to herself.

---

That same spring she was called to another house, a good walk over, to look at a servant-girl who'd taken sick — no kin of anyone's, an orphaned girl bonded into the household for her keep, thin to begin with and thinner now. The moment Praskovya took her wrist to feel the pulse, she saw the grey creeping into the girl's nail-beds.

The mistress stood in the doorway the whole visit, arms folded, saying the girl had always been sickly, had come to them sickly, that they'd fed her better than her own kin ever had and still she would not thrive. Praskovya, turning the grey hands front and back the way she'd turned Marfa Osipovna's, felt a cold small motion under her ribs: *I have felt this exact cold before, this season, in another house.*

She asked whether the girl ate apart from the family or at the same table. Apart, in the kitchen, off what was sent back — which was, the mistress added, no different from how any servant-girl ate in any house on the river. True enough that Praskovya could make nothing more of it. She asked whether anyone else in the house had taken poorly that year. No, thank God, only the girl, who had always been the weak one.

She gave the same nettle, the same bitter root, told the mistress to see she ate and keep her from drafts. There was no drawer in her mind for the thing that would have let her set these two households side by side and ask what the hands had in common rather than what the marsh had done to each separately. Resemblance sat next to resemblance in her memory like grain in separate sacks, never opened together at the same table.

The girl recovered that autumn. The grey retreated, her color came back up through her hands like a stain reversing, and Praskovya heard of it at market, in the middle of an ordinary sentence about someone's flax. The relief came first, plain and physical, a loosening at the back of her neck. Underneath it: if the girl had *not* recovered, there would have been two households this year, not one — and two was a shape, and a shape was a thing a person could be asked about. The girl living meant there was nothing yet that had to be asked. Praskovya let the relief cover that thought the way water covers a stone, and did not go back to ask the mistress anything further. There was nothing to ask that wouldn't sound like an accusation with no evidence behind it, and she had already paid, once, to learn what that costs.

---

Marfa Osipovna died the week after the first hard rime of the following autumn, in the bed she had shared with Foma Silovich, in the house whose door had never once let in a draft or a visitor uninvited. She had stopped being able to hold a cup without his hand steadying it, and his hand steadied it to the last. In the accounting of everyone who came to sit with the body — Prov Andreyevich among them, murmuring the correct words about God's will — she was a wife who had been faithfully nursed through a wasting no one could have arrested, in a house that had done everything a house could do.

Praskovya came to lay her out, and studied the grey hands, turning them the way she had turned them at that very first visit, the memory rising up whole and unwelcome. The color had gone further than she had ever seen it go and lived. The cold motion came again under her ribs, stronger this time, with nowhere to go. She thought of the servant-girl's hands, the same grey, the same turning — and understood that whatever a long working life in this marsh had bought her the right to say out loud, she had spent all of it already, on nothing, and there was none left to spend here either.

She said nothing of it to Prov Andreyevich, nor to Foma Silovich, who thanked her with real feeling for all she had done, and meant it, as far as she could tell — and she could tell a great deal, having spent a lifetime learning to read a room by grief and by guilt, and having never once, in this house, caught the second on any face but the dying woman's own, turned always, unaccountably, against herself.

She walked home in the dark with her basket empty and did not say the thing about the pot again, not even to the reeds.

---

Foma Silovich remarried before long — a girl from a steading upriver, young, new to the settlement, glad by every account of a warm, tight-doored house after a colder childhood.

The following spring Praskovya heard at market that the new wife had taken a cough that would not lift with the thaw. She heard it in the same breath as news of a lambing, and felt her own feet, standing in the mud of the square, already knowing the answer before she asked herself the question: she could walk there. No one would bar the gate. A neighborly visit wanted no summons at all, only a basket and a reason, and she had roots in her bag that traveled well.

She did not go. She finished the business she'd come to market for, bought what she'd come to buy, and turned home along the dike in the ordinary light of an ordinary afternoon, and understood, walking, that this was the cheapest silence she had ever bought — no threat in it this time, no doctor's ledger to fear, no standing left worth protecting that going would have cost her. Only the plain, unglamorous fact that she was old, and tired in a way that did not lift with rest, and that the road was long, and that a woman could tell herself the road was long for exactly as many years as she had left to walk it.

She thought, once, of the grey creeping under fingernails in that kitchen, and how she would very likely hear of it again if she lived long enough. She let the thought settle where all the others had settled, and walked on toward the next house on her circuit, where someone was waiting who had sent for her, and so was hers to tend.
