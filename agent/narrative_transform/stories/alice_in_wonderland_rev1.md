# The Lexicon Problem

Dr. Sarah Okonkwo had been on Kepler-7 for eleven days when she heard the term that would change her research trajectory. She was in Ring C's maintenance bay, recording device pressed against her palm, watching Maya Reyes recalibrate a quantum node that had been throwing errors for six hours.

"*Ang bituin na ito ay nag-uumpisa nang maging patay*," Maya said to her pod-mate, Jun. The star is starting to die.

Sarah's Tagalog was functional enough to catch the metaphor. She pressed record.

Jun handed Maya a diagnostic scanner. "*Kailan mo huling nakita ang ganito?*"

"*Noong nakaraang buwan. Pero mas mabilis ngayon.*"

Maya swapped out the node's cooling assembly, checked the entanglement stability readings, then pulled back and wiped her forehead with her sleeve.

"*Bituin na patay*," Maya said finally, pointing at the failed node. "Dead star."

Sarah made a note: *bituin na patay — catastrophic node failure. Etymology: star metaphor. First recorded use: Maya Reyes, 2089-03-15, 14:23 station time.*

Later that day, she watched Rafael Santos file the incident report. He sat in his cabin in Ring B, typing with the careful precision of someone translating between worlds.

"Node experienced critical malfunction," he wrote. "Cooling system failure led to entanglement destabilization. Replacement required."

Sarah leaned against the doorframe. "Maya called it *bituin na patay*."

Rafael looked up. His face showed the particular exhaustion Sarah had started to recognize in the supervisors.

"Dead star," he said in English. "Yeah. That's Maya's term."

"But you didn't use it in the report."

"Earth-side doesn't speak Technical Tagalog." He turned back to the console. "They need Institutional English. 'Node malfunction' is the term in the manual."

"But it's not the same thing."

Rafael's fingers paused over the keyboard. "No," he said quietly. "It's not."

---

Sarah had come to Kepler-7 to study orbital language evolution. Her previous research had focused on creole formation in isolated communities. Kepler-7 was the logical extension: a space station where forty-seven people from three countries had been rotating through eighteen-month contracts for twenty-eight years, developing their own operational vocabulary.

What she'd found was more complex than simple creole formation. The crew operated in three distinct languages, each serving a different social function.

Technical Tagalog was the work language. Filipino-based but heavily modified with invented terms for orbital engineering problems that had no Earth-side names. It changed constantly as crews encountered new malfunctions and coined new terms to describe them.

Institutional English was the administrative language. Formal, frozen, preserved from the station's 2061 founding documents. It was the language of official reports, crew evaluations, communications with Earth-side management.

Bahay-Tagalog — home Tagalog — was the intimacy language. Pure Filipino with minimal code-switching. It was the language of crew quarters, of meals eaten in the cramped galley, of complaints and jokes and the small moments of connection that made eighteen months in orbit bearable.

Sarah had been documenting the lexical innovations in Technical Tagalog. But what she was beginning to realize was that the three languages weren't just coexisting. They were pulling apart.

---

On her fifteenth day, Sarah interviewed Amihan Cruz in the observation module. Amihan was eight months into her first contract, young enough that her face still showed surprise at the Earth's curve through the viewport.

"How's the adjustment?" Sarah asked in English.

Amihan's shoulders tensed slightly. "*Okay naman*," she said.

"Can we talk about the technical vocabulary?"

"*Ah.*" Amihan looked down at her hands. "Yes. The technical vocabulary is... challenging."

"What's challenging about it?"

"*May mga salita na hindi ko pa alam.*" There are words I don't know yet. Amihan caught herself, switched back to English. "The terms change very fast. Maya and Jun, they use new words every week."

"Do you use English when you're working?"

Amihan's face flushed. "*Minsan*. Sometimes. I know it sounds... *ang formal*." Too formal. "But I don't want to use the wrong Technical Tagalog word and sound stupid."

"What do the others think when you use English?"

"*Hindi ko alam*." I don't know. Amihan looked out the viewport. "Maybe they think I'm trying to sound... *importante*. Important. But I'm not. I just don't know the right words yet."

---

The mistranslation incident happened on day twenty-three.

Sarah was in the command deck when the alarm sounded. Array Delta was showing signal degradation. Rafael pulled up the diagnostic readout.

Maya's voice came through the comm from Ring C. "*May problema sa delta array. Ang kwantum na bituin ay may —*" She paused, searching for the term. "*Pagkasira ng tinig.*"

Rafael's hand hovered over the response button.

"Repeat that," he said in English. "What kind of signal degradation?"

"*Ang bituin ay —*" Maya stopped. Switched to Technical Tagalog. "*Pagkasira ng tinig. Ang signal ay nawawala nang unti-unti.*"

Rafael typed into the console, pulling up the manual. Sarah could see him scanning for the Institutional English equivalent. The seconds stretched out.

"Is it node decay or entanglement drift?" Rafael asked.

Maya's frustration came through the comm clearly. "*Hindi ko alam kung paano sabihin sa English. Tingnan mo lang ang readout.*"

Rafael pulled up the data stream. His face went pale. "How long has it been degrading?"

"*Sampung minuto.*"

Ten minutes. The array had been failing for ten minutes while they argued about terminology.

Rafael slammed his hand on the emergency protocol button. "All crews, array delta offline. Rerouting traffic to gamma and epsilon. Maya, full shutdown procedure, now."

Sarah watched the signal graphs flatline as Maya cut power to the array. The rerouting took six minutes. Six minutes of dead air across sixty percent of Earth's interplanetary data traffic.

When it was over, Rafael sat back in his chair and pressed his palms against his eyes.

"What happened?" Sarah asked quietly.

"*Pagkasira ng tinig*," Rafael said. "Voice damage. Signal damage. It's a Technical Tagalog term for when the entanglement coherence starts to degrade but the node itself is still functional. It's not in the manual because the manual is from 2061 and we didn't understand entanglement degradation patterns until 2075."

"So there's no Institutional English term for it."

"There's 'signal degradation,' but that's too broad. It could mean anything. Maya was trying to tell me it was *pagkasira ng tinig* specifically, but I didn't —" He stopped. "I didn't recognize the term fast enough."

---

The next morning, Sarah found Maya in the Ring C galley, eating breakfast alone. She looked like she hadn't slept.

"*Pwede ba akong maupo?*" Sarah asked.

Maya gestured to the bench across from her.

"I wanted to ask about yesterday," Sarah said carefully. "*Ang pagkasira ng tinig.*"

Maya's jaw tightened. "*Alam mo ba kung ano ang nangyari pagkatapos?*" Do you know what happened after?

"Rafael filed the incident report."

"*Oo.*" Yes. "*At sinabi niya sa Earth-side na 'node malfunction.' Hindi 'pagkasira ng tinig.' Hindi nila maintindihan kung gaano kaseryoso.*"

Sarah pulled out her recording device. "Can I record this?"

Maya looked at the device, then at Sarah. "*Para sa research mo?*"

"Yes. But also because I think it matters."

Maya shrugged. "*Sige.*"

Sarah pressed record. "Tell me about *pagkasira ng tinig*. When did you start using that term?"

"*Mga tatlong buwan na*." About three months ago. "*Nakita ko ang pattern sa readouts. Ang signal ay hindi nawawala nang bigla. Unti-unti. Parang namamatay ang tinig.*"

"And you needed a term for it."

"*Oo. Kailangan namin ng salita para sa bagay na iyon.*" Yes. We needed a word for that thing. "*Kaya ginawa ko ang 'pagkasira ng tinig.' Ginamit ko ito kasama sina Jun at ang iba pang technicians. Naintindihan nila.*"

"But Rafael didn't."

Maya's hands clenched around her fork. "*Si Rafael ay nasa Ring B. Siya ay supervisor. Hindi niya naririnig ang mga salitang ginagamit namin sa Ring C.*"

"So when you needed to communicate during the emergency—"

"*Wala siyang ideya kung ano ang sinasabi ko.*" He had no idea what I was saying. Maya pushed her plate away. "*Pagod na pagod na ako sa pagsasalin.*" I'm so tired of translating.

"What would help?" Sarah asked.

Maya looked at her for a long moment. "*Magtanong ka kay Rafael. Siya ang tulay.*" Ask Rafael. He's the bridge.

---

Sarah found Rafael in his cabin that afternoon. He was reviewing maintenance logs, translating Technical Tagalog terms into Institutional English for the monthly report.

"Can we talk about the language problem?" Sarah asked.

Rafael looked up. "There's no language problem. The system works."

"Yesterday's incident—"

"Was an operational error. I should have recognized the term faster."

"But you couldn't recognize it because you didn't know it."

Rafael closed his screen. "Dr. Okonkwo. I've been on this station for four years. I've translated thousands of maintenance reports. The system works."

Sarah pulled up her data on her tablet. "Three months ago, Technical Tagalog was adding an average of four new terms per week. Last month it was six. This month it's nine. The drift is accelerating."

Rafael stared at the graph. "So what? Languages change."

"But you're the bridge between the languages. And the gap is getting wider."

He rubbed his temples.

"What do you want me to say?" Rafael asked quietly. "That I'm losing words? That there are Technical Tagalog terms I knew six months ago and now I can't remember the English? That every month it takes me longer to find the right institutional term?"

"Is that what's happening?"

Rafael looked at her. "Yes," he said. "That's what's happening."

"Have you told Earth-side management?"

Rafael laughed, but there was no humor in it. "What would I tell them? That the crews are inventing too many words? They'd say: enforce Institutional English as the operational language. Make everyone use the manual terms." He shook his head. "But we can't. The manual is from 2061. It doesn't have terms for half the things we deal with up here. Technical Tagalog exists because we need it."

"So you're trapped."

"We're all trapped," Rafael said. "The crews need Technical Tagalog to do their work. I need Institutional English to communicate with Earth-side. And the gap keeps getting wider, and I'm the only one trying to hold them together, and I can't—"

He stopped. Switched to Filipino. "*Hindi ko na kaya.*"

Sarah sat with that for a moment. Then she asked: "What if you didn't have to hold them together alone?"

---

Sarah spent the next two weeks interviewing crew members across all three shifts. She documented code-switching patterns, lexical innovations, mistranslation incidents. She mapped the semantic drift.

What emerged was a picture of a system under increasing strain.

The older crew members had built fluency in Technical Tagalog over years. They could navigate the constant lexical change because they'd been present for the evolution.

The newer crew members were learning Technical Tagalog as a foreign language, but the language kept changing before they could master it. They overcompensated by using Institutional English, which made them sound formal and awkward.

The supervisors were exhausted from constant translation labor. They spent hours every week converting Technical Tagalog into Institutional English, knowing that each translation lost precision, knowing that the gap was widening.

And Earth-side management, reading the incident reports in Institutional English, had no idea how serious the communication problem was becoming.

Sarah compiled her findings into a presentation. She titled it "The Lexicon Problem: Semantic Drift and Communication Failure on Kepler-7."

---

She gave the presentation in the command deck on day forty-two. The entire gamma shift attended. Rafael came, along with two other shift supervisors. Even Commander Chen showed up.

Sarah pulled up a graph showing lexical innovation rate over the past three years.

"Technical Tagalog is changing faster than it ever has before," she said. "Three years ago, crews were adding about two new terms per week. Now it's nine."

She pulled up the next slide: a timeline of mistranslation incidents.

"Six months ago, there was one mistranslation incident every three months. Now there's one every two weeks."

She pulled up the next slide: archived maintenance logs from 2086-2087.

"That crew had a lower mistranslation incident rate. Their lexical innovation rate was also lower — about four new terms per week instead of nine."

She zoomed in on a note in the archived files. "They had an informal glossary. A shared document where they recorded new Technical Tagalog terms along with their Institutional English equivalents. It was maintained by a technician named Luis Mendoza."

Maya sat up. "*Bakit hindi nila sinabi sa atin?*"

"The glossary wasn't official," Sarah said. "It wasn't part of the station protocols. When that crew rotated out, the document stayed in the archives."

Rafael was staring at the screen. "So the drift is manageable."

"Yes," Sarah said.

---

The meeting ran for two hours. Maya wanted a comprehensive glossary. Rafael wanted Earth-side management buy-in. Amihan wanted learning support. Commander Chen said: "Earth-side won't approve this. They'll say it's not in the protocols."

"Then we do it anyway," Maya said. "*Ginawa ito ni Luis. Kaya natin din.*"

Rafael shook his head. "If we create an unofficial glossary and Earth-side finds out, they'll say we're not following procedures."

"We're already losing them," Jun said. "Every mistranslation incident costs us bonus points. The array went offline for six minutes last month. That cost us three months of bonuses."

Finally, Rafael said: "What if we pilot it? Run it for three months. See if the mistranslation rate goes down. If it works, I'll take the data to Earth-side."

Maya looked at Amihan, then at Jun. "*Okay*," she said. "Three months."

---

Sarah spent the next two weeks documenting Technical Tagalog terms. She recorded Maya and Jun during their shifts, noting every innovation, every coinage. She interviewed technicians across all three shifts.

She compiled everything into a shared document: fifty terms with Technical Tagalog definitions and Institutional English translations.

*Bituin na patay* (dead star): Catastrophic quantum node failure. Coined by Maya Reyes, 2089-03-15.

*Pagkasira ng tinig* (voice damage): Progressive signal degradation. Requires node replacement before complete failure. Coined by Maya Reyes, 2089-02-20.

*Nag-aalab na bituin* (burning star): Overheating quantum node. Requires immediate cooling system intervention. Coined by Jun Santos, 2088-11-03.

She showed the glossary to Maya first. They sat in the Ring C galley, Sarah's tablet between them.

Maya scrolled through the entries slowly. Her finger paused on *bituin na patay*.

"*Ginawa mo ito*," she said quietly.

"You made it," Sarah said. "I just wrote it down."

Maya looked up. Her eyes were wet. "*Alam mo, minsan naiisip ko na walang silbi ang mga salitang ginagawa ko.*"

"They don't disappear," Sarah said. "They're real. They matter."

Maya pressed her palm against the screen. Then her face changed. Something tightened around her mouth.

"*Pero ngayon kailangan kong i-update ito bawat buwan, hindi ba?*" But now I have to update this every month, don't I? "*Isa pang trabaho.*"

Sarah hadn't thought about that.

"We could share the work," Sarah said. "Rotate who updates it."

Maya shook her head. "*Hindi. Ako ang gumagawa ng karamihan sa mga salita. Ako ang dapat mag-document.*" She looked back at the screen. "*Pero natatakot ako na —*" She stopped.

"Afraid of what?"

"*Na kapag nakasulat na, maaari nilang kunin.*" That once it's written down, they can take it. "*Ang Earth-side. Maaari nilang gamitin ang aming mga salita nang hindi kami binabayaran para dito.*"

"*Salamat*," Maya said finally.

---

The glossary went live on day sixty-eight. Sarah posted it to the station's internal network. Rafael added a note at the top: "Pilot program. Three-month evaluation period."

The first week, Sarah received fifteen suggested additions. The glossary grew to seventy terms, then ninety, then one hundred and twelve.

Amihan started using it as a learning tool. When Sarah asked how it was helping, Amihan said: "*Hindi na ako nag-iisa sa pag-aaral.*"

But two weeks later, Amihan came to Sarah's quarters. She looked frustrated.

"The glossary is already out of date," she said. "Maya used a term yesterday — *lumalaking bituin* — and it's not in the document."

Growing star. Sarah made a note.

"How fast is she making new terms?" Sarah asked.

"*Dalawa o tatlo bawat linggo*." Amihan sat down on Sarah's bunk. "I thought the glossary would help me catch up. But I'm still behind."

---

Rafael started referencing the glossary in his incident reports. Instead of translating *pagkasira ng tinig* as "signal degradation," he wrote: "Signal degradation (Technical Tagalog: *pagkasira ng tinig*; see station glossary for definition)."

The first time he did this, Earth-side management sent back a query.

Sarah was in the command deck when the message came through. Rafael opened it, read it, then closed his eyes.

"What is it?" Commander Chen asked.

Rafael turned the screen so she could see.

*Re: Incident Report K7-2089-0847*

*Supervisor Santos,*

*Your report references "Technical Tagalog" terminology not found in the Kepler-7 Operations Manual. Please revise using standard nomenclature per Section 4.2 of the Communications Protocol.*

*Dr. Patricia Alvarez*
*Operations Director, Orbital Infrastructure*

Rafael looked at Commander Chen. "They want me to stop using the glossary."

"They want you to stop referencing it in official reports," Chen said. "That's different."

"Is it?" Rafael pulled up the glossary on his screen. "We built this because the manual doesn't have the terms we need. If I can't reference it in reports, then what's the point?"

"The point is it helps crews communicate," Chen said.

"But Earth-side won't know what we're actually dealing with."

Commander Chen was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "Resubmit the report in Institutional English. But add an appendix. Call it 'Supplementary Technical Notes.' Include the Technical Tagalog terms there. Don't call it a glossary. Call it contextual clarification."

Rafael looked at her. "You think they'll accept that?"

"I think they'll ignore it," Chen said. "Which is what we need."

Rafael started typing. Sarah watched him work, watched him find the language that would satisfy both systems. It took him forty minutes to revise a report that should have taken ten.

When he was done, he leaned back in his chair.

"This is what I do now," he said quietly. "Every report. Every shift handover. Find the approved term that's close enough. Add the appendix that explains what's really happening. Hope someone Earth-side actually reads it."

"Do they?" Sarah asked.

Rafael shrugged. "*Hindi ko alam*."

---

The mistranslation incident rate dropped. Week one: three incidents. Week two: two incidents. Week three: one incident. Week four: zero incidents.

On day ninety-four, Rafael called a meeting.

The command deck was crowded. All three shift supervisors attended, along with technicians from each shift. Commander Chen sat in the front row.

Rafael pulled up a comparison graph: mistranslation incidents before and after the glossary implementation.

"The data is clear," he said. "The glossary works. Mistranslation incidents are down seventy-three percent. Translation time for incident reports is down forty percent."

He looked at Commander Chen. "I want to submit this to Earth-side for official recognition. Make the glossary part of station protocols."

Commander Chen was quiet for a long moment. Then she said: "Earth-side will want to control it. They'll want to standardize the terms. Lock them down. Stop the drift completely."

"That won't work," Maya said. "*Ang drift ay hindi mapipigilan. Kailangan lang natin ng paraan para ma-track ito.*"

"So we propose a living document," Sarah said. "Updated monthly. New terms added as they emerge. The glossary evolves with the language."

"Who maintains it?" Commander Chen asked.

Sarah looked at Maya. "The crews. It's their language."

Rafael nodded. "I'll coordinate with Earth-side. But the Technical Tagalog side belongs to the crews."

"And when Earth-side says no?" Commander Chen asked. "When they say the glossary needs to be reviewed and approved by a committee? What then?"

The room was quiet.

Finally, Maya said: "*Ginagawa natin ito pa rin.*" We do it anyway. "*Tulad ng ginawa ni Luis.*"

"Luis's glossary wasn't official," Chen said. "It stayed in the archives. It didn't change anything."

"It changed things for his crew," Jun said. "They had lower mistranslation rates. That mattered."

"It mattered for eighteen months," Chen said. "Then they rotated out and the next crew didn't know the glossary existed. If we want this to last, it needs to be official."

Rafael rubbed his temples. "Commander, with respect — we've been trying to get Earth-side to update the manual for years. The glossary isn't ideal. But it's what we have."

Chen looked at the graph on the screen. Seventy-three percent reduction in mistranslation incidents.

"Three more months," she said finally. "If the mistranslation rate stays down, I'll submit it to Earth-side with a recommendation for adoption. But I can't promise they'll approve it."

---

Sarah's contract was supposed to end on day one hundred and eighty. But three weeks before her departure date, she received a message from Dr. Alvarez.

*Dr. Okonkwo,*

*We understand you've been conducting linguistic research on Kepler-7. We'd like to schedule a videoconference to discuss your findings.*

*Please confirm your availability for a call at 14:00 station time on 2089-06-15.*

*Dr. Patricia Alvarez*

Sarah showed the message to Rafael. He read it twice.

"They want to know about the glossary," he said.

"Yes."

"What are you going to tell them?"

Sarah thought about Maya's fear that Earth-side would take the words without paying for them. She thought about Rafael's exhaustion from translation labor. She thought about Amihan still struggling to catch up.

"I'm going to tell them the truth," she said. "That the drift is real. That the glossary helps. That the crews need Technical Tagalog to do their work."

"And when they ask if it should be official?"

"I'll tell them that's not my decision to make."

Rafael looked at her. "But you have an opinion."

"Yes," Sarah said.

---

The videoconference took place in the command deck. Sarah sat alone at the console, Commander Chen and Rafael standing just off-camera. The connection was delayed by six seconds — the light-speed lag between Kepler-7 and Earth.

Dr. Alvarez appeared on screen. She was younger than Sarah had expected, maybe forty, with the particular exhaustion of someone who managed too many orbital stations with too few resources.

"Dr. Okonkwo," Alvarez said. "Thank you for making time for this call."

Six-second pause.

"Of course," Sarah said.

Pause.

"I've been reviewing the incident reports from Kepler-7," Alvarez said. "Supervisor Santos has been including references to Technical Tagalog terminology. I understand you've been documenting this language evolution."

Pause.

"Yes," Sarah said. "I've been mapping lexical innovation patterns and their impact on operational coordination."

Pause.

"And you've created some kind of glossary?"

"The crew created it," Sarah said. "I documented it."

Pause.

Alvarez's face was carefully neutral. "We have protocols for technical terminology. All terms used in official communications must be approved. This informal documentation system creates problems for standardization."

Pause.

Sarah chose her words carefully. "The manual is from 2061. It doesn't include terminology for operational problems that weren't understood until later. The crews have been coining new terms to describe phenomena they encounter regularly."

Pause.

"But the terms aren't standardized across stations," Alvarez said. "Kepler-7 uses 'pagkasira ng tinig' for progressive signal degradation. Kepler-3 uses 'signal fade.' Kepler-9 uses 'coherence decay.' If every station invents its own vocabulary, we can't compare data."

Pause.

Sarah glanced at Rafael.

"That's true," Sarah said. "But the alternative is using imprecise terminology that loses operational meaning in translation. When Supervisor Santos writes 'signal degradation' in a report, you don't know if he means catastrophic failure or progressive coherence loss."

Pause.

"So you're suggesting we should adopt Technical Tagalog as an official operational language?" Alvarez's tone was skeptical.

Pause.

"I'm suggesting that the gap between institutional terminology and operational reality creates safety risks," Sarah said. "The glossary is one way to bridge that gap."

Pause.

Alvarez was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "Dr. Okonkwo, I'm going to be direct with you. We have forty-seven people on Kepler-7. We have ninety-three on Kepler-3. We have one hundred and twenty on Kepler-9. If every station develops its own technical language, we lose the ability to coordinate across facilities. We lose standardization, which is what keeps people alive in orbit."

Pause.

"I understand," Sarah said. "But right now, Kepler-7 crews are inventing new terms every week because they need them. The drift is happening whether you approve it or not. The question is whether you want visibility into it."

Pause.

Alvarez leaned back in her chair. "What would visibility look like?"

Pause.

"The glossary stays informal but recognized," Sarah said. "Crews maintain it. Supervisors reference it in appendices. You don't have to approve every term, but you acknowledge that Technical Tagalog exists and serves an operational function."

Pause.

"And what happens when the next crew rotates in and doesn't speak Technical Tagalog?"

Pause.

"That's already happening," Sarah said. "New crew members struggle to learn a language that changes faster than they can master it. The glossary helps, but it's not a complete solution. You might need to adjust rotation schedules. Or provide language training. Or accept that there's a learning curve."

Pause.

Alvarez rubbed her forehead.

"This is complicated," Alvarez said.

Pause.

"Yes," Sarah said.

Pause.

"I need to think about this," Alvarez said. "I'll review your research documentation and discuss with the other operations directors. We'll make a decision about the glossary within sixty days."

Pause.

"Understood," Sarah said.

Pause.

The screen went dark.

---

Rafael and Commander Chen came back into frame.

"Well?" Chen asked.

"She's not saying no," Sarah said. "But she's not saying yes either."

Rafael sat down at the console. "Sixty days. That's after your contract ends."

"Yes."

"So you won't know what they decide."

Sarah looked at him. "Will you tell me? When they decide?"

Rafael nodded. "Yes."

---

Sarah spent her last three weeks on Kepler-7 finishing her documentation. She recorded final interviews with crew members, compiled her lexical innovation data, wrote up her analysis.

On her last day, Maya found her in the observation module.

"*Aalis ka na*," Maya said.

"Yes."

They stood together at the viewport, watching the Earth turn below them.

"*Salamat sa ginawa mo*," Maya said.

"I just documented what was already there."

Maya shook her head. "*Hindi. Binigyan mo ng pangalan ang problema. Pinapakita mo sa amin na kaya nating ayusin ito.*"

Sarah thought about the glossary, now up to one hundred and forty-seven terms. Thought about the monthly update meetings where crews argued about definitions, negotiated translations.

"The drift will continue," she said.

"*Okay lang*," Maya said. "*Hindi namin kailangan ng perpekto. Kailangan lang namin ng sapat.*"

They stood in silence for a while. Then Maya said: "*Ang research mo — ano ang isusulat mo?*"

Sarah had been thinking about this for weeks. Her original research question had been about creole formation. But what she'd found on Kepler-7 was something different. Not a single creole emerging from language contact, but three languages coexisting in deliberate separation, each serving a distinct social function, the boundaries between them carefully maintained even as they drifted apart.

"I'll write about the glossary," Sarah said. "About collective semantic stabilization. About how crews negotiate coordination when the language keeps changing."

"*Maiintindihan ba nila?*"

"I don't know," Sarah said honestly. "I'll try."

Maya smiled. "*Ganyan din ang ginagawa natin araw-araw*."

Sarah looked at Maya. Really looked at her. Saw the exhaustion around her eyes, the way her shoulders carried the weight of being the station's primary linguistic innovator. Saw the pride in what she'd built and the fear of what it might cost her.

"Will you keep updating the glossary?" Sarah asked. "After I leave?"

Maya was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "*Oo. Pero hindi ko alam kung gaano katagal.*" Yes. But I don't know for how long. "*Pagod na ako. At ang susunod na crew — paano kung hindi nila maintindihan kung bakit mahalaga ito?*"

"Then you teach them," Sarah said. "Like you taught Amihan. Like you taught me."

Maya looked out the viewport. "*At kung ayaw nilang matuto?*"

Sarah didn't have an answer for that.

---

Sarah's paper was published eight months later in the *Journal of Linguistic Anthropology*. She titled it "Negotiated Stability: Semantic Drift and Collective Coordination in Orbital Technical Creoles."

The abstract read: "This study examines language evolution in Kepler-7 orbital station, where a three-language system enables operational coordination despite accelerating semantic drift. Data suggests that collective documentation protocols can make drift visible without eliminating innovation, creating a negotiated equilibrium between linguistic change and communicative function. However, this equilibrium requires ongoing maintenance labor and remains vulnerable to institutional resistance and crew rotation patterns."

The paper was cited forty-three times in the first year. Two other orbital stations implemented similar glossary systems. Earth-side management quietly updated the protocols to include "station-specific technical vocabulary documentation" as an acceptable practice — but only as appendices to official reports, and only if supervisors continued to use approved terminology in primary documentation.

Sarah received an email from Rafael six months after the policy change.

*Dr. Okonkwo,*

*The glossary is still running. We're up to 203 terms now. Earth-side accepts the appendices but won't make the glossary official. Commander Chen says that's probably the best we're going to get.*

*Maya is still maintaining it, but she's talking about not renewing her contract. She says she's tired of inventing words that Earth-side doesn't want to acknowledge. Jun is trying to convince her to stay for one more rotation, but I don't know if she will.*

*Amihan is more fluent now. She's started coining her own terms. Last week she invented "tumatakbong bituin" (running star) for a node that's failing but still routing traffic. It's a good term. We added it to the glossary.*

*The drift is still happening. We're still translating. The gap is still there.*

*But we're managing it.*

*Rafael*

Sarah read the email three times. Then she filed it with her research documentation.
