# REVISED STORY

## Bay 12

The air recycler hums like breathing through damaged lungs. I run my fingers along the weld seam in the dark—lighting panels flicker again, third time this shift—and feel where titanium meets aluminum. Point-zero-two millimeters. Within tolerance.

*Gongyi bu shi guize, shi ni de ming.* I whisper it to no one.

The boy used to answer. Three months ago, before his parents filed the transfer request, Chen would laugh. *"Shifu, ni shi shiren."* Now Bay 12 is quiet except for the recycler and my breathing.

I file the quality report in English: "Module 7-449-B meets structural specifications." The workstation screen glows blue. My rating drops another point.

*Xiōngshǒu.* The word follows me through the station like a smell.

---

Chen watches from the observation port as Yao checks another weld. The old man's hands move with absolute certainty, fingers reading the metal like Braille. Chen has seen Liu's fabricators work—they trust the automated scanners, the computational overlays highlighting stress points in green and red. Yao works by touch in near-darkness because Bay 12's equipment is twenty-two years old and no one authorizes upgrades for fabricators with declining ratings.

The module Yao just finished will pass inspection. Chen knows this the way he knows his own pulse. He's watched Yao work for four years, since he was fifteen. Every weld within point-zero-two millimeters. Every seal holding three atmospheres. Every joint surviving thermal cycling that would crack lesser work.

But the numbers say otherwise.

Chen's parents spoke to him in English this morning. "The transfer is finalized. You report to Bay 4 tomorrow. Master Liu expects you at 0600."

Chen had wanted to say *"Yao de gongzuo shi hao de"* but the words wouldn't cross from Cant to English. Instead: "I understand the reasoning."

His mother nodded. "We're protecting you. The statistics are clear."

---

The module fails inspection.

Not my weld—I check the report three times. Atmospheric processor housing, Section 7-449-B. Seal integrity compromised. But I welded the structural joints, not the seal. That's a different fabricator's work, someone in Bay 6 whose name I don't know.

Doesn't matter. The module carries my signature.

I want to file an appeal but the words stick. *Who would believe the jinxed fabricator?* I save the appeal as a draft and delete it.

The mess hall is emptier than usual during second meal. I take my tray to the corner where the lighting is worst. Eat alone. Chen used to sit across from me, showing me problems from his materials science homework, asking questions in Cant. *"Shifu, weishenme zhege hejin zai zheyang de wendu xia hui liekai?"*

Now he sits with his parents, speaking English.

---

Chen's new workstation in Bay 4 has lighting so bright it makes his eyes water. The air is warm. Liu's three other apprentices work in synchronized rhythm. The automated scanner projects green approval markers over Chen's first weld.

"Good," Liu says in Cant. "You're quick. Your old master taught you the basics well enough."

Chen wants to say *"Ta jiao wo yiqie"* but Liu has already moved to the next station. Chen checks the weld by hand anyway, running his fingers along the seam the way Yao showed him. Point-zero-one millimeters.

The scanner already confirmed it. No one else checks by hand.

At evening meal, Chen sits with his parents. His mother speaks in Cant now. *"Ni xihuan xin de shifu ma?"*

"Liu's methods are efficient," Chen says in English.

His father frowns. "You can speak Cant with us. This is family time."

But Chen can't find the words in Cant for what Liu's bay feels like. So he says in English, "I'm still adjusting to the new procedures."

His mother reaches across the table, squeezes his hand. "You'll be safer here. That's what matters."

---

I work alone for six days. The assignments are low-priority—waste processor components, non-critical hull patches, equipment nobody cares about. I check every weld three times. File reports in English that no one reads.

On the seventh day, Bay 12's main fabrication unit fails. The error code means bearing degradation, a mechanical problem that will take three days to fix if maintenance prioritizes it. They won't.

I file the maintenance request and sit in the dark, listening to the recycler's damaged breathing.

Chen appears in the doorway. I know his silhouette—the way he tilts his head when he's uncertain, the way his hands hang loose like he's ready to catch something falling.

"Shifu," he says in Cant.

"You should be in Bay 4."

"Liu gave us long meal. I brought you food." He holds out a container. Real food, not recycled protein. His family's allocation.

I should refuse. Should tell him to go back to his bright bay with its working equipment and high-rated fabricators.

Instead I take the container. "Xie xie."

He sits on the floor beside my workstation, back against the wall, and we eat in silence. The recycler hums. The lights flicker. Chen's breathing is steady and young and certain.

"The module that failed inspection," he says finally. "I checked the records. The seal was fabricated by Zhang in Bay 6. Not your work."

"System doesn't care whose work it was. Module had my signature."

"The system is wrong."

I almost laugh. "System works fine for everyone else. Your parents made the right choice."

"Did they?" He's looking at me in the dimness. "Liu's fabricators don't check by hand. They trust the scanners. What happens when the scanners fail?"

"Scanners don't fail in Bay 4."

"Everything fails eventually." He says it in Cant, and the words carry weight they wouldn't have in English. *Suoyou de dongxi zuizhong dou hui shibai.*

I set down the empty container. "You should go back. Long meal is ending."

He stands, but doesn't leave immediately. "Tomorrow is my rest day. Can I come help with the maintenance request? The bearing replacement?"

"Maintenance won't approve my request for three days. Maybe longer."

"Then I'll help you work around it. We can recalibrate the unit to compensate for the bearing degradation. It won't be permanent, but it'll buy time."

"Your parents—"

"I'll tell them I'm studying at the library." He says it in English. Then switches back to Cant: *"Wo hui lai."*

He leaves before I can refuse.

---

Chen's parents are arguing in their quarters when he returns. He hears them through the door—his mother's voice tight, his father's lower and more measured. Both speaking English.

"—checking the records. Looking at Bay 12's maintenance requests."

"He's curious. That's good for an apprentice."

"It's loyalty to the wrong person. We moved him for a reason. Yao's ratings are collapsing. The last thing Chen needs is to be associated with a jinxed fabricator."

Chen stands outside the door, hand on the panel, not entering.

His father: "Yao taught him well. You said so yourself."

"That was before the numbers proved otherwise."

"Pattern of bad luck or pattern of bad ratings? They're not the same thing."

"They're the same thing when everyone else sees them as the same thing. We're protecting our son. That's our duty."

Chen opens the door. Both parents turn, and his mother's expression shifts immediately. She switches to Cant: *"Haizi, ni chile ma?"*

"Yes," Chen says in English. "I ate with Liu's other apprentices. We discussed advanced welding techniques."

The lie sits in his mouth like metal. His parents nod, and his mother starts talking about tomorrow's schedule, about the family's ration allocation for next week, about ordinary things.

Chen listens and responds and feels the distance growing.

---

The boy comes back.

I'm working on the recalibration when I hear footsteps—his footsteps, lighter than mine, quicker. He's carrying tools from somewhere, good tools, newer than anything in Bay 12.

"Borrowed these from Bay 4's equipment locker," he says in Cant. "Liu won't notice until next shift."

"You'll get in trouble."

"Only if the recalibration fails." He kneels beside the fabrication unit, already analyzing the bearing assembly. "Show me what you've tried."

We work for three hours. Chen's hands are steadier than mine, faster, but he checks with me before each adjustment. *"Zheyang dui ma?"* And I guide him through the compensation calculations, the thermal load redistribution, the improvised fixes you learn when you work with failing equipment for two decades.

The unit comes back online. Not perfect—there's a grinding sound in the bearing that will get worse—but functional.

"That'll hold for two weeks," Chen says. "Maybe three if you keep the load light."

"Maintenance will have replaced it by then."

We both know that's not true.

He starts to pack up the borrowed tools, then stops. "Shifu, why don't you organize? There are other fabricators with low ratings. If you pooled resources, shared equipment, the system couldn't isolate you."

I've thought about it. Late at night when Bay 12 is empty and the station's rotation makes the hull creak, I've imagined it—ten fabricators with declining ratings forming a cooperative, proving the system wrong through collective action.

But I say, "System works. I'm the problem."

"You don't believe that."

"What I believe doesn't matter. What everyone else believes is the system."

Chen is quiet for a long time. Then he says in Cant, very softly: *"Wo xiangxin ni."*

The words hang in the recycled air of Bay 12, and I don't know what to do with them.

---

The next module passes inspection. And the next. Chen comes back on his rest days. We don't talk much—just work, checking welds in the dimness, recalibrating equipment that should have been replaced years ago, maintaining discipline when no one is watching.

His parents don't know. Or maybe they do and they're choosing not to see it, because Chen's work in Bay 4 is exemplary and his ratings are rising.

On the fifteenth day, Bay 4's main scanner fails during a critical assembly. Power surge, equipment malfunction, the kind of random failure that happens in orbit when you're dependent on technology designed for planetary gravity. Liu's apprentices stop work immediately—they don't know how to proceed without the green approval markers, without the computational overlay telling them where to weld.

Chen checks the joint by hand. Finds the stress point by touch, the way I taught him. Completes the weld while the other apprentices wait for the scanner to reboot.

Liu files a commendation for Chen's initiative.

---

Chen sits in his family's quarters, listening to his parents celebrate in Cant. *"Wo wei ni jiaoao!"* His mother's voice is warm. "Liu says you have instincts the other apprentices lack. Natural talent."

"It's not natural," Chen says. "Yao taught me to work without scanners."

The warmth in the room cools. His father sets down his cup. "You're still visiting Bay 12."

Not a question.

"On my rest days. To help with maintenance."

His mother's face tightens. "We moved you to protect you from that association."

"The association is why I knew what to do when Bay 4's scanner failed." Chen keeps his voice level. "Yao's methods work. His ratings are wrong."

"The ratings aggregate everyone's judgment. That's how the system maintains quality. One person's opinion doesn't override statistical evidence."

"What if the statistics are measuring the wrong thing?"

His parents exchange a glance. His mother speaks, and her voice is gentle but immovable: "You will stop visiting Bay 12. This is not negotiable. We are your parents. This is our duty."

Chen wants to argue. Wants to explain that Yao's isolation is the system's failure, not Yao's. That the old man maintains discipline that younger fabricators have forgotten, that his work is sound even when his ratings aren't.

But his parents are speaking English, and Chen is nineteen and economically dependent and living in quarters his parents control.

So he says, "I understand."

And he does understand. His parents love him. They're protecting him the only way they know how. The system works for them—has worked for them for twenty years—and they can't see what Chen sees because they're not trapped in Bay 12's darkness, not watching a craftsman maintain discipline when everyone has decided he's jinxed.

That night, Chen lies in his hammock and counts his own breathing.

---

I notice Chen's absence on the third rest day. The borrowed tools don't appear. The boy doesn't come.

I tell myself it's better this way. He has a future in Bay 4, with Liu's high ratings and bright lights and scanners that tell you what to do. He doesn't need an old fabricator in a failing bay, doesn't need the *xiōngshǒu* label following him through the station.

But Bay 12 is quieter without him. The recycler's breathing sounds more labored. The lights flicker more frequently. My hands shake slightly when I check welds alone.

On the fourth week, I receive a priority assignment—deep-space vessel hull segment, critical path work, the kind of assignment I haven't seen in months. The work order includes a note: "Fabricator Yao's recent modules have passed inspection consistently. Reassigning to priority queue for evaluation."

Someone looked at the numbers. Someone noticed.

I read the work order three times, hands steady, and then I start the fabrication. Hull segment, titanium-aluminum composite, tolerances so tight that a point-zero-three deviation could kill the crew.

The kind of work I was born to do.

---

Chen hears about the assignment in Bay 4. Liu mentions it casually—"They're giving Yao a test piece, seeing if his recent improvement is real or statistical noise"—and Chen's hands freeze on his current weld.

"When?"

"He started this morning. Results due in three days."

Chen finishes his shift mechanically, his mind in Bay 12. Yao working alone on critical-path work, no peer review, no backup, just the old man and his discipline and the system watching to see if he fails.

At evening meal, Chen's parents talk about station politics, about ration allocations, about ordinary things. Chen eats and nods and calculates.

After meal, he tells his parents he's going to the library. They nod, distracted. Chen walks past the library entrance, past the bright corridors of the newer bays, down to Bay 12 where the lights flicker and the air is cold.

Yao is checking a weld joint by hand, fingers moving with absolute certainty in the dimness. He doesn't look up when Chen enters.

"Thought you weren't coming back," Yao says in Cant.

"I wasn't." Chen moves to the work bench, looks at the hull segment taking shape. "My parents forbade it. Said it was their duty to protect me from bad associations."

"They're right."

"They're afraid." Chen picks up a tool, tests its weight. "There's a difference."

Yao finally looks at him. The old man's face is shadowed, carved with exhaustion and something else—not hope, exactly, but the absence of surrender. "You should go back. This work is my test, not yours."

"I'm not here to help with the work. I'm here because—" Chen stops, searching for words that exist in Cant but not in English. "Because *gongyi* isn't just technique. It's transmission. And if you fail this test because you're working alone in a failing bay with no peer review, then what you taught me dies with your rating."

"What I taught you lives in Bay 4. In your hands."

"It lives in Bay 4 because I bring it there. But I learned it here." Chen sets down the tool. "I'm not asking to help. I'm just—" He switches to English: "I'm bearing witness."

Yao is quiet for a long time. Then he nods once and returns to his work.

Chen sits against the wall, back in the spot he occupied for four years, and watches the old man's hands move in the flickering light. He doesn't touch the work. Doesn't offer advice. Just watches and remembers and keeps count of Yao's breathing, steady and certain and alive.

---

The hull segment takes three days. I work sixteen-hour shifts, checking every weld by hand, every seal by pressure test, every joint by thermal cycling. Chen comes every evening, tells his parents he's studying, and sits in Bay 12's darkness while I work.

We don't talk much. Don't need to. He's learning something I can't teach directly—the difference between technique and discipline, between following procedures and maintaining craft when everything is failing around you.

On the third day, I file the completion report: "Hull segment 8-227-A meets all structural specifications. Ready for final inspection."

The inspection takes six hours. I wait in Bay 12, hands steady, breathing steady, while inspectors I've never met examine every weld under full illumination with equipment that works.

Chen waits with me. His parents think he's at the library.

The inspection report comes through at 2300 hours: "Hull segment 8-227-A approved for integration. Zero defects. Fabricator Yao's work meets critical-path standards."

Chen reads the report over my shoulder and says in Cant, very quietly: *"Wo yizhi zhidao."*

---

Chen's parents discover his deception on the fourth day. Someone from Bay 4 mentions seeing Chen near Bay 12, and his mother checks the library logs, finds the gaps.

They confront him in English. "You lied to us. You disobeyed a direct order. You risked your reputation to associate with a fabricator we explicitly forbade you to contact."

Chen stands in the family quarters, hands at his sides. "Yes."

His father's face is tight. "Why? We're protecting you. That's our duty as parents."

"I know." Chen keeps his voice steady. "And I love you for it. But I have duties too. Not just to you. To the work. To what I was taught."

His mother's anger breaks into something more complicated. *"Ni bu dong,"* she says in Cant, the words slipping out.

"I do understand," Chen says in English. "You see the system working. You see Liu's ratings, see his success, see the bright bay and the working equipment. And you're right—that system works for most people. But it doesn't work for everyone. And when it fails, someone has to remember what the system was supposed to measure in the first place."

His father sits down heavily. "You're nineteen years old. You don't have the experience to judge when the system is wrong."

"Maybe not. But I have the experience to know when someone's work is good. And Yao's work is good. The hull segment proved it."

"One data point doesn't override statistical trends."

"Neither does one fabricator's judgment. But Yao isn't alone anymore. I'm carrying forward what he taught me. And maybe that's not collective action in the way the system recognizes, but it's transmission. It's keeping the discipline alive when the ratings say it should die."

His parents are silent for a long time. Then his mother says, in Cant: *"Ni zheme jueding, women bu neng zuzhi ni."* "But we don't agree. And we won't protect you if this association damages your reputation."

"I know," Chen says. "That's the cost. I accept it."

---

I don't see Chen for a week after the hull segment approval. I assume his parents finally succeeded in keeping him away, assume he's made his choice and chosen the bright bay over the failing one.

I work alone. I receive more priority assignments—not critical-path work, but better than waste processors. The system is recalibrating, slowly, testing whether my improvement is real.

On the eighth day, Chen appears in Bay 12 during second shift. Not his rest day. Not evening. Middle of a regular work day when he should be in Bay 4.

"Filed for independent fabricator status," he says in Cant. "Liu approved it. I'm no longer his apprentice."

I set down my tools. "You're not old enough for independent status."

"I'm nineteen. That's the minimum age. And my ratings are high enough." He moves to the work bench, starts examining my current project—a life support module, medium priority. "I told my parents I'm moving to different quarters. Smaller allocation, but mine."

"They'll cut you off."

"Probably." He picks up a tool, tests a weld joint the way I taught him. "But I've been saving credit. I can manage for a while."

"Why?"

Chen looks at me in the flickering light of Bay 12, and his face is young and certain and completely without doubt. "Because someone has to remember what *gongyi* means. And because you're the best fabricator on this station, and everyone is too busy looking at your ratings to see your work."

"One person's opinion doesn't change the system."

"No. But one person's work might. Over time. If I keep doing what you taught me, keep checking by hand when the scanners say it's unnecessary, keep maintaining discipline when it's easier to trust the automation—maybe the next generation notices. Maybe they ask why. Maybe the system recalibrates again."

"That's not transformation. That's just—" I search for the word. "Transmission."

"Yes," Chen says. "That's exactly what it is."

He stays for the rest of the shift. We work side by side, checking each other's welds, maintaining discipline in a failing bay that no one else remembers. The recycler hums its damaged breathing. The lights flicker. Our hands move with certainty in the dimness.

And somewhere in Bay 4, Liu's apprentices are learning to trust the scanners, learning efficiency over precision, learning a different kind of discipline that will serve them well until the day the technology fails and no one remembers how to work by touch.

But here in Bay 12, in the cold and the flickering light, two fabricators maintain the old discipline. Not because the system rewards it. Not because the ratings recognize it.

But because the work demands it.

---

Three months later, Bay 4's climate control fails during a critical assembly. Temperature spike, forty degrees in fifteen minutes, the kind of thermal stress that makes metal expand unpredictably. Liu's apprentices stop work immediately—procedure says to abort during environmental anomalies, wait for conditions to stabilize.

Chen continues working. Compensates for the thermal expansion by touch, adjusts his technique in real-time, completes the assembly while the environmental systems reboot.

The work passes inspection. Zero defects.

Liu files another commendation. Mentions in his report that Chen's training under Fabricator Yao provided skills that newer teaching methods don't develop.

It's not transformation. The system still operates on ratings and reputation. The *xiōngshǒu* label still whispers through the station's corridors. Most fabricators still trust the scanners over their hands.

But in Bay 12, where the lights flicker and the recycler breathes its damaged rhythm, an old man works with steady hands. And in Bay 4, where the equipment gleams and the climate is controlled, a young fabricator checks welds by touch when no one is watching.

The discipline is transmitted. The seed is planted.

And somewhere in the future—maybe Chen's children, maybe their children—someone will ask why they check welds by hand when the scanners are faster. And someone will remember the old fabricator in Bay 12, the one who maintained discipline when the ratings said he was jinxed, the one who kept the work alive in the darkness.

Someone will remember.
