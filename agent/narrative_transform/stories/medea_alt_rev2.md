# THE LONG HALL

## Part One: The Quota

The Long Hall holds three hundred clerks at wooden desks arranged in fifteen rows of twenty. Each desk: two feet wide, three feet deep, stained dark from palm oil and ink. The hall runs 120 feet east to west, 80 feet north to south. Forty windows on the long walls, but the light never reaches the center rows. By midday the air is thick with the smell of bodies and wet paper.

Wei sits in row seven, position twelve. He has been here for eight months.

The morning quota is 847 characters. This is an increase from last month's 823, which was an increase from the month before. Wei does not remember when the quota was lower than 800, though he knows it must have been. When he arrived, his hands could manage the morning quota with time to spare. Now his right hand cramps by the fourth column.

The work is copying land registry documents from old records into new ledgers. The characters must be exact. A single error ruins the page, and ruined pages are deducted from the daily count. Three ruined pages in a week triggers a formal review. Five ruined pages means dismissal.

Wei's hand cramps at character 623. He flexes his fingers, continues. The brush feels heavier than it did an hour ago.

Around him, three hundred brushes scratch against paper. The sound is like rain on a tile roof, constant and directionless. In the center rows where the light is worst, some clerks lean close to their work, noses inches from the page. Wei can see the shadows under their eyes, the way their shoulders curve forward.

At the front of the hall, Supervisor Chen walks between the rows. He is forty-three, has worked in the archives for twenty years. He does not speak unless he sees an error. When he sees an error, he marks it in his record book and moves on.

Wei finishes character 847 as the morning bell sounds. His hand is shaking. He sets down his brush and looks at the page. The final column is uneven—the characters drift rightward as his hand tired. But they are legible. They are correct.

He has met the quota.

The afternoon quota is 735 characters. This is lower than the morning quota because the afternoon light is worse and the hands are tired. Wei knows he will meet it. He has always met it. But the space between his capacity and the requirement shrinks each month.

He does not know what happens when the space closes completely.

## Part Two: The Lending System

Wei sends half his wages home to his village. The other half pays for his room (shared with three other clerks), his food (two meals a day), and his brushes (which wear out faster now that his hand shakes).

In the fourth month, his father wrote that the rice harvest was poor. The family needed 20 taels to buy seed for the spring planting. Wei had 8 taels saved. He borrowed 12 taels from the Clerks' Benevolent Fund at 8% monthly interest.

The loan was due in six months. Wei calculated: if he sent nothing home and ate one meal a day, he could repay it in five months and avoid the compounding.

In the fifth month, the quota increased to 798 characters. Wei's daily output dropped because his hand cramped earlier. He was fined for three ruined pages. The fine was 2 taels.

In the sixth month, his mother fell ill. The family needed 15 taels for medicine. Wei borrowed again.

Now, in the eighth month, he owes 34 taels. The monthly interest is 2.7 taels. His wages are 12 taels per month. After room and food, he has 4 taels remaining. He sends 2 taels home. He pays 2 taels toward the interest.

The principal grows by 0.7 taels each month.

Wei has calculated this many times. The numbers do not change.

At night, in the room he shares with three other clerks, he lies awake and calculates different scenarios. If the quota does not increase next month. If his hand stops cramping. If his father has a good harvest and needs no money. If he eats every other day instead of every day.

The numbers still do not work.

One of his roommates is named Feng. Feng has been in the archives for two years. He owes 67 taels. His hands shake so badly that he wraps his right wrist with cloth to steady it. The cloth is stained dark with ink and sweat.

Feng says: "The fund is how they keep us. You can't leave while you owe. And you can't stop owing."

Wei says: "There must be a way."

Feng says: "Calculate it."

Wei has calculated it. Feng is right.

But Wei continues to calculate anyway, because calculating is different from accepting, and he is not ready to accept.

In his village, Wei's father farms three mu of rice paddy. In a good year, the harvest feeds the family and provides a small surplus to sell. In a bad year, they eat less and borrow seed. But the land is theirs. The work is theirs. When the rice grows, they see it growing.

Here, Wei copies characters that describe land he will never see, owned by families he will never meet. The characters accumulate in ledgers that are stored in rooms he is not permitted to enter. He does not know what happens to the ledgers after they are filled.

He knows only that the quota increases, and his hands cramp earlier, and the debt grows despite his payments.

This is what he left his village to escape: the uncertainty of harvests, the risk of hunger. He came to the capital for steady wages and the dignity of scholarly work.

He did not understand that steady wages could be consumed by steady debt, or that dignity could be measured in characters per hour.

Feng tells him: "Your hands will get worse. Everyone's do. When you can't meet the quota anymore, they'll dismiss you and call the debt. You'll go home with nothing and owe everything."

Wei says: "How long do I have?"

Feng says: "I don't know. For me it was eighteen months. For some it's faster."

Wei has been here eight months. His hand cramps at character 623. Last month it cramped at character 701.

He calculates: if the cramping advances at this rate, he has four months before he cannot meet the morning quota. If the quota increases again, he has less.

Four months to find a way out of 34 taels of debt that grows by 0.7 taels per month.

He lies awake and calculates. The numbers do not change.

## Part Three: The Mechanism

In the ninth month, a clerk in row four drops his brush during the morning quota. His name is Liu. He has been in the archives for three years. His hand is cramping and he drops the brush and it rolls off the desk onto the floor.

Liu bends to pick it up. When he straightens, his face is gray. He tries to continue but his hand will not grip the brush properly. The brush wavers. A drop of ink falls onto the page, spreading into the character he has just written.

The page is ruined.

Liu stares at it. Then he sets down the brush, stands, and walks out of the Long Hall.

Supervisor Chen watches him go. He makes a note in his record book. He does not follow.

Liu does not return after lunch. His desk remains empty for the afternoon quota.

The next morning, there is a new clerk at Liu's desk. The new clerk is young, perhaps nineteen. His hands are steady. He meets the morning quota with time to spare.

Wei learns later that Liu was dismissed for failure to meet quota. The Clerks' Benevolent Fund called his debt immediately. Liu owed 89 taels. His family's land was seized as payment. Liu returned to his village with nothing.

Wei thinks: That will be me in four months.

But he does not say this aloud, because saying it aloud would make it real in a different way than knowing it silently.

Instead he asks Feng: "Is there any way to pay off the debt faster?"

Feng says: "You can work overtime. Copy additional pages after the daily quota. They pay 0.3 taels per hundred characters."

Wei calculates: 100 characters takes approximately forty minutes when his hand is already tired from the daily quota. To earn 2.7 taels (one month's interest), he would need to copy 900 additional characters. That is six hours of overtime work. Per month.

Six hours per month is possible. But the quota increases each month, which means the daily work takes longer, which means less time for overtime. And his hand cramps earlier each month, which means the overtime work will take longer than forty minutes per hundred characters.

Wei calculates different scenarios. In all of them, the overtime earnings lag behind the debt growth within three months.

But three months is better than zero months.

He begins staying after the evening bell. The Long Hall empties except for a dozen clerks bent over their desks, copying by lamplight. The lamps are dim and the characters blur. Wei's hand cramps worse at night, but he continues.

He earns 0.9 taels the first week. He pays it toward the principal. The debt drops to 33.8 taels.

The next month, the quota increases to 823 characters. Wei's morning cramping starts at character 589. He still meets the quota, but it takes longer. He has less time for overtime.

He earns 0.6 taels the second week.

His hand begins to hurt even when he is not writing. A dull ache in the palm and wrist that wakes him at night. He wraps it like Feng does, but the cloth does not help.

In the third week, he ruins two pages during the morning quota. The characters drift and blur. He is fined 1.3 taels.

The overtime earnings are consumed by the fine. The debt grows to 34.2 taels.

Wei stops staying for overtime. The calculation no longer works.

He lies awake and thinks about Liu's empty desk, and the new clerk with steady hands, and his father's three mu of rice paddy that is no longer enough to secure against the debt.

He thinks: There must be a way.

But he can no longer imagine what it is.

## Part Four: The Observer

Chen has been a supervisor for six years. Before that he was a clerk for fourteen years. He remembers when the morning quota was 680 characters. He remembers when the Clerks' Benevolent Fund charged 5% monthly interest instead of 8%.

He remembers when his own hands were steady.

Chen's job is to ensure quota compliance. He walks the rows during work hours, checking for errors, marking deficiencies in his record book. At the end of each week, he submits a report to the Board of Archive Administration. The report lists each clerk's output, each error, each fine.

The Board reviews the reports and adjusts the quotas.

Chen does not set the quotas. He does not determine the fines. He observes and records. This is the nature of supervision.

But Chen is beginning to notice patterns that trouble him.

The quota has increased every month for the past eighteen months. The increases are small—between 2% and 4%—but they compound. A clerk who could comfortably meet the quota a year ago now struggles. A clerk who struggles now will fail within months.

The failure rate has increased. Last year, twelve clerks were dismissed for quota non-compliance. This year, twenty-three have been dismissed so far, and there are still three months remaining.

The dismissed clerks are replaced within days. The new clerks are younger, their hands steadier. They meet the quotas easily at first.

But within six months, they begin to struggle. Their hands cramp. They ruin pages. They borrow from the Benevolent Fund.

Chen sees this pattern repeat across three hundred desks.

He does not know what to do with this observation.

At the monthly Board meeting, Chen sits with five other supervisors and the Archive Director. The Director is sixty-one, has never worked as a clerk. He reviews the output reports and nods.

"Production is up 4% from last year," the Director says. "Excellent work."

Chen says: "The dismissal rate is also up."

The Director says: "That's expected. We're maintaining higher standards. The clerks who can't meet the standards are being replaced with clerks who can."

Chen says: "The new clerks meet the standards initially, but within six months they begin to decline."

The Director says: "Then we replace them as well. The archive requires a certain level of productivity. It's not our responsibility to accommodate individual limitations."

Chen does not argue. He has learned that arguing with the Director is unproductive.

But he continues to notice.

He notices that the clerks who work overtime decline faster than those who don't. He notices that the Benevolent Fund's loan volume has increased by 34% in the past year. He notices that the average tenure of a clerk has dropped from 2.4 years to 1.7 years.

He notices that the archive's total output has increased by 4%, but the number of clerks has remained constant at three hundred. This means each clerk is producing more, but each clerk is also failing faster.

Chen does not know what this means. He knows only that it troubles him.

One evening, after the quota bell, Chen remains in the Long Hall. A dozen clerks are working overtime, copying by lamplight. Chen watches them.

Their hands shake. Their shoulders curve forward. They lean close to the pages, squinting in the dim light.

One clerk—Chen recognizes him as Wei, row seven, position twelve—sets down his brush and flexes his hand. The hand is trembling. Wei stares at it for a long moment, then picks up the brush and continues.

Chen thinks: This is not sustainable.

But he does not know what "sustainable" would look like, or whether sustainability is the archive's concern.

He is a supervisor. His job is to observe and record.

He observes. He records.

The quotas continue to increase.

## Part Five: The Organizing Attempt

In the tenth month, a clerk named Lin begins talking to other clerks during the lunch break.

Lin has been in the archives for sixteen months. He owes 45 taels to the Benevolent Fund. His hands shake, but not as badly as some. He can still meet the quota, though the margin is shrinking.

Lin says to Wei and Feng and four other clerks: "The quota increases every month. Our hands get worse. The debt grows. This is not sustainable."

Wei says: "We know."

Lin says: "So what do we do?"

Feng says: "Nothing. There's nothing to do. You meet the quota until you can't, then you're dismissed."

Lin says: "What if we refuse to meet the quota?"

The other clerks stare at him.

Wei says: "They'll dismiss us."

Lin says: "They'll dismiss all of us? All three hundred?"

Feng says: "They'll dismiss enough to make an example. The rest will comply."

Lin says: "Not if we coordinate. Not if we all refuse together."

There is a long silence.

One of the other clerks—a woman named Mei who has been here for eleven months—says: "How would we coordinate? We can't talk during work hours. We have fifteen minutes for lunch."

Lin says: "We talk during lunch. We talk in the evenings. We pass the word carefully."

Wei says: "And then what? We refuse to work?"

Lin says: "We work, but we work at the old quota. Last year's quota. We all agree: 680 characters in the morning, 590 in the afternoon. We meet that and no more."

Feng says: "They'll fire us."

Lin says: "They can't fire all of us. The archive needs three hundred clerks. If we all hold to the old quota, they have to accept it or shut down."

Mei says: "Or they replace us one by one. Dismiss the organizers, scare the rest into compliance."

Lin says: "Not if we're organized. Not if we have a hundred clerks committed."

Wei thinks about this. The logic is appealing. The archive does need three hundred clerks. The work must be done. If enough clerks refuse the new quotas, the Board would have to negotiate.

But Wei also thinks about Liu's empty desk, and the new clerk with steady hands, and how quickly replacement happens.

He says: "How do we know we have a hundred clerks? How do we know who will hold and who will break?"

Lin says: "We ask. Carefully. We build trust."

Over the next three weeks, Lin talks to clerks during lunch breaks and in the evenings. He is careful. He does not approach anyone in the Long Hall during work hours. He does not write anything down.

Wei watches this happen. He does not commit, but he does not refuse either.

Feng says to Wei: "This will fail. They're watching."

Wei says: "How do you know?"

Feng says: "Because they always watch."

But Lin continues. By the third week, he tells Wei that forty clerks have committed. By the fourth week, sixty.

The fifth week, Lin calls a meeting in a rented room three streets from the archive. Twenty clerks attend, including Wei and Feng. Lin stands at the front of the room.

He says: "We have sixty committed. We need a hundred to be safe. Once we have a hundred, we deliver a petition to the Board. We state our terms: return to last year's quotas, or we work only to those quotas regardless."

Mei says: "What about the Benevolent Fund? The debt?"

Lin says: "We demand debt forgiveness for all current loans. The fund is how they trap us. We can't organize if we're trapped by debt."

Someone says: "They'll never agree to that."

Lin says: "Then we hold the line. We work the old quotas. They can dismiss us, but they can't dismiss all of us."

The room is silent. Wei can feel the tension—the desire to believe this might work, and the fear that it won't.

Then Feng says: "When do we deliver the petition?"

Lin says: "When we have a hundred signatures. Another two weeks."

Wei thinks: Two weeks. In two weeks, either we coordinate or we fail.

He does not know which is more likely.

The meeting ends. The clerks leave in small groups, spacing their departures so as not to attract attention.

Wei walks home with Feng. Feng says: "This is a mistake."

Wei says: "Maybe."

Feng says: "They're watching. They know."

Wei says: "How can they know? Lin is careful."

Feng says: "They always know."

But Wei wants to believe that Feng is wrong. He wants to believe that sixty clerks coordinating in secret is possible. That a hundred clerks making demands together is possible.

He wants to believe there is a way out besides waiting for his hands to fail.

The next week, Lin approaches Wei during lunch. He says: "We're at seventy-three. Will you commit?"

Wei thinks about his father's land, and the 34.2 taels he owes, and the way his hand cramps at character 589.

He says: "Yes."

Lin nods. He does not smile. He says: "Don't tell anyone who hasn't already committed. We're close."

That evening, Wei lies awake and thinks: Maybe this will work. Maybe seventy-three clerks is enough. Maybe the Board will negotiate rather than lose a quarter of their workforce.

He does not let himself calculate the odds. Calculating would require accepting how unlikely this is.

Instead he imagines: the petition delivered, the Board meeting to discuss terms, the quotas reduced, the debt forgiven. He imagines his hands healing, his father's land secure, a future that is not just grinding toward dismissal.

He imagines this for three days.

On the fourth day, four clerks are summoned before dawn.

Wei is one of them.

---

They are brought to separate rooms in the administrative wing. Wei has never been in this part of the archive before. The room is small, windowless. A single lamp on a desk. A supervisor Wei doesn't recognize sits behind the desk.

The supervisor says: "You've been meeting with other clerks. Discussing quota refusal."

Wei's stomach drops. He says nothing.

The supervisor says: "We're offering you a choice. You can denounce the organizing and return to work. Or you can be dismissed immediately, and your debt will be called today."

Wei says: "I haven't refused any quota. I've met every quota."

The supervisor says: "The organizing is refusal. Coordinating to undermine archive productivity is grounds for immediate dismissal."

Wei thinks: They were watching. Feng was right.

He says: "What happens if I denounce it?"

The supervisor says: "You return to work. Your debt remains but is not called. You continue as before."

Wei thinks about the other three clerks in other rooms, receiving the same choice. He thinks about Lin, and Mei, and the seventy-three who committed.

He thinks: If I denounce it, I betray them. If I refuse, I'm dismissed and my family loses everything.

He says: "Who else have you summoned?"

The supervisor says: "That's not your concern. Your concern is your choice."

Wei sits in silence. The lamp flickers. He can hear his own breathing.

He thinks: This is the trap. Not the debt, not the quotas. This. The moment when coordination requires sacrificing yourself, and self-preservation requires betraying others.

He says: "I won't denounce it."

The supervisor makes a note. He says: "You're dismissed. Your debt of 34.2 taels is due immediately. You have three days to pay or your family's land will be seized."

Wei stands. His legs are shaking. He says: "What about the others?"

The supervisor says: "Leave."

Wei leaves. In the hallway, he sees one of the other summoned clerks—a man named Zhao who has been here for fourteen months. Zhao's face is pale. He does not meet Wei's eyes.

Wei understands: Zhao took the deal.

Wei walks to the Long Hall to collect his things. It is just after dawn. The morning quota has not yet begun. The hall is full of clerks preparing their desks, arranging brushes and ink.

Lin is at his desk in row nine. He sees Wei and his face goes still.

Wei shakes his head slightly. Lin understands.

Wei collects his brushes and his few personal items. As he leaves, he sees Supervisor Chen standing at the front of the hall. Chen is watching him. Their eyes meet for a moment.

Chen's expression is unreadable.

Wei leaves the Long Hall. He will never enter it again.

---

That afternoon, Supervisor Chen makes an announcement to the assembled clerks.

He says: "Four clerks have been dismissed for organizing to undermine archive productivity. This behavior will not be tolerated. Any clerk found coordinating to refuse quotas will be dismissed immediately and their debt called."

He pauses. The hall is silent.

He says: "The archive operates on merit and compliance. Those who meet the standards are retained. Those who don't are replaced. This is fair and necessary."

In row twelve, a clerk named Jiang starts to stand. The clerk next to him—a woman named Shu—grabs his arm and pulls him back down.

Jiang sits. The moment passes.

Chen says: "Return to work."

Three hundred clerks pick up their brushes. The scratch of brush on paper resumes, constant and directionless.

Lin sits at his desk in row nine. His hands are shaking, but not from cramping. He thinks: Seventy-three committed. Four dismissed. The rest are scared.

He thinks: It's over.

He picks up his brush and begins copying. He meets the morning quota. He meets the afternoon quota.

The next month, the quota increases to 847 characters.

Lin's hands cramp at character 701. He wraps his wrist with cloth and continues.

He does not speak to other clerks about organizing again.

## Part Six: The Breaking Point

Feng lasts three more months after Wei's dismissal.

In the eleventh month, his hand cramps at character 512. He wraps the wrist tighter, but the cloth doesn't help anymore. The cramping is deeper now, in the tendons and bones. Some mornings he cannot make a fist.

He still meets the quota. He does not know how, but he does.

In the twelfth month, the quota increases to 871 characters. Feng's hand cramps at character 445. He begins taking longer breaks between columns, flexing his hand, waiting for the cramping to subside enough to continue.

Supervisor Chen notices. He does not say anything, but Feng sees him watching.

In the thirteenth month, Feng ruins four pages in one week. His hand shakes so badly that the characters drift and blur. He is fined 2.6 taels.

He owes 71 taels now. The interest is 5.7 taels per month. His wages are 12 taels. After room and food, he has 4 taels remaining.

The debt grows by 1.7 taels each month.

Feng does not bother calculating anymore. The numbers are clear.

One morning in the fourteenth month, Feng's hand cramps at character 380. He tries to continue but the brush slips. A line of ink drags across the page.

The page is ruined.

Feng stares at it. Then he sets down the brush and stands.

Supervisor Chen is at the front of the hall. He sees Feng stand. He walks toward him.

Feng says: "I can't do this anymore."

Chen says: "Sit down. Finish the quota."

Feng says: "I can't. My hand won't work."

Chen says: "If you leave now, you'll be dismissed."

Feng says: "I know."

Chen looks at him for a long moment. Then he says: "Go to the medical examiner. Get a formal assessment."

Feng goes to the medical examiner. The examiner is a thin man in his fifties who has seen hundreds of clerks with hand injuries.

The examiner manipulates Feng's hand, testing the range of motion. Feng winces.

The examiner says: "Chronic tendon strain. Inflammation in the wrist and palm. Likely permanent damage."

Feng says: "Can it be treated?"

The examiner says: "Rest. No writing for at least three months. Possibly longer."

Feng says: "I can't rest for three months. I'll be dismissed."

The examiner says: "If you continue writing, the damage will worsen. You may lose function entirely."

Feng says: "I'll lose function anyway. At least if I keep working, I can pay some of the debt."

The examiner writes his assessment on a form. He says: "This is my medical opinion. What you do with it is your choice."

Feng takes the form back to Supervisor Chen. Chen reads it and makes a note in his record book.

Chen says: "You're dismissed for medical inability to perform duties. Your debt is due in thirty days."

Feng says: "Thirty days?"

Chen says: "Standard policy. Medical dismissals receive thirty days to settle accounts."

Feng thinks: Thirty days to find 71 taels. It's impossible.

But he says: "Thank you."

Chen says: "Collect your things."

Feng returns to his desk. Around him, three hundred clerks are copying. The scratch of brushes on paper continues, constant and directionless.

Feng collects his brushes, his ink stone, the cloth he used to wrap his wrist. He looks at the desk where he has sat for two years.

He thinks: I came here to escape my village. I came here for steady wages and a future.

He thinks: I'm leaving with nothing and owing everything.

He thinks: Wei was right to refuse to denounce the organizing. At least Wei chose his dismissal. I just ground myself down until I broke.

Feng walks through the Long Hall toward the door. As he passes row seven, he sees Wei's old desk. A new clerk sits there now, young, hands steady, meeting the quota easily.

Feng wonders how long that clerk will last.

He leaves the Long Hall. The door closes behind him. The scratch of brushes continues.

---

Feng returns to his village with 71 taels of debt and hands that shake too badly to hold a farming tool properly.

His family's land is two mu of rice paddy. It is not enough to secure against 71 taels.

The Benevolent Fund sends a representative. The representative is polite but firm. He says: "You have thirty days to pay, or we seize the land."

Feng's father is sixty-three. He has farmed this land his entire life. He says: "Can we negotiate a payment plan?"

The representative says: "The fund's terms are standard. Full payment in thirty days, or seizure."

Feng's father looks at Feng. He says: "You were supposed to send money home. You were supposed to help us."

Feng says: "I tried."

His father says: "You borrowed more than you earned. You came back broken. What were you doing there?"

Feng does not know how to explain the quotas, the cramping, the way the debt compounds faster than the wages accumulate. He does not know how to explain that he was trying, every day, and it was not enough.

He says: "I'm sorry."

His father turns away.

Feng spends the thirty days trying to find a way to pay. He asks relatives for loans. He tries to sell his labor, but his hands shake too badly for farm work. He considers begging, but begging will not raise 71 taels in thirty days.

On the twenty-ninth day, his father says: "Let them take the land. We'll work as tenant farmers."

Feng says: "I'm sorry."

His father says: "Stop saying that."

On the thirtieth day, the representative returns. Feng's father signs the seizure documents. The land that has been in the family for three generations is transferred to the Clerks' Benevolent Fund.

The fund sells it immediately to a local landlord for 68 taels. The remaining 3 taels of Feng's debt is forgiven as uncollectable.

Feng and his family become tenant farmers on land they used to own.

Feng's hands never fully recover. He can do light work—weeding, harvesting—but he cannot grip a hoe properly. His father and younger brother do the heavy work.

At night, Feng lies awake and thinks about the Long Hall. He thinks about the scratch of three hundred brushes on paper. He thinks about Wei, dismissed for refusing to betray the organizing. He thinks about Lin, still there, still copying, hands cramping earlier each month.

He thinks: The archive is still running. The quotas are still increasing. Three hundred clerks are still grinding themselves down.

He thinks: I escaped, but I didn't escape. I just moved the grinding from the Long Hall to the rice paddy.

But at least here, he can see the sky.

## Part Seven: The Witness

Chen is reassigned three months after Feng's dismissal.

The reassignment is presented as a promotion. Chen is moved from supervision to the historical archives section. His new role is to catalog and preserve old records.

The historical archives are in a separate building, smaller and quieter than the Long Hall. Chen works alone most days, organizing documents that are decades or centuries old.

He understands: this is a removal. He is being moved away from the clerks because he asked too many questions at Board meetings.

The Director told him: "Your skills are better suited to historical work. You have an eye for detail and a respect for documentation."

Chen did not argue. He accepted the reassignment.

But he continues to think about the Long Hall.

He thinks about the quota increases, the dismissal rates, the debt accumulation. He thinks about Wei, who refused to betray the organizing and was dismissed. He thinks about Feng, who ground himself down until his hands broke.

He thinks about the pattern: quotas increase, hands fail, clerks are dismissed, new clerks are hired. The cycle repeats. The archive's output increases by 4% per year. The cost is borne by the clerks, not the archive.

Chen begins to document what he saw.

He works at night in his small room. The walls are thin—he can hear his neighbors through the plaster. He writes in small characters to save paper. He cannot requisition archive paper for this, so he buys his own with wages that should go to food.

He writes:

"The quota system operates as follows: monthly increases of 2-4% compound over time. A clerk who could comfortably meet quota in month one will struggle by month twelve and fail by month eighteen. The failure is attributed to individual inadequacy, but the pattern is structural. The quotas are designed to exceed human capacity over time."

He writes:

"The Benevolent Fund operates as a retention mechanism. Clerks borrow to cover shortfalls or family emergencies. The 8% monthly interest ensures the debt grows faster than wages can repay it. Dismissal triggers immediate debt collection, often resulting in land seizure. The threat of dismissal keeps clerks compliant even as quotas become unsustainable."

He writes:

"The organizing attempt in month ten was suppressed through selective dismissal and forced denunciation. Four clerks were given a choice: betray the organizing or be dismissed with debt called immediately. One complied, three refused. The suppression was sufficient to prevent further coordination. The remaining committed clerks returned to compliance out of fear."

Chen writes for three months. His hand cramps—he is experiencing what the clerks experience, though on a smaller scale. He buys a cloth wrap like Feng used. It helps slightly.

He loses weight. He is spending his food money on paper and ink. His colleagues notice but do not ask.

One night, a guard makes rounds earlier than usual. Chen hears footsteps in the hallway. He hides the pages under a stack of old tax records and pretends to be reviewing historical documents.

The guard opens the door, looks in, sees Chen at his desk surrounded by old records. The guard nods and moves on.

Chen's hands are shaking. He realizes: if the guard had looked more carefully, if he had asked what Chen was writing, Chen would have been dismissed.

After that night, Chen writes at home. But his room is small and the walls are thin. He writes in the early morning before his neighbors wake, or late at night after they sleep.

The documentation grows. After six months, Chen has 230 pages. The pages describe:
- The quota progression over three years
- The dismissal rates and replacement patterns
- The Benevolent Fund's loan volume and interest calculations
- The organizing attempt and its suppression
- Individual case studies: Wei, Feng, Lin, twelve others

Chen reads through the pages. He thinks: This is evidence. This is proof that the system extracts more than it provides, that the cost is borne by individuals while the benefit accrues to the archive.

But he does not know what to do with this evidence.

He considers: send it to provincial authorities? But the provincial authorities approved the quota system. They review the archive's output reports and praise the productivity increases.

He considers: circulate it among the clerks? But that would be organizing, which is grounds for dismissal. And the clerks already know what the system does to them. They don't need documentation to know their hands are cramping.

He considers: keep it private, as a personal record? But then it's just catharsis, not action.

He considers: leave it in the historical archives, hidden, for someone to find later? This is the option that appeals most. Future historians might find it. They might understand what was happening here.

But "might" is not certainty. And future historians might not care, or might not have access, or might interpret it as the complaints of a disgruntled employee.

Chen sits with this decision for a week. He does not write during this week. He only thinks.

Finally, he decides: he will make three copies. He will hide them in different locations within the historical archives. He will place them in sections that are rarely accessed—old tax records from the previous dynasty, land surveys from fifty years ago, personnel files from the archive's founding.

Someone might find them. Or no one might find them.

But at least they will exist.

Chen spends two weeks making the copies. His hand cramps badly. He is copying his own documentation of how copying destroys hands. The irony is not lost on him.

He loses more weight. He is eating one meal a day now, sometimes less. His colleagues ask if he is ill. He says he is fine.

He is not fine. He is grinding himself down for work that might not matter. He is becoming like the clerks—sacrificing his body for output that serves a system he cannot change.

But he continues because stopping would mean accepting that what happened to Wei and Feng was acceptable. And he cannot accept that.

When the copies are complete, Chen hides them:
- One in the tax records from the Qing dynasty, section 47, shelf 12
- One in the land surveys from 1821, section 23, shelf 8
- One in the personnel files from the archive's founding in 1793, section 61, shelf 3

He does not mark the locations. He does not tell anyone. The documents are simply there, waiting.

Chen thinks: This is all I can do. I cannot change the system. I cannot save the clerks. I can only document what I saw.

He thinks: Maybe that matters. Maybe it doesn't.

He does not know.

He returns to his work cataloging old records. His hand still cramps. He still wraps it with cloth. He still loses sleep thinking about the Long Hall.

But the documentation exists. Hidden, uncertain, possibly futile.

But it exists.

## Part Eight: The Return

Wei returns to his village with 34.2 taels of debt and hands that cramp when he holds a farming tool.

His father is waiting when Wei arrives. His father looks older than Wei remembers—the lines in his face deeper, his back more curved.

Wei's uncle (his father's younger brother) is also there. The uncle looks at Wei's hands, then at his face.

The uncle says: "What happened?"

Wei tries to explain. The archive, the quotas, the cramping, the dismissal. The words sound inadequate.

The uncle says: "You left to make money. You come back with nothing. Now you bring debt. We're supposed to feed you?"

Wei says: "I'll work. I'll pay it back."

The uncle says: "With what? You can't even hold a hoe properly."

Wei's father says: "He's my son."

The uncle says: "He's two mouths. We barely have enough for one family."

Wei's father says: "We'll manage."

The uncle looks at Wei for a long moment. Then he leaves without another word.

Wei understands: his return is costing the family. Not just the debt, but the food, the space, the labor he cannot fully provide.

His father says: "Don't mind your uncle. He's worried about the harvest."

Wei says: "He's right. I'm a burden."

His father says: "You're my son. You're not a burden."

But Wei can see the worry in his father's face. The harvest was poor last year. The family has no surplus. Adding another mouth means less for everyone.

Wei says: "I'll work. I'll do whatever I can."

His father nods. He does not say: your hands are ruined. He does not say: you can't do the work you used to do.

But they both know it.

---

The first week, Wei tries to help with the harvest. The rice is ready for cutting. Wei takes a sickle and goes to the field with his father and younger brother.

The sickle feels wrong in his hand. His grip is weak. The cramping starts after twenty minutes.

He cuts slowly, carefully. His father and brother cut three times as fast.

Other villagers are in adjacent fields. Wei sees them watching. He sees them whisper to each other.

One older farmer—a man named Gao who has known Wei's family for decades—approaches during the midday break.

Gao says to Wei's father: "Can he actually help? Or are we just carrying him?"

Wei's father says: "He's learning."

Gao says: "He's slow. And clumsy. We need every hand for the harvest. If he can't keep up, he's taking food without earning it."

Wei's father says: "He'll improve."

Gao looks at Wei. He says: "You went to the capital for wages. You came back with nothing. Now you expect us to feed you?"

Wei says: "I'll work harder."

Gao says: "Work smarter, not harder. Harder doesn't matter if you can't keep pace."

He walks away.

Wei's father says: "Don't listen to him."

But Wei knows Gao is right. He is slow. He is clumsy. His hands cramp and the sickle slips and he cuts less rice in an hour than his brother cuts in twenty minutes.

That night, Wei lies awake and thinks: I left the village to escape this. I came back to this anyway. But now I'm worse at it than I was before.

The next day, Wei and his father work through lunch. They work past sunset. Wei's hands are bleeding from the sickle grip, but he continues.

By the end of the day, they have finished their section of the field.

The next morning, Gao brings Wei a different sickle—one with a padded grip that is easier on the hands.

Gao says nothing. He just hands Wei the sickle and walks away.

Wei understands: this is acceptance. Not approval, but acknowledgment that Wei is trying.

It is not much. But it is something.

---

Three months after Wei's return, Feng arrives at the village.

Wei is in the field when he sees Feng walking up the road. Feng's hands are wrapped in cloth. He walks slowly, like each step costs something.

Wei sets down his sickle and goes to meet him.

They stand on the road, looking at each other. Neither speaks for a long moment.

Finally Wei says: "Your family's land?"

Feng says: "Seized. We're tenant farmers now."

Wei says: "I'm sorry."

Feng says: "Don't be. You tried to warn me."

Wei says: "I didn't warn you. I just refused to inform."

Feng says: "That's more than most did."

They walk to Wei's father's house. Wei's father sees Feng and understands without explanation. He says: "There's room. Not much, but room."

Feng says: "I can work. My hands aren't good, but I can do light work."

Wei's father says: "We'll find something."

That night, Wei and Feng sit outside the house. The air is cool. They can hear insects in the rice paddies.

Feng says: "Do you think about the Long Hall?"

Wei says: "Every day."

Feng says: "Do you think it's still the same?"

Wei says: "Yes."

Feng says: "Three hundred clerks. Quotas increasing. Hands cramping."

Wei says: "Yes."

They sit in silence.

Finally Feng says: "We got out."

Wei says: "We got out with nothing."

Feng says: "We got out alive. That's more than some."

Wei thinks about this. He thinks about Lin, still in the Long Hall, still copying, hands cramping earlier each month. He thinks about the new clerk at his old desk, meeting quotas easily now, but grinding down toward the same failure.

He says: "Getting out isn't the same as escaping."

Feng says: "No. But it's something."

They sit in silence and listen to the insects.

---

Wei and Feng work in the fields. The work is hard. Their hands still cramp. But the work is different.

In the Long Hall, the work was copying characters that described land they would never see. The output disappeared into ledgers they could not access. The quotas increased regardless of their effort.

Here, the work is planting and harvesting rice. The output is visible: the rice grows, they cut it, they eat it. The work follows seasons, not quotas.

The work is still hard. Wei's hands still hurt. Feng's hands still shake.

But when they plant rice, they see it grow. When they harvest rice, they eat it.

It is not freedom. It is not prosperity. It is subsistence.

But it is theirs.

## Part Nine: What Persists

Chen is still in the historical archives, cataloging old records.

His documentation is hidden in three locations. No one has found it. Perhaps no one will.

Chen does not know if the documentation matters. He only knows that he could not not write it.

He has lost weight. His hand still cramps. He still thinks about the Long Hall.

But he continues his work, cataloging records that are decades or centuries old, preserving what was.

---

The Long Hall still holds three hundred clerks at wooden desks arranged in fifteen rows of twenty.

The quota is now 923 characters in the morning, 798 in the afternoon. This is an increase from last month's 901 and 776.

Lin is still there, in row nine, position seven. His hands cramp at character 534. He wraps his wrists with cloth. He meets the quota.

He does not speak about organizing. He does not speak about the four clerks who were dismissed. He copies characters from old records into new ledgers.

He has been here for nineteen months. He calculates: he has perhaps three months before he cannot meet the quota anymore.

He does not know what he will do then.

Around him, three hundred clerks copy. The scratch of brushes on paper is constant and directionless.

Some of the clerks are new—hired in the past three months to replace those who were dismissed. Their hands are steady. They meet the quotas easily.

Lin watches them and thinks: They don't know yet. They will learn.

The quotas will increase. Their hands will cramp. They will borrow from the Benevolent Fund. The debt will grow.

Some will try to organize. They will be dismissed.

Some will grind themselves down until they break. They will be dismissed.

Some will leave before they break. They will return to their villages with debt and ruined hands.

The archive will continue. The quotas will increase. The output will grow by 4% per year.

This is how the system works.

Lin knows this. He has known it since Wei was dismissed, since the organizing attempt failed, since he realized that coordination is possible but usually prevented.

But he continues to copy. He meets the quota. He wraps his wrists and flexes his hands and continues.

He does not know why he continues. Perhaps because stopping would mean accepting that the grinding is inevitable. Perhaps because continuing is the only form of resistance available to him.

Or perhaps because he has nowhere else to go, and no way to pay the debt, and continuing is simply what he does until he can't anymore.

The scratch of brushes continues. Three hundred clerks copy. The quotas increase.

The archive persists.

---

Wei and Feng are in the rice paddy, planting.

The work is slow. Their hands cramp. But they continue.

Wei's father works beside them. He does not speak much, but his presence is steady.

At midday, they rest. They eat rice and pickled vegetables. The food is simple but sufficient.

Feng says: "Do you think we made the right choice?"

Wei says: "What choice?"

Feng says: "Leaving. Coming back here."

Wei thinks about this. He thinks about the Long Hall, the quotas, the debt. He thinks about the organizing attempt, the dismissals, the grinding.

He says: "We didn't choose to leave. We were dismissed."

Feng says: "You chose not to inform. That was a choice."

Wei says: "Yes."

Feng says: "Was it the right choice?"

Wei looks at the rice paddy. The young plants are green and fragile. In four months they will be ready to harvest. In four months, Wei and Feng will cut them and thresh them and eat them.

He says: "I don't know. But it was my choice."

They return to work. Their hands move through the water and mud, planting rice.

The work is hard. The work is slow. But the work is theirs.

## Coda

Chen writes in the historical archives, cataloging records that are decades old.

The Long Hall continues. Three hundred clerks copy. The quotas increase.

Wei and Feng plant rice in their village. Their hands cramp, but they continue.

The archive persists. The village persists. The grinding persists.

Chen's documentation is hidden in three locations. Perhaps someone will find it. Perhaps no one will.

Wei and Feng's choice to leave is not a solution. It is a survival.

Lin's choice to stay is not acceptance. It is endurance.

The system continues. The costs are borne by individuals. The benefits accrue to the archive.

This is what persists:

Three hundred clerks at wooden desks. Brushes scratching paper. Hands cramping. Quotas increasing.

Chen in the historical archives, documenting what he cannot change.

Wei and Feng in the rice paddy, planting what they will harvest.

Their hands move. Right to left. Column after column.

The work continues.
