# The Archive of Hands (Revised)

## Part One: Wei

The morning bell rings at the hour of the rabbit. I wake in darkness, dress in darkness, walk through darkness to the Long Hall. I am at desk 287. I have been at desk 287 for five years.

The folio arrives at dawn: tax records from Guangxi province, year twelve of the Hongwu Emperor. My task is to copy them into the permanent archive. The characters must be exact. The columns must align. Any error means the page is rejected, the time is lost, the quota remains.

My quota is forty-five folios per day.

I completed forty-two yesterday. Forty-one the day before. Forty-three the day before that.

The gap accumulates like water behind a dam.

I pick up my brush. The handle is smooth bamboo, worn by five years of gripping. My hand knows its weight without thinking. First character: 税 (tax). The brush moves. My hand trembles.

Not much. A small tremor in the final stroke. But enough that the character is slightly malformed. I examine it in the lamplight. The supervisor will reject this. I dip my brush in water, try to blur the ink, start again.

Second character: 收 (collect). The brush moves. My hand steadies.

I work.

The Long Hall is silent except for the sound of brushes on paper. Three hundred clerks copying. We are forbidden to speak. We are forbidden to look up from our desks. We are forbidden to leave except for the morning and afternoon breaks, fifteen minutes each.

At the hour of the snake, my hand cramps. I set down my brush, flex my fingers. The joints are swollen. They have been swollen for three years. In the first year, they would recover overnight. In the second year, they stayed swollen through the morning. Now they never recover.

I pick up my brush. I work.

Supervisor Liu walks between the rows. He is a small man with a precise mustache and eyes that notice everything. He stops at my desk, examines my work. I keep my face blank.

"Seventeen folios," he says. "It's nearly midday."

"Yes, supervisor."

"You need twenty-three more by the evening bell."

"Yes, supervisor."

He moves on. I exhale slowly.

The math is simple. Twenty-three folios in seven hours. That's three folios per hour, twenty minutes per folio. Possible if my hand doesn't cramp again. Possible if I don't make errors. Possible if the documents are simple tax records and not complex legal contracts.

I look at the next folio. Legal contract. Seventeen clauses in small characters.

I pick up my brush. I work.

At the hour of the horse, the break bell rings. Fifteen minutes. I stand, my back protesting. The walk to the courtyard takes two minutes. That leaves thirteen minutes to eat, drink, relieve myself, and return.

The courtyard is small, surrounded by high walls. A hundred clerks crowd around the water barrel and the rice buckets. I fill my bowl, find a corner, eat standing. The rice is cold and slightly sour. I eat it anyway.

Feng finds me. He is forty-three years old, twenty years my senior. He has been a clerk for forty years. His hands are gnarled like tree roots, the fingers permanently curved from holding a brush. He can barely grip his bowl.

"How many?" he asks.

"Seventeen."

He nods. "I'm at twelve."

"You should be at twenty."

"I know."

We eat in silence. Around us, other clerks eat quickly, faces blank. No one speaks about quotas. No one speaks about the gap between what we complete and what we owe. Speaking makes it real.

"I can cover three folios," Feng says quietly.

I look at him. "You're already behind."

"I know."

"You'll fall further behind."

"I know."

The gratitude sits in my throat like a stone. Three folios is two hours of his pain, two hours of his quota sacrificed for mine. I should refuse. I should tell him to save himself.

"Thank you," I say.

He nods. We finish eating. The bell rings. We return to our desks.

At the hour of the monkey, my hand cramps again. This time it doesn't release. I set down my brush, massage the palm, the wrist, the forearm. The muscles are knotted. I press my thumb into the knots, working them slowly. Five minutes pass. The cramp eases but doesn't fully release.

I pick up my brush. The characters are shaky. I work more slowly, compensating for the tremor. Twenty-one folios completed. Twenty-four to go.

The math doesn't work.

At the hour of the rooster, Supervisor Liu returns. He counts my folios. Twenty-eight.

"Seventeen more," he says.

"Yes, supervisor."

"The evening bell rings in two hours."

"Yes, supervisor."

He doesn't move. I keep my face blank, my eyes on the desk.

"You've been behind quota for three weeks," he says.

"Yes, supervisor."

"Do you know what happens when a clerk falls too far behind?"

"Yes, supervisor."

"Tell me."

"The clerk is dismissed."

"And?"

"The clerk's debt is called."

"And?"

"The clerk has thirty days to repay."

"And if the clerk cannot repay?"

"The debt is transferred to the clerk's family."

He nods. "Your father is a farmer in Anhui province. Small plot. Two mu of land. Is that correct?"

My throat tightens. "Yes, supervisor."

"Two mu produces approximately fifteen shi of rice per year. After tax, perhaps ten shi. Market price is currently three taels per shi. That's thirty taels annual income. Your debt is currently eighty-four taels. How long would it take your father to repay?"

I don't answer.

"Three years," Liu says. "If he spent nothing on food, clothing, or seed. If the harvest was perfect. If the price didn't drop. Three years."

He leans closer. "But the harvest is never perfect. And the price always drops. So really, it would take five years. Maybe six. By which time your father would be dead and your younger brother would inherit the debt."

I keep my face blank.

"Do you understand?" Liu asks.

"Yes, supervisor."

"Then work faster."

He walks away. I pick up my brush. My hand is shaking. Not from the cramp now. From something else.

I work.

At the hour of the dog, the evening bell rings. I have completed thirty-nine folios. Six short of quota.

I gather my materials, clean my brush, return the unused folios to the supply desk. The clerk there marks my count in the ledger. Thirty-nine. The gap widens.

I walk back to the dormitory in darkness. The dormitory is a long building with rows of sleeping mats. Each clerk has a mat, a blanket, a small wooden box for belongings. I own three robes, one spare brush, a whetstone, and a letter from my father written five years ago when I left for the capital.

I don't read the letter anymore. I know what it says: "Work hard. Make us proud. The examination fees cost everything. Don't waste this chance."

I didn't waste it. I passed the examination. I was assigned to the Imperial Archives. I was given a desk, a quota, a salary.

The salary is six taels per month. Room and board cost four taels. That leaves two taels for everything else: clothing, medicine, ink, paper for personal use, savings.

But there are fees. Brush replacement: two strings of cash per month. Lamp oil: one string. Uniform cleaning: one string. Dormitory maintenance: two strings. These are mandatory deductions. After fees, I have one tael and three strings remaining.

But there are fines. Each folio short of quota: ten cash. Yesterday I was six short. That's sixty cash. The day before, seven short. Seventy cash. The fines accumulate faster than the salary.

Three months ago, I borrowed money from the archive's loan office. Five taels at twenty percent monthly interest. I used it to pay fines. Now I owe six taels plus interest. Next month, seven taels. The month after, eight.

The math doesn't work.

I lie on my mat. Around me, other clerks are already asleep, exhausted. Feng is two mats away. His breathing is labored. I listen to it in the darkness.

Tomorrow the bell will ring at the hour of the rabbit. I will wake in darkness, dress in darkness, walk through darkness to the Long Hall. I will sit at desk 287. I will pick up my brush.

I will complete forty-two folios. Maybe forty-three. Not forty-five.

The gap will widen.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

## Part Two: Chen

I wake at the hour of the rabbit to the sound of my wife moving in the kitchen. Warm light seeps under the door. I dress slowly, enjoying the quiet. My daughter is still asleep in the next room. She is eight years old. She likes to draw birds.

I am Chen Wei-ming, Senior Verification Officer of the Imperial Archives. I have held this position for eight years. Before that, I was a clerk in the Long Hall for fourteen years.

I remember the Long Hall. The rows of desks, the oil lamps, the silence. I remember the quota: forty-five folios per day. I remember completing them. Not easily—some days were harder than others—but consistently. I was promoted because I was reliable.

That's what the system rewards. Reliability.

My wife brings tea and rice porridge. We eat together in comfortable silence. She asks about my day. I tell her about the verification work: reviewing clerks' output, checking for errors, compiling performance reports. It's detailed work but not physically demanding. My hands no longer ache.

After breakfast, I walk to the archives. The morning is cool, the streets quiet. I live in the administrative quarter, ten minutes from the Long Hall. The house is small but well-built: three rooms, a courtyard, a kitchen. I rent it from the archives at subsidized rate. Four taels per month. My salary is twenty-two taels.

After rent, I have eighteen taels. Food costs six. Clothing, medicine, my daughter's tutor, savings: another eight. That leaves four taels for discretionary spending. We eat meat twice a week. My daughter has new shoes when she needs them. We are comfortable.

I earned this. Twenty-two years of service. Fourteen in the Long Hall, eight in verification. I worked hard, made no errors, never complained. The system recognized merit. That's how it's supposed to work.

I arrive at the verification office. It's a smaller building adjacent to the Long Hall, with twenty desks arranged in rows. Each verification officer is responsible for reviewing the output of fifteen clerks. I review desks 280-294.

I begin with the daily reports. Each clerk's folio count is recorded in the ledger. I scan the numbers:

Desk 280: 44 folios (quota: 45, shortfall: 1)
Desk 281: 46 folios (quota: 45, surplus: 1)
Desk 282: 43 folios (quota: 45, shortfall: 2)
...
Desk 287: 39 folios (quota: 45, shortfall: 6)

I pause at desk 287. Wei Zhen. Five years of service. His numbers have been declining for three months. I pull his file.

Performance history:
- Year 1: Average 46 folios/day
- Year 2: Average 45 folios/day
- Year 3: Average 44 folios/day
- Year 4: Average 43 folios/day
- Year 5 (current): Average 41 folios/day

The decline is steady. Predictable. I've seen this pattern before.

I check his medical reports. No formal complaints, but the dormitory supervisor noted "frequent hand cramping" six months ago. I check his loan status. Five taels borrowed three months ago, currently six taels owed with interest.

I make a note in his file: "Recommend counseling session. Discuss time management and hand care techniques."

This is standard procedure. When a clerk falls behind, we offer guidance. Sometimes it helps. Sometimes the clerk improves, stabilizes, continues. Sometimes the decline continues and we have to make harder decisions.

But we give chances. That's important. The system isn't cruel. It's demanding, yes. The empire requires accurate records. The quotas reflect that requirement. But we don't dismiss clerks arbitrarily. We look for merit, for effort, for improvement.

I continue reviewing files. Desk 288: steady performance. Desk 289: slight improvement. Desk 290: significant decline, recommend dismissal review.

At midday, I attend the weekly coordination meeting. The other verification officers gather in the conference room. We discuss performance trends, quota adjustments, staffing needs.

Chief Verification Officer Wang presides. He is fifty years old, precise and methodical. He has held his position for fifteen years.

"The Board has requested a quota increase," Wang announces. "Current quota is forty-five folios per day. Proposed increase: forty-eight folios."

The room is silent. I calculate quickly. Forty-eight folios is seven percent increase. Most clerks are already struggling to meet forty-five.

"What's the justification?" someone asks.

"Backlog reduction," Wang says. "The archives are currently eighteen months behind on provincial records. The Board wants this reduced to twelve months by year end."

"That's aggressive," I say.

Wang nods. "The Board is aware. They've allocated budget for additional clerks. We'll hire thirty new positions."

"That will help," someone says.

"It will help the backlog," I say. "But increasing quota for existing clerks—many are already at capacity."

Wang looks at me. "Your concern is noted. However, the Board's analysis shows that average completion rate is forty-three folios per day. That suggests capacity exists for increase."

I want to argue. Average completion rate doesn't account for the distribution. Some clerks complete fifty folios easily. Others struggle to complete forty. Raising the quota to forty-eight will help the strong clerks and crush the weak ones.

But I don't argue. The Board has made the decision. My role is implementation, not policy.

"When does this take effect?" I ask.

"Next month," Wang says.

The meeting continues. We discuss hiring procedures, training protocols, dormitory capacity. Everything is methodical, rational, carefully planned.

The system works. It has to work. The empire requires accurate records. The archives provide them. The clerks are the mechanism. The quotas ensure efficiency.

I return to my desk. I pull Wei Zhen's file again. Forty-eight folios per day. He's currently completing forty-one. The gap will widen from four folios to seven. His debt will compound faster. His dismissal will come sooner.

I make another note: "Recommend loan extension. Provide additional time for performance improvement."

This is what I can do. I can't change the quota. I can't change the Board's decision. But I can give Wei Zhen more time. Maybe he'll improve. Maybe his hands will strengthen. Maybe he'll find a technique that works.

Maybe.

I close the file. I continue reviewing. The afternoon passes. At the hour of the rooster, I finish my work and walk home.

My daughter is in the courtyard, drawing. She shows me her picture: a crane standing in water. The proportions are wrong but the spirit is right. I praise her work. She beams.

We eat dinner together. My wife has made fish with vegetables. We talk about small things: my daughter's lessons, my wife's garden, the neighbor's new baby. Normal life. Comfortable life.

After dinner, I sit in the courtyard and drink tea. The evening is warm. I think about Wei Zhen, about the quota increase, about the thirty new clerks who will arrive next month.

They will be young, eighteen or nineteen years old. They will have passed the examination. They will be assigned desks, given quotas, taught the procedures. Some will thrive. Some will struggle. Some will fail.

That's how the system works. It identifies merit. It rewards capability. It's not perfect—no system is—but it's fair. It gives everyone the same chance.

I believed this when I was a clerk. I believe it now.

I finish my tea. I go inside. My wife is putting our daughter to bed. I listen to them talking, my daughter's voice high and bright, my wife's voice soft and patient.

I earned this. This house, this family, this peace. Twenty-two years of service. I worked hard. I made no errors. The system recognized merit.

That's how it's supposed to work.

## Part Three: Wei

Feng's hands fail on a Tuesday morning.

I'm at my desk, working on folio twenty-three, when I hear the brush clatter. I don't look up—we're forbidden to look up—but I hear Feng's breathing change. Sharp intake. Held breath. Slow exhale.

The brush clatters again.

Supervisor Liu appears. "Problem?"

"No, supervisor." Feng's voice is steady.

"Then why did you drop your brush?"

"Cramp, supervisor. It's passing."

"Pick up your brush."

Silence. Then: "I'm trying, supervisor."

I risk a glance. Feng's right hand is curled into a claw. He's using his left hand to try to straighten the fingers. They won't straighten.

Liu watches for a moment. Then: "Go to the medical office."

"I can work, supervisor."

"Go to the medical office."

Feng stands. His legs are unsteady. He walks down the aisle, his right hand still curled. The Long Hall is silent except for the sound of three hundred brushes on paper.

He doesn't return.

At the afternoon break, I find him in the courtyard. He's sitting against the wall, his right hand in his lap. The fingers are still curled.

I sit beside him. "What did the physician say?"

"Permanent damage. Tendons shortened from overuse. He gave me liniment."

"Will it help?"

"No."

We sit in silence. Around us, other clerks eat quickly, faces blank.

"How many years?" I ask.

"Forty."

"How many folios?"

He calculates. "Approximately four hundred thousand."

Four hundred thousand folios. Forty years of copying. Forty years of brush strokes, character after character, page after page. And now his hand is a claw.

"What will you do?" I ask.

"I don't know."

"Can you work with your left hand?"

"Not fast enough. The quota is forty-five folios. I might manage twenty with my left hand."

"They'll dismiss you."

"Yes."

"Your debt?"

"Sixty-three taels."

The number sits between us like a stone. Sixty-three taels. At forty years of service, Feng should have saved money. But there were fines, fees, medical costs, loans. The system extracts everything.

"I have no family," Feng says. "No wife, no children. My parents are dead. My brother died ten years ago."

"Then the debt dies with you."

"Yes."

"That's something."

"That's something."

We sit in silence. The bell rings. We return to our desks.

Feng works with his left hand. The characters are shaky but legible. He completes eight folios by evening. Thirty-seven short of quota.

That night in the dormitory, I lie on my mat and listen to Feng's breathing. It's labored, painful. His hand must hurt. Forty years of copying, and this is the end: a claw, a debt, dismissal.

I think about my own hands. They're swollen, the joints stiff. Five years of copying. How many more years until they fail? Ten? Fifteen? And then what? Dismissal, debt, my father's land sold to pay what I owe.

The math doesn't work.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

The next morning, Feng is at his desk. He works with his left hand. He completes nine folios. Thirty-six short of quota.

The day after, eight folios. Thirty-seven short.

The day after, seven folios. Thirty-eight short.

On Friday, Supervisor Liu calls him to the front of the Long Hall. We're forbidden to watch, but I hear Liu's voice, calm and precise:

"Feng Chen-wei, you have failed to meet quota for four consecutive days. Your cumulative shortfall is one hundred forty-eight folios. Your debt is sixty-three taels. You are dismissed from service effective immediately. You have thirty days to settle your debt."

Silence.

"Do you understand?"

"Yes, supervisor."

"Collect your belongings. Report to the administrative office for final accounting."

Feng walks down the aisle. He passes my desk. He doesn't look at me. His face is blank.

He doesn't return.

That night, I go to his mat in the dormitory. His belongings are gone: his spare robe, his brush, his small wooden box. The mat is empty.

I ask the dormitory supervisor where he went.

"He left," the supervisor says.

"Where?"

"I don't know. He didn't say."

I return to my mat. I lie in darkness. Around me, other clerks sleep. I listen to their breathing.

Forty years of service. Four hundred thousand folios. And then: dismissed, debt, disappeared.

This is what the system does. It uses you until you break. Then it discards you.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

The next morning, the bell rings at the hour of the rabbit. I wake in darkness, dress in darkness, walk through darkness to the Long Hall.

A new clerk sits at Feng's desk. He is young, maybe eighteen. He copies with careful strokes, his face concentrated. He believes precision matters.

I sit at desk 287. I pick up my brush. My hand trembles.

I work.

At midday, I complete twenty-two folios. Twenty-three to go. The math doesn't work.

But I work anyway.

At the afternoon break, I see Feng in the courtyard. He's sitting in the corner, his right hand still curled. He has a bowl of rice. He's eating with his left hand.

I sit beside him. "You came back."

"I have nowhere else to go."

"Your debt—"

"Still owed. They gave me thirty days. I have twenty-six remaining."

"What will you do?"

"I don't know."

We eat in silence. The rice is cold and slightly sour.

"I can cover three folios," I say.

He looks at me. "You're already behind."

"I know."

"You'll fall further behind."

"I know."

"Why?"

I don't have an answer. The math doesn't work. Covering three folios for Feng means I'll complete thirty-nine instead of forty-two. The gap will widen. My debt will compound. My dismissal will come sooner.

But the alternative is watching Feng drown alone.

"Because," I say.

He nods. We finish eating. The bell rings. We return to our desks.

I work. I complete thirty-nine folios. Six short of quota.

Feng completes seven folios with his left hand. Thirty-eight short.

The gap widens.

## Part Four: Chen

The paper arrives in bundles from the provincial mill. Each bundle contains five hundred sheets, wrapped in cloth and sealed with wax. I inspect them as part of my verification duties.

The quality has been declining for two years.

I notice it first in the texture. The paper should be smooth, slightly glossy, with even fiber distribution. The new paper is rough, with visible clumps of fiber and irregular thickness. It tears more easily. The ink bleeds.

I report this to Chief Wang. He nods, makes a note, says nothing.

The decline continues. Six months ago, I noticed the paper yellowing faster. A document copied last year is already showing signs of degradation. The edges are brittle. The ink is fading.

I report this to Chief Wang. He nods, makes a note, says nothing.

Three months ago, I examined a tax record from five years ago. The paper was so degraded I could barely read it. The characters were faded, the edges crumbling. In another five years, it will be illegible.

I report this to Chief Wang. He schedules a meeting with the Board.

The meeting is today.

I arrive at the Board chamber at the hour of the snake. The room is large, with a long table and twelve chairs. The Board members are already seated: six senior administrators, three financial officers, two archival specialists, and the Director.

Chief Wang presents the issue. He shows examples of degraded documents. He explains the timeline: current paper will be illegible within ten years. Historical records are being lost.

The Board examines the documents. They pass them around the table, holding them up to the light, testing the texture.

The Director speaks. "What's the cost difference between current paper and higher quality?"

Wang consults his notes. "Current paper costs two taels per thousand sheets. Higher quality paper costs five taels per thousand sheets."

"That's a hundred fifty percent increase."

"Yes, Director."

"How many sheets do we use annually?"

"Approximately two million sheets."

The Director calculates. "Current cost is four thousand taels per year. Higher quality would be ten thousand taels. That's a six thousand tael increase."

"Yes, Director."

"What's our current budget surplus?"

"Three thousand taels."

"So we'd need to request additional funding."

"Yes, Director."

The Director looks at the degraded documents. He looks at the budget numbers. He looks at the Board members.

"The empire is currently funding military expansion in the south," he says. "The Ministry of Revenue has requested all departments reduce discretionary spending. We were asked to cut our budget by five percent. We're proposing to increase it by fifteen percent."

Silence.

"I understand the concern," the Director continues. "But we must consider priorities. The archives serve the empire by maintaining current records. Historical records are valuable, but they're not operationally critical. If we must choose between maintaining current operations and preserving documents from ten years ago, we choose current operations."

One of the archival specialists speaks. "But Director, if the paper continues to degrade, we'll lose not just historical records but recent records. Documents copied five years ago are already failing."

"Then we'll recopy them."

"That will require additional clerks."

"We're already hiring thirty new clerks for backlog reduction. They can handle recopying as needed."

"But if the paper continues to degrade—"

"Then we'll recopy again. The cost of recopying is less than the cost of higher quality paper."

The specialist falls silent.

I raise my hand. "Director, may I ask a question?"

"Proceed."

"If we continue using current paper, and documents degrade within ten years, and we recopy them, but those copies also degrade within ten years—doesn't that create a permanent copying cycle?"

"Yes."

"And doesn't that mean we're asking clerks to copy the same documents repeatedly, indefinitely?"

"Yes."

I pause. The Director waits.

"That seems..." I stop. The words are there but won't come. Wasteful. Cruel. Unsustainable. But these are not words you say to the Board.

"That seems like a question for the budget committee," I finish.

The Director nods. "The budget committee has reviewed this. The decision stands. We continue with current paper quality. We allocate budget for recopying as needed."

The meeting continues. Other topics. Hiring procedures. Training protocols. Dormitory capacity.

I sit at the table. I look at the degraded documents. I calculate.

Current quota: forty-eight folios per day.
Degradation cycle: ten years.
Average clerk tenure: six years.

Most clerks will be dismissed before they see their work degrade. They'll copy documents that will need to be recopied by other clerks who will also be dismissed before they see their work degrade. The cycle continues. The hands fail. The paper crumbles. Nothing persists except the structure that extracts the labor.

I look at the Director. He's discussing dormitory maintenance. His voice is calm, methodical. He's explaining that the cost of repairing the dormitory roof is less than the cost of building new dormitories, even though the roof will need to be repaired again in five years. The same logic. The same calculation. Minimize immediate cost. Accept permanent cycles. Extract labor. Discard bodies.

My hands are shaking.

I set them flat on the table. The Director doesn't notice. He's moved on to another topic.

The meeting ends. I return to my office.

I sit at my desk. I pull Wei Zhen's file.

Debt: ninety-two taels.
Average completion: forty-one folios per day.
Quota: forty-eight folios per day.
Shortfall: seven folios per day.
Estimated time to dismissal: eight weeks.

I write: "Recommend loan extension."

I close the file. I pull the next file. Another clerk. Another decline. Another recommendation.

I work through the afternoon. At the hour of the rooster, I finish. Eight loan extensions. Three counseling sessions. Two dismissals.

I walk home. My wife has made dinner. My daughter shows me her drawing: a phoenix. The proportions are better.

We eat together. We talk about small things.

After dinner, I sit in the courtyard. I drink tea.

I think about the Board meeting. About the degraded documents. About the permanent copying cycle. About the clerks who will copy documents that will crumble. About the hands that will fail. About the system that extracts everything and preserves nothing.

I think about Wei Zhen. Eight weeks.

I think about my own fourteen years in the Long Hall. I completed my quota. I met the standard. I was promoted.

But what if the standard is designed to break people? What if I survived because my body held up longer, not because I was more capable? What if the only difference between me and Wei Zhen is luck?

I finish my tea. I go inside.

My wife is reading to our daughter. I listen to her voice.

I earned this. I have to believe I earned this.

I lie in bed. My wife sleeps beside me. I listen to her breathing.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

## Part Five: Wei

Zhao finds me in the courtyard during the afternoon break.

"We need to talk," he says quietly.

I look at him. Zhao is twenty-six, three years my senior. He's been a clerk for seven years. His hands are swollen like mine.

"About what?"

"Not here. Tonight. After the evening bell. Meet me at the tea house on Dongcheng Street."

"We're not supposed to leave the dormitory after evening bell."

"I know."

He walks away before I can respond.

That night, I wait until the dormitory is quiet. Then I slip out through the side door. The streets are dark, mostly empty. I find the tea house. It's small, poorly lit, nearly empty. Zhao is in the back corner.

I sit across from him. He pours tea.

"How many folios today?" he asks.

"Forty."

"Quota?"

"Forty-eight."

He nods. "They raised it last month. I'm completing forty-two. Used to complete forty-six. My hands are getting worse."

"Everyone's hands are getting worse."

"Exactly."

He leans forward. "There are three hundred clerks in the Long Hall. How many do you think are meeting the new quota?"

I calculate. "Maybe a hundred. Maybe fewer."

"I've been watching. I think it's eighty. That means two hundred twenty clerks are falling behind. Two hundred twenty clerks accumulating debt, facing dismissal."

"What's your point?"

"My point is: we're all drowning. Individually, we're drowning. But together—"

"Together we're still drowning."

"Maybe. But maybe not. What if we all refused to work until they lowered the quota?"

I stare at him. "That's—"

"Dangerous. I know. But what's the alternative? We keep working until our hands fail? Until we're dismissed with debt we can't repay?"

"If we refuse to work, they'll dismiss us immediately."

"All of us? Three hundred clerks? They can't replace three hundred clerks overnight. The backlog would become catastrophic. The Board would have to negotiate."

"Or they'd make examples of the organizers and intimidate everyone else back to work."

Zhao is silent for a moment. "Maybe. But at least we'd have tried."

I drink my tea. It's bitter and lukewarm.

"How would this work?" I ask.

"We'd need to organize. Spread the word carefully. Build consensus. Choose a day. Everyone stops working simultaneously."

"How do you spread the word to three hundred people without the supervisors noticing?"

"Slowly. Carefully. One person at a time. We have break periods. We have the dormitory. We can talk."

"Talking is forbidden."

"Everything is forbidden. That's the point."

I look at him. His face is earnest, hopeful. He believes this can work.

I want to believe it too. But I've been here five years. I've watched clerks try to help each other, try to resist, try to find ways around the system. The system always wins.

"I'll think about it," I say.

"Don't think too long. Every day we wait, more people fall behind. More people accumulate debt. More people lose hope."

I finish my tea. I leave.

Over the next week, Zhao approaches me three more times. Each time, he's more urgent. He's been talking to other clerks. He has twenty people willing to organize. He needs fifty to make it work. He needs a hundred to make it unstoppable.

I don't commit. But I don't refuse either.

On the eighth day, Zhao finds me in the courtyard. "We're meeting tomorrow night. Same tea house. Bring anyone you trust."

"I don't trust anyone."

"Then come alone."

The next night, I go to the tea house. There are fifteen people in the back room. I recognize some of them: clerks from my section, clerks from other sections. Young men with swollen hands and desperate eyes.

Zhao stands at the front. "Thank you for coming. I know this is risky. But we're already at risk. Every day we work, we fall further behind. Every day we fall behind, the debt grows. This isn't sustainable."

He pauses. "We're proposing a work stoppage. All of us, simultaneously. We stop copying until the Board agrees to lower the quota back to forty-five folios. We're not asking for special treatment. We're asking for the quota we could actually meet."

Someone asks: "What if they refuse?"

"Then we continue the stoppage."

"What if they dismiss us?"

"They can't dismiss all of us. The archives would collapse."

"What if they dismiss the organizers and intimidate everyone else back to work?"

Zhao hesitates. "That's a risk. But if we have enough people, if we're unified, they can't break us."

"How many people do we have?"

"Twenty committed. Fifty interested. We need a hundred to make this work."

"How do we get a hundred?"

"We spread the word. Carefully. One person at a time. We have two weeks."

The meeting continues. People ask questions, raise concerns, debate tactics. Zhao answers patiently, persistently. He believes this can work.

I watch him. I want to believe too. But I keep thinking about Feng. Forty years of service, dismissed with a claw hand. The system doesn't negotiate. It extracts until you break, then replaces you.

After the meeting, I walk back to the dormitory. The streets are dark. I think about the math.

Three hundred clerks. Twenty committed to organizing. Fifty interested. That leaves two hundred thirty who haven't been approached or who refused. How do you reach two hundred thirty people when conversation is forbidden, when supervisors watch constantly, when everyone is isolated at their desk?

You can't.

The coordination barrier isn't just surveillance. It's structure. Three hundred desks in rows. Supervisors circulating. Fifteen-minute breaks with a hundred people crowded around water and rice. No time to talk, no space to gather, no way to build trust.

The system is designed to prevent coordination.

Over the next two weeks, I watch Zhao try. He approaches people during breaks, whispers quickly, moves on. Some nod. Some shake their heads. Some look away.

I see him approach the clerk at desk 245. The clerk listens, then walks away quickly. I see him approach the clerk at desk 198. The clerk refuses to make eye contact.

I see him approach the clerk at desk 312. They talk for thirty seconds. Then Supervisor Liu appears. Zhao stops talking. The clerk returns to his rice.

The surveillance isn't constant, but it's enough. You can't organize when any conversation might be observed, any gathering might be reported.

On the fourteenth day, Zhao finds me in the courtyard. "We have forty-three people. We need more."

"How many more?"

"At least fifty. Ideally a hundred."

"You're not going to get a hundred."

"Why not?"

I watch the other clerks eating. They're hunched over their bowls, faces blank. Some of them Zhao has approached. Some refused. Some are still considering.

But I see something else now. The clerk at desk 302—Zhao approached him last week. He nodded, seemed interested. Now he's eating alone in the corner, not making eye contact with anyone. The clerk at desk 267—also approached, also interested. He's eating quickly, glancing at the supervisors.

They understand Zhao's argument. They know they're drowning. But they're calculating: if I join and we fail, I'm dismissed immediately. If I don't join, I have three more months. Maybe four.

The system doesn't just prevent coordination through surveillance. It makes individual survival strategies always slightly more rational than collective action. Even when drowning, three more months of air feels better than zero months.

"Because," I say to Zhao, "helping each other means drowning faster."

He stares at me. "So we just accept it?"

"I don't know. But I know that every person you approach is doing the same calculation. And the calculation says: save yourself."

"The calculation is wrong. We can't save ourselves individually."

"I know. But the system has taught us that trying to save ourselves individually is the only option. Even when it doesn't work. Even when we know it doesn't work."

Zhao is silent.

That night, there's another meeting. Forty-three people attend. Zhao proposes moving forward with the work stoppage. Some people agree. Some want to wait for more support. Some want to abandon the plan.

The debate continues for an hour. No consensus emerges.

Finally, someone says: "What if we're caught before we even start? What if someone reports us?"

Silence.

"We've been meeting for two weeks," the person continues. "We've approached over a hundred people. Any one of them could report us. Any one of them could tell a supervisor. We might already be compromised."

More silence.

Zhao says: "We have to take that risk. If we don't act, we're definitely crushed. If we act, we might be crushed, but we might also succeed."

"Those aren't good odds."

"They're the only odds we have."

Another clerk speaks. "But are they? I'm behind quota too. I'm accumulating debt. But if I join this and we fail—I'm dismissed immediately. If I don't join, I have maybe three more months. Maybe four. That's three more months to figure something out. To save money. To—"

"To what?" Zhao interrupts. "What are you going to figure out in three months that you haven't figured out in three years?"

The clerk doesn't answer.

The meeting ends without decision. People leave quietly, separately, returning to the dormitory in darkness.

I walk back with Zhao. He's silent, defeated.

"You were right," I say. "We're all drowning. But we can't help each other. Because helping each other means drowning faster."

"So we just accept it?"

"What else can we do?"

He doesn't answer.

Three days later, Supervisor Liu calls me to his desk.

"Wei Zhen, you were seen at a tea house on Dongcheng Street. Multiple times. With other clerks. Explain."

My throat tightens. "We were drinking tea, supervisor."

"Why?"

"To relax. After work."

"Clerks are not permitted to leave the dormitory after evening bell."

"I didn't know that, supervisor."

"You did know that. It's in the regulations you signed when you started service."

I say nothing.

"You were meeting to discuss organizing a work stoppage. Is that correct?"

"No, supervisor."

"Don't lie. We have reports from multiple sources. You were planning to refuse work until the quota was lowered. Is that correct?"

I keep my face blank. "No, supervisor."

Liu looks at me for a long moment. "Wei Zhen, you have been a clerk for five years. Your performance has been declining. Your debt is currently ninety-two taels. You are on track for dismissal within three months. Do you understand this?"

"Yes, supervisor."

"And yet you chose to participate in organizing activities that would result in immediate dismissal. Why?"

I don't answer.

"I'll tell you why," Liu says. "Because you're desperate. Because you see no other option. Because you think collective action might save you."

He pauses. "It won't. The system is designed to prevent exactly this kind of coordination. We have three hundred clerks working in isolation. We have supervisors monitoring constantly. We have regulations prohibiting gathering. We have informants among the clerks themselves. You cannot organize. The structure prevents it."

He pulls out a document. "This is a list of everyone who attended the meetings. Forty-three names. All of them will be called in for counseling. All of them will be warned. If any of them continue organizing activities, they will be dismissed immediately."

He slides the document across the desk. I see Zhao's name at the top. I see my name. I see forty-one others.

"You're not being dismissed today," Liu says. "You're being given a chance. Return to your desk. Meet your quota. Pay your debt. Serve the empire. That's your only option."

"Yes, supervisor."

"You're dismissed."

I return to my desk. I pick up my brush. My hand is shaking.

That night, I lie on my mat. Around me, other clerks sleep. I think about Zhao, about the forty-three names, about the meetings in the tea house.

We tried to coordinate. We tried to build collective action. We tried to create an alternative.

The system crushed us before we started.

Not through violence. Not through punishment. Through structure. Through isolation, surveillance, fear. Through making coordination impossible. Through making individual survival always slightly more rational than collective action.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

The next morning, Zhao is not at his desk. I ask the clerk next to him where he went.

"Dismissed," the clerk whispers. "Last night. They called him in, gave him his final accounting, told him to leave."

"What about the others?"

"I don't know. I think some were dismissed. Some were warned."

I sit at desk 287. I pick up my brush. My hand trembles.

I work.

At midday, I complete twenty-three folios. Twenty-five to go. The math doesn't work.

But I work anyway.

Because there is no alternative.

## Part Six: Chen

The quarterly Board review is scheduled for the hour of the snake. I arrive carrying three years of performance data.

The Board chamber is full. All twelve members are present, plus Chief Wang and three other senior verification officers. I'm here to present the quarterly performance analysis.

I distribute the reports. The Board members scan the numbers.

"Summary?" the Director asks.

I stand. "Overall performance has declined five percent compared to last quarter. Average folio completion is forty-two per day, down from forty-four. Dismissal rate is fifteen percent annually, up from twelve percent."

"Why?"

"Physical degradation. The quota of forty-eight folios is—" I pause. My throat tightens. "The quota of forty-eight folios exceeds the sustained capacity of most clerks. Hand injuries are increasing. Dismissals are accelerating."

One of the financial officers speaks. "But total output is increasing. We're completing more folios per day than we did before the quota increase."

"Yes. Because we hired thirty new clerks. But those new clerks will—" I stop. The words are there but won't come. Will fail within three years. Will be dismissed with debt. Will be replaced by other clerks who will also fail.

The Director looks at me. "Officer Chen, are you unwell?"

"No, Director."

"Continue your report."

I look at the numbers. Average completion: forty-two folios per day. Dismissal rate: fifteen percent. Average tenure: six years, down from eight years, down from twelve years twenty years ago.

I look at the Board members. They're waiting for me to continue.

I look at my hands. They're shaking.

"Officer Chen?"

I set the papers on the table. "I've been reviewing historical data. Twenty years ago, the quota was forty folios per day. Dismissal rate was five percent annually. Average tenure was twelve years."

The Director nods. "We're aware of the historical trends."

"Then you're aware that we're not maintaining a stable system. We're accelerating extraction. Every quota increase pushes more clerks toward failure. Every failure creates debt. Every debt—"

I stop. My throat is tight. I can't finish the sentence.

"Every debt is a contractual obligation that the clerk agreed to," the Director says. "We're not forcing anyone to borrow money. We're providing a service to clerks who need financial assistance."

I look at him. I try to speak. The words won't come.

He continues: "Officer Chen, you seem troubled by the performance trends. But you must understand: the system is designed to identify capable individuals and promote them. You were a clerk for fourteen years. You met the quota. You were promoted. The system rewarded your merit. That's how it's supposed to work."

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out.

"The clerks who fail," the Director continues, "are not being punished. They're simply not meeting the standard. The standard exists for a reason. The empire requires accurate records. The quotas reflect that requirement. If a clerk cannot meet the quota, then the clerk is not suitable for this work. That's not cruelty. That's selection."

I try to speak. My hands are shaking. My throat is closed.

The Director waits. The Board members wait.

I pick up my papers. My hands are shaking so badly the papers rattle.

"Officer Chen, do you need a moment?"

I stand. The chair scrapes against the floor. The sound is too loud.

"Officer Chen?"

I walk toward the door. My legs are unsteady.

"Officer Chen, we're not finished with the review."

I reach the door. I open it. I walk out.

Behind me, the Director's voice: "Officer Chen, if you leave this meeting, you'll be considered in violation of your duties."

I keep walking.

## Part Seven: Wei

Feng's left hand fails on a Thursday morning.

I'm in the courtyard during afternoon break when I see him. He's sitting against the wall. Both hands are in his lap. Both hands are claws.

I sit beside him. "When?"

"This morning. Hour of the snake. I was copying with my left hand. It cramped. It didn't release."

"The physician?"

"Gave me liniment. Said there's nothing else he can do."

We sit in silence. Around us, other clerks eat quickly, faces blank.

"How many days until your debt is due?" I ask.

"Eight."

"How much do you owe?"

"Sixty-three taels."

"Can you pay?"

"No."

"What will you do?"

"I don't know."

The bell rings. We return to our desks. Feng sits at his desk but doesn't pick up his brush. He can't pick up his brush. His hands are claws.

Supervisor Liu appears. "Problem?"

"No, supervisor."

"Then why aren't you working?"

"I can't hold the brush, supervisor."

Liu looks at Feng's hands. "Go to the medical office."

"I've been to the medical office. They can't help."

"Then you're dismissed. Collect your belongings. Report to administrative office for final accounting."

Feng doesn't move. "I have eight days remaining on my debt repayment period."

"You're dismissed effective immediately. The debt is still due in eight days."

"But I can't work. How am I supposed to earn money to repay the debt?"

"That's not the archives' concern. You borrowed money. You agreed to repay it. The terms are clear."

Feng stands. His legs are unsteady. He walks down the aisle. He passes my desk. He doesn't look at me.

That night, I find him in the dormitory. He's sitting on his mat, his belongings in a small bundle beside him. His hands rest in his lap like dead things.

I sit beside him. "Where will you go?"

"I don't know."

"Family?"

"All dead."

"Friends?"

"You."

We sit in silence. Around us, other clerks prepare for sleep.

"I've been thinking," Feng says. "Forty years. Four hundred thousand folios. What did it mean?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I. I copied tax records, legal contracts, administrative reports. I copied them accurately. I met my quota. I served the empire. And now my hands are claws and I owe sixty-three taels and I have nowhere to go."

He pauses. "What did it mean?"

I don't have an answer.

"I thought," Feng continues, "that if I worked hard, if I was reliable, if I served faithfully—the system would take care of me. Not reward me, necessarily. But at least not destroy me."

"The system doesn't take care of anyone."

"I know that now. But I believed it for forty years. I believed that my service mattered. That my work had meaning. That the empire valued my contribution."

"Did it?"

"No. The empire valued my output. When my output declined, I became disposable. That's all I ever was. A mechanism for producing folios. When the mechanism broke, it was discarded."

He looks at his hands. "Forty years. And this is the end."

"It doesn't have to be the end."

"What else is there?"

I think about this. "You could leave. Go somewhere else. Find different work."

"With these hands? What work can I do?"

"I don't know. But staying here—they'll put you in debtor's prison. Or worse."

"I know."

"Then leave."

"Where?"

"Anywhere. Just leave."

He's silent for a long moment. Then: "I tried. Three weeks ago. I made it to the south gate. I stood there for an hour. I watched people coming and going. Merchants, farmers, travelers. People who knew where they were going. People who knew how to be something."

He pauses. "I stood there and I realized: I don't know how to be anything except a clerk. Forty years in the Long Hall. Forty years at a desk. Forty years copying. That's all I am. I don't know how to think like a merchant. I don't know how to work like a farmer. I don't know how to move through the world like a person who isn't a clerk."

"You could learn."

"Could I? Or has forty years of institutional life destroyed that capacity? The system didn't just train my hands to hold a brush. It trained my mind to think in quotas, in folios, in debts. It trained my body to move from dormitory to desk to courtyard to dormitory. It trained me to be a mechanism. And now the mechanism is broken but I'm still a mechanism. I don't know how to be anything else."

He looks at me. "So I came back. Because even though this is killing me, even though it's already killed me, I don't know how to imagine anything else. The system destroyed that capacity along with my hands."

I sit with him in the silence.

"Will you come with me?" he asks finally.

I think about this. I think about my desk, my quota, my debt. I think about two more months of copying, two more months of falling behind.

I think about my father's land. Two mu. He sold half of it to pay my examination fees. If I'm dismissed, they'll take the rest.

I think about Feng at the south gate, unable to walk through it. Forty years of institutional life destroying not just his hands but his capacity to imagine alternatives.

"I can't," I say.

"Why not?"

"Because if I leave, they'll go after my family. If I stay, I have two more months. Maybe three."

"And then they'll go after your family anyway."

"I know."

"So what's the difference?"

"Two months. Maybe three."

"Is that worth it?"

I don't answer.

Feng stands. "I'm leaving tomorrow. Before dawn. I'll walk south. I don't know where I'll go. I don't know what I'll do. But I can't stay here."

He picks up his bundle. "Even if I don't know how to be anything else. Even if the system destroyed that capacity. I have to try."

He lies down on his mat. I return to mine.

That night, I lie in darkness. Around me, other clerks sleep. I listen to their breathing.

Feng is right. If I stay, I'll be dismissed in two months. My debt will be called. My family will lose their land. If I leave now, the same thing happens. The difference is two months of copying.

Is that worth it?

I think about my father. He sold his land to pay my examination fees. He believed I would succeed. He believed the system would reward merit.

If I leave now, his sacrifice means nothing. If I stay and get dismissed, his sacrifice means nothing. Either way, his sacrifice means nothing.

Because the system doesn't reward merit. It extracts labor until bodies break. Then it discards the bodies and extracts more labor from new bodies.

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

The next morning, the bell rings at the hour of the rabbit. I wake in darkness. I dress in darkness. I walk through darkness to the Long Hall.

Feng's mat is empty. His bundle is gone.

I sit at desk 287. I pick up my brush. My hand trembles.

I work.

At midday, Supervisor Liu calls me to his desk.

"Wei Zhen, Feng Chen-wei left the dormitory last night without authorization. Do you know where he went?"

"No, supervisor."

"Did he tell you he was leaving?"

"No, supervisor."

"You were seen talking to him last night."

"We talked. He didn't tell me he was leaving."

Liu looks at me for a long moment. "If you're lying, you'll be dismissed immediately."

"I'm not lying, supervisor."

"Feng owes sixty-three taels. If we discover you helped him escape, you'll be held responsible for his debt in addition to your own."

My throat tightens. "I didn't help him, supervisor."

"Then return to your desk."

I return to my desk. I pick up my brush. My hand is shaking.

I work.

That night, I lie on my mat. Feng's mat is still empty. I think about him walking south in darkness, his hands useless, his debt unpaid. I think about where he'll go, what he'll do, whether he'll find a way to survive outside the structure that destroyed him.

I think about his question: "Forty years. What did it mean?"

I don't have an answer.

I think about my own five years. What do they mean? Swollen hands, ninety-two taels of debt, two months until dismissal. My father's land sold. His sacrifice wasted.

Five years. And for what?

I close my eyes. I don't sleep.

The next morning, the bell rings at the hour of the rabbit. I wake in darkness. I dress in darkness. I walk through darkness to the Long Hall.

A new clerk sits at Feng's desk. He is young, maybe eighteen. He copies with careful strokes. He believes precision matters.

I sit at desk 287. I pick up my brush. My hand trembles.

I work.

At midday, I complete twenty-one folios. Twenty-seven to go. The math doesn't work.

But I work anyway.

Because there is no alternative.

## Part Eight: Chen

The morning after I walk out of the Board meeting, I wake at the hour of the rabbit. My wife is already in the kitchen. I dress slowly. My hands are steady.

I walk to the archives. The morning is cool. The streets are quiet.

I arrive at the verification office. Chief Wang is at his desk. He looks up when I enter.

"Officer Chen. The Director wants to see you."

"I know."

"He's waiting in his office."

I don't go to the Director's office. I go to my desk. I pull the files for desks 280-294.

Wei Zhen: 39 folios yesterday. Nine short of quota. Debt: 94 taels.
Desk 283: 41 folios. Seven short. Debt: 67 taels.
Desk 289: 38 folios. Ten short. Debt: 103 taels.

I open my ledger. I begin calculating dismissal timelines. Wei Zhen: six weeks. Desk 283: eight weeks. Desk 289: four weeks.

Chief Wang appears at my desk. "Officer Chen, the Director is waiting."

"I'm completing my verification work."

"The Director said immediately."

"I'm completing my verification work."

Wang hesitates. Then he returns to his desk.

I continue calculating. Desk 291: ten weeks. Desk 292: twelve weeks. Desk 294: three weeks.

I pull the historical data. Twenty years ago: average tenure twelve years, dismissal rate five percent, quota forty folios. Fifteen years ago: average tenure ten years, dismissal rate eight percent, quota forty-two folios. Ten years ago: average tenure eight years, dismissal rate twelve percent, quota forty-five folios.

Current: average tenure six years, dismissal rate fifteen percent, quota forty-eight folios.

I write the numbers in my ledger. I calculate the trend line. In five years: average tenure four years, dismissal rate twenty percent, quota fifty-one folios.

The system is accelerating. Not maintaining. Accelerating.

I pull the paper quality reports. Current degradation cycle: ten years. Historical degradation cycle: thirty years. The decline began eight years ago when the Board switched suppliers to reduce costs.

I calculate: documents copied today will be illegible in ten years. They will need to be recopied. Those copies will be illegible in ten years. They will need to be recopied again.

Permanent cycle. Permanent extraction. Nothing preserved except the structure.

I pull the loan data. Total outstanding clerk debt: 47,000 taels. Average debt per clerk: 157 taels. Monthly interest collected: 940 taels. Annual interest collected: 11,280 taels.

The archives' annual budget surplus: 3,000 taels.

The loan program generates more revenue than the archives' entire operational surplus.

I write this in my ledger.

Chief Wang appears again. "Officer Chen, the Director has requested your immediate presence. If you don't comply, you'll be considered in violation of your duties."

I look at him. "I'm completing my verification work."

"Your verification work can wait."

"No. It can't."

Wang stares at me. Then he leaves.

I continue working. I pull Wei Zhen's file. I read his performance history. Year one: 46 folios average. Year two: 45. Year three: 44. Year four: 43. Year five: 41.

Steady decline. Two folios per year. Predictable. Inevitable.

I pull my own file from fourteen years ago. Year one: 47 folios average. Year two: 46. Year three: 46. Year four: 45. Year five: 45. Years six through fourteen: 45.

My decline stopped at year three. Wei Zhen's decline continued.

Why?

I examine the medical reports. Wei Zhen: hand cramping noted in year three. My file: no hand cramping noted until year eight.

Why?

I don't know. Genetic variation. Luck. The particular documents assigned. The particular angle of the lamp. The particular chair. The particular weather. A thousand small variables that determine whether a body breaks in three years or eight years.

Not merit. Not capability. Not effort.

Luck.

I was promoted because my body held up longer. That's all.

I write this in my ledger.

The Director appears at my desk. "Officer Chen, you walked out of a Board meeting yesterday. You ignored multiple requests to report to my office. You are in violation of your duties. Explain yourself."

I look up at him. "I'm completing my verification work."

"Your verification work is not more important than Board meetings."

"Yes it is."

He stares at me. "Excuse me?"

I gesture at the files on my desk. "Fifteen clerks. Desks 280-294. Average debt: 89 taels. Average completion rate: 41 folios per day. Quota: 48 folios per day. Average time to dismissal: seven weeks."

I pause. "Seven weeks. And then their debt is called. And then their families lose their land. Fifteen families. Seven weeks."

"Those clerks agreed to the terms of employment. They agreed to the quota. They agreed to the loan terms. No one forced them."

"No one forced them," I repeat. "They passed the examination. They were assigned desks. They were given quotas they cannot meet. They borrowed money to pay fines for failing to meet quotas they cannot meet. And now they will be dismissed and their families will lose their land. But no one forced them."

The Director's voice is cold. "Officer Chen, you are describing the standard operation of the archives. If you have a problem with standard operations, you should have raised it through proper channels."

"I did raise it. Yesterday. In the Board meeting. I asked about the permanent copying cycle. I asked about the quota increases. I asked—"

"You asked inappropriate questions and then walked out of the meeting."

"The questions weren't inappropriate. The system is inappropriate."

Silence.

The Director says quietly: "Officer Chen, you were a clerk for fourteen years. You met the quota. You were promoted. The system rewarded your merit. And now you're suggesting the system is inappropriate?"

"I met the quota because my body held up longer. Not because I had more merit. Not because I worked harder. Because I was lucky."

"That's not—"

"It is. I've reviewed the data. My performance history matches Wei Zhen's performance history for the first three years. Then his decline continued and mine stopped. The difference is medical. Not behavioral. Not merit-based. Medical."

I pull out the historical data. "Twenty years ago, average tenure was twelve years. Now it's six years. Twenty years ago, dismissal rate was five percent. Now it's fifteen percent. The system isn't maintaining a stable workforce. It's accelerating extraction. Every quota increase pushes more clerks toward failure. Every failure generates debt. Every debt generates interest. The loan program generates more revenue than the archives' operational surplus."

I pause. "The system isn't designed to reward merit. It's designed to extract labor until bodies break, then extract wealth from broken bodies through debt."

The Director's face is blank. "Officer Chen, you are relieved of your duties. Effective immediately. Report to the administrative office for final accounting."

"I'm not finished with my verification work."

"You are finished. Leave now or I will have you removed."

I look at the files on my desk. Fifteen clerks. Seven weeks.

I stand. My legs are steady.

I walk out of the verification office. I walk past the Long Hall. I walk through the gate.

I don't know where I'm going. I just walk.
