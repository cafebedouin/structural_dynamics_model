# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## REVISED NARRATIVE

# THE SEPARATION

## I.

The drill bit screams through ice older than human civilization, and Kira feels it in her teeth. Sixty meters down in Europa's crust, the vibration travels up through the rig, through her suit, through bone. She's been on surface for four months now. Two more until rotation back to Ganymede.

Her radiation counter ticks. Always ticking. 540 rem per deployment, the Consortium says. Safe enough if you don't plan to reproduce. Which you don't. Which you can't. Which the implant in your arm makes sure of, checked quarterly, removal requires approval you'll never get.

"Flow rate dropping," Lena says through the comm. "Adjust angle five degrees."

Kira makes the correction. The drill bites deeper. Ice becomes water becomes commodity becomes the reason they're all here, 628 million kilometers from Earth, trapped in a rotation that never ends: six months surface, six months station, twenty years until payout. She's twelve years in. The number sits in her chest like a stone.

The ice gives. Water floods the collection system. Lena marks it in the log. Another quota met. Another day closer to something that might be freedom.

"Good work," Lena says. "Shift change in six hours."

Six hours. Then the transport back to Ganymede, back to the drums, back to 1.2g that'll make her legs ache for the first three days. Back to the Compact.

Back to enforcement.

---

The thing they don't tell you about high gravity is that standing becomes a decision. Sitting becomes a calculation. Every movement costs, and you learn to budget the cost the way you budget oxygen, water, time. Kira's been back in Drum Section 7 for eight hours, and her back already remembers why she hates rotation week.

She finds Lena in the common area, staring at the viewport. The hub floats beyond the glass: a sphere of gardens and open space and 0.3g luxury. You can see it from anywhere in the drums. You can never reach it.

"We need to talk," Kira says.

Lena doesn't turn. "I know."

"You were looking at him during shift change. Marcus saw. If Marcus saw, others saw."

"I was just—"

"Doesn't matter what you were doing. Matters what it looked like." Kira sits, feels the chair take her weight. "How long?"

"Three weeks." Lena's voice is small. "He asked if we could talk. Just talk. I said no. But I wanted to say yes."

"Who is he?"

"Yuri. From Section 4. Ice processor. I worked with him before the Compact. Before everything." Lena finally turns. Her eyes are red. "It's been three years, Kira. Three years."

1,095 days. Forty-seven women. Zero defections.

"I know," Kira says. "I know what it costs. You think I don't feel it?"

"Then why—"

"Because it's working." Kira keeps her voice level. "Three years ago, we had nothing. No leverage. No voice. Now? Tanaka's people are asking questions. Running audits. Looking for ways to 'resolve the operational disruption.' That's us. We're the disruption. And the only reason they're talking to us instead of just waiting us out is because we've held."

"For how much longer?"

"Until they change the policy. Until Extractors can apply for conception approval like Coordinators can." Kira leans forward, feels the 1.2g pull. "The Separation trapped us here. We turned the trap into a foundation. Forty-seven of us, all standing in the same place, all pulling the same direction. That's the only power we have. The moment one of us breaks, the foundation cracks."

Lena looks back at the viewport. The hub rotates slowly, gardens visible through the glass. "What if I can't hold?"

"You can. You have. For 1,095 days." Kira stands, feels her knees protest. "Yuri's been trapped for the same 1,095 days. I'm not saying it doesn't hurt him. I'm saying it hurts all of us, and we're carrying it together. That's the difference between a trap and a tool. We chose this. We maintain it. We end it when it gets us what we need."

"And if it never does?"

Kira doesn't answer. Because she doesn't know. Because three years is a long time to climb a rope that might not have an end.

"It will," Kira says, and leaves before Lena can see the lie.

---

## II.

Three years earlier, the meeting happens in a place that doesn't exist.

Cargo Bay 3, off-shift, 0200 hours. Thirty women at first—the ones who've been talking in small groups, the ones who've been asking the same question in different words: *Why can Coordinators apply for conception approval but we can't?*

Kira stands near the bay door, watching faces. Some she knows. Some she's only seen at shift changes. All of them tired. All of them trapped by the same architecture: drums to surface to drums, 1.2g constant load, twenty-year contracts, reproduction prohibited.

"We can't meet like this," someone says. Irina, ice processor, Section 4. "Security scans. They'll know."

"They already know," Kira says. "Question is what we do about it."

"What can we do?" Another voice. Younger woman, six years in. "The policy's been in place since 2067. It's not changing."

"It's not changing because nobody's made them change it." Kira moves to the center of the bay. "The prohibition applies to all Extractors. The approval pathway applies to all Coordinators. Same station. Different access. The difference is the Separation—where you live determines what you can request."

"The radiation exposure is real," someone says. "I've seen the medical data. 540 rem per deployment. That's genetic damage territory."

"For us. Not for them." Kira looks around the bay. "Coordinators never deploy to Europa surface. They face negligible exposure. But they're the ones with approval access. We're the ones doing the work that creates the exposure, and we're the ones prohibited from applying."

Silence. The bay hums with ventilation. Somewhere in the station, shift change is happening. People moving from drums to industrial modules, from modules back to drums. The rotation that never stops.

"So what do we do?" Irina asks.

Kira doesn't answer immediately. Because the thing she's about to propose will trap people. Not the Consortium—they're already trapped by their own architecture. The men. The ones who aren't in this bay, who don't know this meeting is happening, who will wake up tomorrow to find the world changed.

"We organize," Kira says. "All of us. Collective action. We stop cooperating. With the prohibition. With the asymmetry."

"How?" Multiple voices now, overlapping.

"We maintain distance. All of us. Coordinated. No exceptions. We make the prohibition visible by making its cost visible. Right now, the Consortium extracts reproduction from us individually—one person prohibited, another person prohibited, each of us isolated in the trap. We collectivize the trap. Make it everyone's problem instead of each person's problem."

"That's—" Someone stops. Starts again. "That traps them too. The men. The ones who aren't making policy."

"Yes," Kira says.

The bay is very quiet.

Kira waits. Lets the silence hold. Lets them see what she's proposing without dressing it up.

She doesn't say: *And the costs for them will be worse than the costs for us, because we chose this and they didn't.*

She doesn't say: *We're about to make forty-seven men pay for a policy they have no voice in changing.*

She doesn't say: *This is the only way I can see, and I hate that it's the only way.*

"They're already trapped," she says finally. "Same prohibition, same architecture, same lack of leverage. The difference is: we can organize. We have the numbers. Forty-seven women in the drums, all subject to the same constraint. We coordinate, we maintain, we create operational disruption the Consortium can't ignore."

"But they can't organize," Irina says. "The men. They're scattered across sections, no shared infrastructure, no collective leverage point."

"No," Kira says. "They can't."

More silence. Longer this time.

"You're saying we use them." Lena. She's been silent until now. "We create pressure by making their situation impossible."

"I'm saying we use the only leverage we have." Kira's voice is steady. "The Separation created the trap. The Consortium maintains the trap. We're turning the trap into a tool. The tool has costs. For us. For them. For everyone except the people in the hub who designed this architecture."

She says: "We vote. Everyone here. And if we vote yes, we commit. No individual defections. No exceptions. We maintain collective action until we achieve the objective or until we collectively decide to dissolve. The only way this works is if all of us hold."

They vote. Hands raised in a cargo bay at 0200 hours, off-shift, in a place that doesn't officially exist.

Thirty women become thirty-one. Become thirty-five. Become forty-two.

By the end of the week, forty-seven.

Zero defections.

Kira watches them leave—small groups, staggered timing, nothing that looks like a meeting. She stays until the bay is empty.

Then she sits on the floor, back against the wall, and thinks about Yuri. About Dmitri. About Sergei and Marcus and all the others whose names she knows, whose faces she knows, who will wake up tomorrow and find themselves trapped by something she built.

She thinks: *They'll hate us.*

She thinks: *They should.*

She doesn't move for a long time.

## III.

Dmitri's hands won't stop shaking.

It started during the shift—just a tremor in his right hand, nothing that affected the work. Ice processing is mostly monitoring: watch the temperature gradients, adjust the flow rates, make sure nothing crystallizes in the wrong pipes. He's been doing it for fourteen years. His body knows the rhythm.

But today his body forgot.

He's in his quarters now, shared space with fifteen others, curtains for privacy, acoustic only. He sits on his bunk and stares at his hands. The tremor's worse. Not a medical thing—he knows what those look like, has seen them in other men who've been here too long, bodies starting to fail under the constant load. This is different.

This is the third year.

His wrist counter reads 47 rem accumulated this rotation. Well below danger threshold. The implant scan came back clean last month. Everything by the numbers. Everything compliant.

Everything trapped.

"You okay?" Sergei asks from the next bunk. Older guy, six months left on his contract. Almost out.

"Fine," Dmitri says.

"You look like shit."

"I'm fine."

Sergei doesn't push. Nobody pushes anymore. There's nothing to say that hasn't been said a thousand times in the past three years.

The tremor spreads to his left hand.

Dmitri thinks about the first year. The rage. The certainty that it couldn't last, that someone would defect, that the whole thing would collapse under its own weight. He tried negotiating. Went to Irina—one of the forty-seven, someone he'd worked with for years—and asked if they could just talk. She said no. Not unkind, but absolute.

He tried going through official channels. Requested psychological support services. Got a form letter suggesting "meditation and exercise protocols." He requested transfer to a different drum section. Denied—crew cohesion requirements. He requested early contract completion. Denied—no provision for voluntary termination without payout forfeiture.

The second year was despair. Watching the Compact hold. Watching the women maintain it through something that looked like magic but was actually just organization.

The third year is numbness. Mostly. Except for moments like this, when his body remembers what it wants and can't have, and the tremor starts, and he sits on his bunk staring at his hands like they belong to someone else.

Six years left. 2,190 days. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then freedom from this place, this rotation, this trap.

He just has to last.

The tremor doesn't stop.

---

Shift change. Dmitri moves through the transit tube with a hundred other Extractors, heading from drums to industrial modules. The tube is narrow, one-way flow, security scanners every fifty meters. His ID badge opens the checkpoints. It won't open the hub tubes. He's tried. Everyone's tried.

The Separation is architectural. It's also absolute.

He processes ice for twelve hours. Temperature gradients. Flow rates. Crystal formation prevention. His hands don't shake while he's working. The work is its own kind of numbness, and he's grateful for it.

During break, he sees her.

Irina. One of the forty-seven. She's in the next module over, checking readouts, making notes. She doesn't look at him. She hasn't looked at him in three years.

He remembers: they used to play chess. Bad chess, neither of them very good, but it passed the time. They used to share rations when supply shipments ran late. They used to talk about Earth—neither of them had ever been, but both their contracts promised citizenship at completion, so they talked about it like it was real. Moscow for him. Lagos for her. Places that existed in their heads as escape routes from this station, this rotation, this life.

Then the Compact formed. Then she stopped looking at him.

He wants to cross the module gap. He wants to ask: *Is it working? Is the leverage real? Are you getting what you need?* He wants to ask: *Does it hurt you the way it hurts me?*

He doesn't move.

The break ends. Irina leaves without looking at him. Dmitri goes back to the ice.

His hands don't shake until the shift ends.

---

## IV.

Amira Osei crosses the boundary for the third time this week.

The transit tube runs from hub to drums—0.3g to 1.2g, gardens to metal, space to confinement. Her compliance officer badge opens every door on the station. She's the only person who moves freely between sections.

Which means she's the only person who feels the difference.

The shift hits at the drum-side exit: her heart rate spikes, her legs remember weight, her inner ear recalibrates to gravity that pulls instead of suggests. She stands at the tube exit and breathes until the dizziness passes. Every crossing, the same adjustment. Every crossing, her body registers what the architecture does.

She has interviews scheduled. Three women, all denied applications. Standard follow-up protocol after policy modification.

She's been on Ganymede for twenty-one months now. Thirteen more until her rotation ends. Long enough to understand the station's physics. Long enough to notice what the reports don't say.

---

The first two interviews are routine. Emotional state: stable. Support systems: adequate. Understanding of decision rationale: demonstrated. Plans for appeal or reapplication: none at present. She notes it in their files. No intervention required.

The third interview is with Kira.

Kira arrives exactly on time. Sits down in the compliance office—drum section, 1.2g, air that tastes like metal. Waits.

Amira asks the standard questions. Emotional state. Support systems. Understanding of decision rationale. Plans for appeal or reapplication.

Kira answers in Consortium Standard. Perfect compliance. Zero affect.

Then Amira asks: "Do you feel the policy modification achieved the Compact's objective?"

Kira is quiet for a moment. Then: "We got equal access to a system that still says no. But now when they say no, they have to explain why. That's something."

"Is it enough?"

"It's what we could get." Kira looks at the viewport—drums visible beyond, hub visible above. "The Separation is still here. The architecture is still here. We're still in the drums. But we forced them to change the terms. That's leverage we didn't have before."

Amira makes notes. Standard documentation. Then she asks a question that's not in the protocol: "What did it cost?"

Kira looks at her. Really looks, like she's trying to see if Amira understands what she's asking.

"Three years," Kira says. "Forty-seven women. Zero defections until we chose to dissolve. We held because we held each other. The cost was daily. The cost was maintenance. The cost was enforcement—not of them, of us. Making sure none of us broke. Making sure none of us defected. Making sure we all kept climbing even when we couldn't see the top of the rope."

"And the men?"

"They were trapped by a tool we built. That was the point. The trap created pressure. The pressure created this." Kira gestures at the office, the station, the architecture. "We're not friends now. We're not enemies. We're just people who survived the same mechanism from different positions."

Amira writes: *Subject demonstrates structural awareness and realistic outcome assessment.*

She doesn't write: *Subject is exhausted.*

She doesn't write: *Subject climbed for three years and got documentation, not approval.*

She finishes the interview. Kira leaves. Amira sits in the compliance office and looks at her notes.

Then she pulls up her quarterly report. Types:

**CONSORTIUM COMPLIANCE AUDIT REPORT**  
**Ganymede Station, Jupiter Orbit**  
**Reporting Officer: Amira Osei**  
**Date: 2089.11.22**

She stops. Thinks about the crossing she made this morning. The way her body registered the shift from 0.3g to 1.2g. The way her legs remembered what weight felt like. The way she noticed Extractors in the drums standing differently than Coordinators in the hub—posture, gait, how long before they had to shift position.

Thinks about Dmitri Volkov's interview last month. Routine psychological check after reported tremor incidents. He'd sat across from her, hands folded to hide the shaking, and answered every question with perfect compliance. His radiation counter read 47 rem. His medical scans were clean. His contract compliance was perfect. His hands wouldn't stop shaking.

She'd asked: "Do you feel the situation is sustainable?"

He'd looked at her—really looked, like he was trying to see if she understood what she was asking. Then he'd said: "For six more years? I don't know. Ask me in six years."

She'd noted it in the file. *Subject demonstrates awareness of temporal constraint. No immediate intervention required.*

Thinks about the approval statistics. Forty-three applications processed since policy modification. Two approvals granted. Both Coordinators. Zero Extractors approved.

The radiation exposure data supports the denials. 540 rem per deployment. Twelve years average tenure. The math is clear.

The math has always been clear.

She types:

**SUMMARY:**

Policy modification converted prohibition into bureaucratic denial. Extractors gained equal access to application process. Coordinators maintain structural approval advantage through differential radiation exposure. Operational disruption resolved. Underlying asymmetry persists.

Application statistics: 43 processed, 2 approved (both Coordinator population, negligible surface exposure). Extractor denials justified per medical review: cumulative radiation exposure exceeds safe conception thresholds in 100% of cases.

Architectural note: Drum section maintains direct viewport access to hub section. Visual access to inaccessible space is continuous. Design rationale per original station documents: "maintains crew awareness of station totality." Practical effect: daily reminder of differential access.

The Separation remains operational. The architecture functions as designed.

She looks at what she wrote.

It's true.

It's also outside compliance officer scope.

She sits with it for a long time. Her office in the drums. The viewport shows the hub rotating above—gardens, open space, low gravity. She can see it from here. She can reach it with her badge.

The Extractors can see it too. They can't reach it at all.

She deletes what she wrote. Types:

**SUMMARY:**

Policy modification resolved operational disruption designated "Compact." Station stability restored. Compliance within acceptable parameters.

She submits the report.

Then she crosses back to the hub. The transit tube carries her from 1.2g to 0.3g. Her body forgets weight. Her legs forget pressure. By the time she reaches her quarters, the drums are just something she can see through the viewport.

She thinks: *I'm the only one who crosses. I'm the only one who feels the difference.*

She thinks: *That's not an accident.*

She doesn't put that in any report.

## V.

Director Tanaka reads the compliance report in her office—executive section, lowest gravity on the station, floor-to-ceiling viewports showing Jupiter's banded face. She's been with the Consortium for twenty-two years. She designed this operational structure.

She reads Amira's data. Policy modification successful. Stability restored. Compliance within acceptable parameters.

She opens the application statistics file. Forty-three processed. Two approved. Both Coordinators.

The numbers tell her: the architecture is working as designed.

She closes the file. Jupiter rotates beyond the glass. The drums rotate below.

The Consortium values operational stability above ideological consistency. The policy modification achieved stability. The station functions.

She has thirteen reports to review before end of shift.

---

## VI.

The meeting happens in Cargo Bay 3, off-shift, forty-seven women in a space designed for equipment storage. Kira stands in the center. Her legs ache. Her back aches. She's been standing for twenty minutes, explaining Tanaka's offer, and the 1.2g makes standing a decision she has to keep making.

"Equal access," she says. "Same approval pathway Coordinators use. Radiation review, medical clearance, standard processing. We apply, they evaluate, they approve or deny based on documented criteria."

"They'll deny everyone," someone says. Irina. "Radiation exposure is real. We all have it. They'll use the medical data to justify the same prohibition, just dressed up as individual review."

"Maybe," Kira says. "But they can't prohibit everyone without justification. The policy becomes visible. The asymmetry becomes documented. Right now, the prohibition is absolute and invisible. Under the new system, every denial has to be explained."

"That's not victory," Lena says. "That's bureaucracy."

"It's leverage," Kira says. "Different leverage. We're trading the Compact—which costs us daily, which we're all exhausted from maintaining—for access to a system that costs them. Every application they process, every review they conduct, every denial they document. That's labor. That's exposure. That's vulnerability."

"What about the men?" someone asks. "What about the people we've been—" She stops. Doesn't finish.

Kira finishes for her. "Trapping. Yes. The Compact trapped them. That was the point. The trap created pressure. The pressure created negotiation. The negotiation created this offer."

"And now we let them go?"

"Now we dissolve a tool that served its purpose." Kira looks around the cargo bay. Forty-seven faces, all exhausted. "The Compact held because we held it together. Every day for three years, we chose to maintain it. We can choose to end it."

"What if this is a trick?" someone asks. "What if we dissolve the Compact and they just reinstate the prohibition?"

"Then we organize again," Kira says. "We've done it once. We can do it again. But I don't think it's a trick. I think Tanaka is satisficing. She's tired of managing the disruption. She's willing to modify policy to restore stability. That's rational institutional behavior."

"Is it enough?" Lena asks.

Kira doesn't answer immediately.

"It's what we can get," she says finally. "Right now. With the leverage we have. We can hold out for more, but the Compact is eroding. I see it in every meeting. Every vote to hold passes by smaller margins. We're exhausted. The cost is real. And if we wait too long, the leverage runs out and we get nothing."

Silence. The cargo bay hums with ventilation, with the station's rotation, with the weight of 1.2g pressing down on forty-seven women who've been carrying something heavy for three years.

"Vote," someone says.

They vote. Hands raised. Forty-seven women, same as always. Kira doesn't look away from any of them.

Thirty-one for dissolution. Sixteen against.

The Compact ends.

## VII.

Kira's application takes four hours to complete.

Medical history. Radiation exposure documentation. Deployment records. Genetic screening results. Psychological evaluation. Contract status verification.

Every field requires evidence. Every claim requires documentation. The system doesn't accept statements; it accepts proof.

She pulls her medical file. Twelve years of scans, quarterly implant checks, radiation accumulation logs. The numbers are precise: 540 rem per deployment, six deployments per year, twelve years on station.

38,880 rem lifetime dose. The threshold for safe conception: 5,000 rem. She's seven times over.

The form has a section for mitigating factors. She writes: *All exposure from required surface operations. No voluntary exposure. All safety protocols followed per Consortium standards.*

The genetic screening section requires samples. She schedules the appointment. Three days for results. Then two weeks for review. Then the decision.

She submits the application. The system confirms receipt. Processing time: fourteen days.

She sits back. Her quarters feel smaller than usual. The curtain doesn't block sound—she can hear others moving, talking, existing in shared space. She can hear someone crying. Quiet, controlled, but unmistakable.

She doesn't know who. She doesn't ask.

---

Fourteen days later, the denial arrives.

**APPLICATION DECISION: DENIED**

**Rationale:** Cumulative radiation exposure (38,880 rem) exceeds safe conception threshold (5,000 rem) by factor of 7.78. Medical review indicates significant genetic damage risk. Approval cannot be granted per Consortium medical safety standards.

Kira reads it in her quarters. Alone. The curtain drawn.

She thinks about the two approvals. She heard about them through the network—informal communication, the kind that happens in shared quarters and shift changes. One Coordinator, Environmental Systems. One Extractor, administrative role, minimal surface exposure.

The exceptions that prove the mechanism.

Eight years left on her contract. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then Lagos, which exists in her head as an escape route from this station, this rotation, this architecture.

She thinks: *We climbed for three years. We got access to a process that documented our damage and called it medical review.*

She thinks: *The Separation is still here. The architecture is still here. We're still in the drums.*

She thinks: *Is that victory or surrender?*

She doesn't know.

## VIII.

Dmitri hears about it during shift change. The news travels through the drums faster than official channels, faster than Consortium announcements, faster than anything. Someone tells someone who tells someone, and within an hour, everyone knows.

The Compact is dissolving. The policy is changing. Equal access to conception approval.

His hands stop shaking.

It takes him a moment to notice. He's in the transit tube, heading to the industrial modules, and his hands are steady. The tremor that's been with him for months—gone. Just like that.

He doesn't feel relief. He doesn't feel anything. Just absence. The trap opened, and the first thing he notices is numbness.

The shift is normal. Temperature gradients, flow rates, crystal formation prevention. He processes ice for twelve hours. During break, he sees Irina in the next module over.

She looks at him.

For three years, she hasn't looked at him. Now she does. It's not an apology. It's not reconciliation. It's just acknowledgment. *You're here. I'm here. We both survived.*

He nods. She nods back. Then they both return to work.

---

After shift, he goes back to his quarters. Sits on his bunk. Looks at his hands. Steady.

He thinks about 1,095 days. About the trap that tightened, about the numbness that became his default state.

He thinks about the new policy. Equal access. Approval pathway. Radiation review.

He thinks: *I can apply.*

He doesn't apply.

Not because he doesn't want to. Not because the policy hasn't changed. Because he knows what the numbers say. Fourteen years on station. Fourteen years of exposure. The radiation is in his body the same way it's in theirs.

He thinks about three years. About Irina and Kira and all the others who organized, who held, who climbed a rope together while he was caught in the mechanism they built.

He thinks: *They weren't trying to trap me. They were trying to escape.*

He thinks: *Both things are true.*

But sitting on his bunk, hands finally steady after months of tremor, staring at the wall of shared quarters where fifteen other men are trying to sleep—he doesn't feel the truth of both things. He feels anger.

Not rage. Not the first-year fury that burned through him when the Compact formed. Just anger. Clean and steady and aimed at nothing he can name.

They organized because they had no other option. He understands that.

They used him as leverage in a negotiation he had no voice in. He understands that too.

Both things are true. Understanding doesn't make the anger go away.

His hands stay steady. That's something. After three years of tremor, after three years of numbness punctuated by moments when his body remembered what it wanted and couldn't have—his hands are steady.

He lies back on his bunk. Listens to the station rotate. Closes his eyes.

Six more years. 2,190 days. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then Moscow, which exists in his head as an escape route from this station, this rotation, this life.

The Compact is gone. The Separation remains. The architecture remains. He's still in the drums. The hub is still inaccessible. The 1.2g still makes standing a decision.

But the trap opened.

His hands don't shake.

The anger doesn't go away.

---

## IX.

Six weeks after dissolution, Kira stands at the viewport in Drum Section 7, looking at the hub. Gardens visible through the glass. Open space. Natural light. People who move freely because their badges make the doors open.

She's been back from surface rotation for four weeks. Her legs have stopped aching. Her back hasn't.

The Compact dissolved six weeks ago. The enforcement meetings stopped. The collective strategy sessions stopped. The daily coordination that took forty-seven people acting as one—stopped.

She feels the absence. Not relief—absence. The rope she was climbing with forty-six other people disappeared, and now she's standing on a platform that's still inside the drums, still inside the Separation, still inside the architecture that trapped her twelve years ago.

But the platform is higher than where she started.

She applied for conception approval. Standard process, medical review, radiation exposure assessment. The denial came back in fourteen days: exposure levels exceed safe thresholds, genetic damage risk documented, approval cannot be granted at this time.

She'd expected it. The radiation exposure is real. She's been on Europa surface for twelve years. Her body carries the accumulated dose.

But the denial was documented. The rationale was explained. The process was accessible.

Three years ago, there was no process. Just prohibition. Just: *You cannot, because we say you cannot.*

Now there's: *You cannot, because the radiation exposure you accumulated during the work we required you to do has made it medically unsafe.*

It's still no. But it's a different no. A no that exposes the mechanism.

She thinks about the other women. Forty-six of them, scattered across the drums, back to their shifts and their crews and their lives. Some applied for approval. Some didn't. The ones who applied mostly got denials—radiation exposure, same as her.

But two got approvals. One Coordinator, Environmental Systems. One Extractor, administrative role, minimal surface exposure.

The exceptions that prove the mechanism.

She doesn't envy them. Doesn't resent them. Just notices: the system still sorts people. Still separates those who can from those who cannot. Still makes some bodies acceptable and others damaged.

But the sorting is visible now. That's the difference. That's what three years of climbing bought.

She turns from the viewport. Her shift starts in two hours. Europa surface deployment in three months. Another 540 rem. Another six months in the ice.

Eight years left on her contract. Then payout. Then Earth citizenship. Then Lagos, which exists in her head as an escape route from this station, this rotation, this architecture.

She thinks: *I just have to last.*

But she's not climbing alone anymore. Forty-seven women proved they could organize, could maintain, could force negotiation. The Compact dissolved, but the knowledge remains: collective action works. Coordination is possible. The Separation can be leveraged.

Maybe they do it again someday. Maybe they don't. But they know they can.

She leaves the viewport. Walks through the drums—1.2g constant weight, recycled air, metal walls. The architecture that trapped her. The architecture she turned into a foundation. The architecture that remains.

Her legs ache. Her back aches. She has eight years left.

She keeps walking.
