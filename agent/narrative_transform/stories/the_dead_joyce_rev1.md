# THE PUMP-ROOM HOUR

## I.

The Rittmeister's lady was much occupied this season with her correspondence. Nobody asked with whom.

Klára knew the schedule the way she knew the shape of her own hands. The waters at their hour, the promenade beneath the colonnade's iron lace, her arm through Anton's, her face arranged to the particular warmth that greeted the Baroness Fischer's cool nod and the young Hoffmanns' eager one, calibrated separately, correctly, every time. Calling hours after that. Dinner in the evening, seated by rank, which meant seated beside Anton, always.

None of this required her attention.

What required her attention was the pump-room's last hour, after dark, when the crowd had gone home to dress for supper.

She could feel it coming from some way off — a lightening under the sternum, not unlike hunger, not unlike relief. At the pump-room the light through the glass roof went a particular color toward evening, a blue with iron in it, the color of the mineral water itself held up to a lamp. She never looked at a clock to confirm it. Her body kept the hour better than any clock in the establishment.

In her own room, with the door shut and Anton settled below with his brandy and Colonel Bittner's tedious opinions, she crossed to the writing-desk, opened the lower drawer, and touched the latch of the small rosewood box inside it.

It was not locked. It had never needed to be.

The latch was brass, gone a little green at the hinge, cold always, even in August. She touched it with two fingers. She did not open the box. She had not opened it in years.

She held the cold of it for the length of a held breath and let it go.

That was the whole of it.

She did not think his name. She had stopped needing to.

---

The Rittmeister Anton Vesely considered himself a fortunate man, and saw no reason his friends should think otherwise.

He had said as much to Oberst Řehák that morning, over the waters, the two of them observing the crowd assemble beneath the colonnade. "My wife wants for nothing," he had said — not boastfully, but as a simple accounting. "She has her house, her name, her position. I don't believe I've heard her complain of anything beyond the dressmaker's prices."

Řehák laughed, the dressmaker being a subject of general grievance that season, and the conversation moved on to young Hoffmann's engagement and the uncle's gambling debts.

Anton did not think of the remark again. Klára managed the house without complaint, dressed well within what he provided, was universally allowed to be a handsome and gracious woman for her years. He did privately calculate, that same morning, the sum he owed his tailor, and something worse to Reiner's apothecary — a coming economy in Klára's accounts he had not yet found words to raise. This ran alongside the remark to Řehák without either thought troubling the other. A man's ledgers were his own business. His wife's contentment was a separate account, and it stood, as far as he had ever had reason to audit it, comfortably in credit.

He did not think to ask whether the account was hers to keep or his to check. Nothing in his marriage had ever suggested there was a question.

He finished his water, grimaced once more for form's sake, and went to find his hat.

## II.

The difficulties, when they came, came in the ordinary way — a seating.

At the dinner given by the Baroness Fischer for her nephew's name-day, the Rittmeister Vesely found himself placed several places down, below a young district judge whose father had lately been made a councillor in Prague.

Anton said nothing at the table. He ate his soup, complimented the cook, laughed at the judge's mild joke about the water-cure with every appearance of a man at ease.

But he slept badly, and the next morning he waited a shade longer than usual among Dr. Reiner's little knot of callers, for the nod that would tell the room the Rittmeister remained a man of consequence whatever some Prague councillor's son might suppose.

Reiner gave him the nod eventually. Reiner believed he ran the fairest waiting-room in the kingdom. He did not notice that the judge's father's appointment had reached him by breakfast, nor that this was why the judge was seen first, nor that such adjustments moved through his waiting-room every week, invisible to him as adjustments, visible only as the natural sorting of deserving cases.

Anton got his nod. He felt the fatigue of correct standing settle into his feet, into his throat, worn from careful talk. His wife had not been at the Baroness's dinner — a slight headache — and if it crossed his mind that her absence had spared her a difficult evening, it did not cross far enough to become a thought he kept.

On his way out he noticed Frau Nemcová at the entrance to her pension-house, receiving two elderly sisters from Brno, both trunks and both women fussing over a lost hatbox. Her face wore, throughout the fuss, an expression of perfect, unhurried welcome, already halfway to charming two cross and travel-worn women into two who would tell their friends at home what a superior establishment it had been.

Anton thought, passing, that the woman had a genuinely good nature. He did not wonder what it cost her to produce that welcome so many times a season, with the bookings still short of what she needed for spring. He had never had to be liked in order to eat, and a man does not easily imagine a hunger he has never felt.

---

Klára had gone to the pension-house herself that week, on an errand about trimming, and while she waited she watched Frau Nemcová at the booking desk receive a query from a gentleman about rooms for the following week.

There were, in fact, no good rooms left. But Frau Nemcová's face went through no visible calculation — it arrived at warmth a half-beat before the question had even finished — and she said something about "seeing what might be managed" in a tone that promised nothing and reassured everything. The gentleman went away pleased, having secured no commitment whatsoever.

Klára recognized the work the way one musician recognizes another's fingering, differently made.

Frau Nemcová caught her eye once, briefly, while the gentleman was still gathering his hat and stick. Neither woman smiled at the other — the ordinary smile was otherwise engaged — and something passed between them that neither could have stated.

The trimming was fetched. Klára paid what was owed and went home to dress for the afternoon calls.

That night the light went its blue, and she crossed to the drawer and touched the cold brass, and this had nothing to do with Frau Nemcová at all, and everything else in her day had.

## III.

The last fortnight ran a little faster than the ones before it — the same fixed hours, the same promenade and dinner, but something in the light already thinning toward September, so that everyone hurried through the fixed points as though hurrying might make there be more of them left.

Anton found himself unusually restless. The gout was better; Reiner had adjusted the waters and pronounced him improved, which pleased him disproportionately. The matter of the seating resolved itself in the ordinary way — a return dinner of his own restored the visible order of things.

It was one of the last evenings, unseasonably cool, when he came up to Klára's room later than his custom and found her door not quite latched, a thread of candlelight along its edge.

He had not meant to observe anything. But the door gave a little under his hand, and there she was at the writing-desk with the lower drawer open, her fingers resting on something small and brass he had never once seen.

"Klára," he said. Not sharply. He was not a jealous man; jealousy required a suspicion he had never had grounds to form.

She did not startle. That surprised him more, afterward, than the box itself — that she simply lifted her hand from the latch and turned to him with the same face she wore at the promenade, except that something in it had not yet finished arriving.

"It is nothing," she said, which was true, and which he did not believe — not because he suspected a lie but because a man does not easily credit that a silence over an unremarkable box could contain, truly, nothing.

He might have let it go. But he was tired in a way that made him, for once, less patient with a mystery than curious about it, and he said, "You've never shown me that box."

"No," she agreed. "I haven't."

"Is there a reason?"

The hour was wrong, the light through the window still the ordinary gray-gold of a candle and not the particular blue, and something in her that kept its hour so faithfully every other night of the year simply did not rise to guard the door in time —

"There was a man," she said. "Before you. He died before we married, an accident at the garrison in Temesvár, before I ever knew you existed. I have kept something for him every night since. Not a letter. Not a photograph. There is nothing in the box you would call evidence of anything."

She stopped there. Her hand had gone back to the drawer's edge without her sending it, and she looked at her own fingers on the wood as though they belonged to someone she was in the process of being introduced to.

Anton stood in the doorway for a long moment.

He did not ask his name.

## IV.

Anton walked, the next several mornings, to the promenade at his accustomed hour, greeted the Baroness with his accustomed bow, drank his water, discussed with Řehák the now nearly certain collapse of the Hoffmann engagement — and did all of this with no outward alteration whatsoever, which was itself the most alarming thing about him, though no one was positioned to notice it.

The box crossed his mind more than he thought it should, given how little there had been to see in it. He had not, in the end, asked to see inside; something in her stillness had made the asking feel like it would cost more than he had any right to spend. He had held, without ever once stating it to himself, that a man who provided a house, a name, a table, a place in the promenade's ordering, had thereby purchased clear title to the whole of what his wife was. He could no longer avoid noticing there had been a floor beneath the house, and that something had always been going on under it, at a fixed hour, unaudited, entire. There was no entry for this in any ledger he had ever kept.

On the second-to-last evening, he came up early, deliberately, and found her door properly shut. He did not open it. He went down to the smoking room, where Bittner held forth on troop movements nobody present had authority to confirm or deny, and Anton took a brandy, and found, to his mild surprise, that he had nothing to say about the Balkans that night at all.

A sentence assembled itself somewhere in him before he could stop it: that it was, in its way, a rather touching thing, a wife who had carried such a fidelity so quietly — an observation he might carry to Reiner next season, over the waters, and that would reflect, in the telling, rather well on the teller's own perceptiveness in having been married to such depths without ever suspecting them.

He let the brandy sit a while before he finished it. He left the glass on the little table by the cold hearth for the maid to find in the morning, which was not a thing he had ever once done in twelve years of otherwise tidy habits. He did not, in the weeks that followed, tell the sentence to Reiner, or to anyone. But he kept turning it over some mornings at the pump-room rail, testing its polish the way he tested the fit of a coat he had not yet decided to have taken in.

The baths closed some days later. The Colonnade's board came down, the schedule with it. Frau Nemcová began, that same week, quietly repainting the pension-house shutters against the spring — the same unhurried welcome already composing itself for whoever arrived cross and travel-worn next May.

On the last night in town, Klára went to the drawer at her hour. The light did its blue. She opened it, touched the latch with two fingers, and waited for the cold to come up into them the way it always had — and felt only brass. Ordinary brass, warmed faintly by the room.

She kept her fingers there longer than she needed to.

The latch gave back nothing it had not always given back.

She closed the drawer. She did not know, going down to see about the last packing of the trunks, whether she would open it again tomorrow, in whatever room came next.
