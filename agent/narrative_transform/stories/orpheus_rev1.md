# The Backward Glance

## I.

*I taught him the words before he had the grief to put in them. That's the way of it in Verholt. You learn the Keening before there's any water that could take you.*

*He sang it clean the first time, a boy barely into his growing, over his grandfather's collar. Clean and empty, a boy's voice going through motions. I remember thinking: good, let it stay empty as long as it can.*

*It didn't stay empty.*

---

The wool of her shawl has gone stiff in the chest for winter upon winter now and I have never once rubbed salt into it. Ness asks me about this, sideways, over the pan.

"You going to keep-salt-shy the whole of your life," she says, working the black grain between her palms, "or you going to sing the thing properly and let it dull?"

"It doesn't need dulling."

"Everything needs dulling, Cal. That's what dulling's for."

I don't answer that. I fold the shawl back into the chest and go down to the skiff, because the eels don't care what a man is or isn't carrying.

The reeds don't give back what they take. I have heard this said the way I've heard men say the tide doesn't wait for a slow hand. Weather-talk. Nobody in this parish says wall. Nobody says law.

For a long while I believed that too. I went to the Crossing House gate unbidden and stood at the reed-line looking at the stone house with its one lit window, and I understood, the way you understand a locked door before you've tried the handle, that there was nothing behind it that would open for a man with nothing to offer but wanting. I turned around. I stayed away a good while after.

Then Ness sang the anniversary Keening at the graveside, the way she does every year, and something in me came open like the chest, and I heard my own voice under hers, and I understood, coldly, that the song wasn't only for comfort. It was a key as much as a wound.

I have used it as a key since. I notice Ness watching my hands when I sing now, the way she used to watch a boy's hands to see if he'd learned the shape of the words right. Both, maybe. I don't have room to be delicate about which.

---

At the gate I straighten my collar the way you straighten it for anyone with authority.

"I've come to put a petition to the Warden," I say. "Renwick, Cal, of the eastern flats. I'll sing the Keening if he'll hear it."

The Watchman is Alder Voss, whose mother pans salt a good stretch down from Ness's stretch. Something crosses his face that isn't quite pity.

"Third bell," he says. "If you can hold the whole song without breaking, he'll hear the case." Then, quieter: "Not many hold it whole, Cal. Don't mistake that for a kindness of his if you do."

I hold the whole song. Her whole name, the exact hour, the way the water took the ferry-boat's line taut when it left the eastern dock. My voice doesn't break, though something behind my ribs does, where no one at the gate can see. Voss's face doesn't change, but he doesn't look away either.

The Warden hears the case.

## II.

Warden Ossory kept the Passage rolls in his own hand, as his father had, and his father's father before that. A man's whole petition, reduced to a clean line: *name, parish, cause, disposition.* It comforted him the way a well-swept hearth comforts a man who has never had to sleep cold.

He had refused most of the season's petitions already. Not cruelty — arithmetic that didn't need to call itself arithmetic. Most griefs, however sung, didn't meet the threshold his family's office had maintained across generations, so that the dead could keep their dulled, colder living undisturbed by hope arriving on every tide.

His own father had granted only a bare handful across a whole tenure. This was not a rule written anywhere. It was the rhythm the office had always kept, and Ossory trusted rhythm the way other men trusted scripture.

Cal Renwick's Keening was, he noted, setting down his pen, unusually complete. Most men faltered on the hour; grief blurred time before it blurred anything else. This one hadn't. He granted the case a hearing on that basis, which was, he felt, entirely fair. The rules of his office did not require him to feel anything about a petitioner. They required him only to determine whether the case met the form.

He did not consider, setting the terms that afternoon — *the petitioner may walk the reed-flats and bring the deceased to the Crossing House gate; he must not look back until both have passed fully onto Verholt clay; the condition is absolute; there is no second trial* — whether the condition was severe. Severity was not a category his office measured. Fairness was: had every petitioner in living memory been offered the same unadorned terms? They had. He understood this as proof of the rule's justice, not a flaw in it.

He signed the case and set down his pen. The fire had burned low and he sat a moment in the particular warmth of a decision that asked nothing further of the man who'd made it, before rising to bank the coals himself.

## III.

*He came to me the night before with the fistful of salt still ungranted in his coat, and asked if I'd sing over him once more before he went. Not the anniversary Keening — something smaller, just for the walking.*

*I sang it. My mouth knew the shape before my mind caught up.*

*I pressed a fistful of the black grain into his palm after. In case, I said, and didn't finish the sentence, because I didn't know what the rest of it was. I've panned that grain the whole of my working life and it's never once done anything but cure the manageable.*

*But my hands wanted to give him something, and that was the only something my hands know how to make.*

## IV.

The reeds close over the causeway almost at once, the light through them gone the color of weak tea. I do not look back at the gate, though every animal part of me wants one clean look at the stone house before it's out of reach. I am already practicing not looking, the way you practice not touching a wound before someone tells you not to touch it.

The ground gives underfoot in a way Verholt clay never gives — a wet, sucking give, each step drawing a small sound out of the mud. The reed-hiss is constant, dry and papery, never resolving into wind.

I find her where the causeway bends, standing exactly where a living woman would stand if she'd been waiting a long while without impatience. I don't look at her long. Something in Ossory's flat voice when he explained the terms told me the beginning matters — that whatever grace exists between seeing her and turning away is thin, and I shouldn't spend it.

I spend some of it anyway. I let myself have her face — the actual weather of it, the particular way her mouth sat when she wasn't yet smiling but about to — for the length of a held breath.

"Come on, then," I say, easy, unceremonious. Ceremony would break something in my throat.

I hear her step onto the causeway behind me. After that it's only sound.

## V.

Married life teaches a man things he doesn't know he's learned until they're all he has left. I could pick Mara's tread out of a room crowded with women blindfolded — the particular unhurried set-down of her heel, a walk that favored the left slightly from an old fall on the eel-dock steps when she was a girl.

I hear it behind me now. The reed-hiss makes everything harder to be sure of, and the mud-suck of my own steps threatens to swallow the smaller sound of hers, but underneath both, if I hold still inside my own walking, there it is: heel, then the soft drag, then the small catch that isn't quite a limp.

The path bends around a stand of taller reed and the ground firms, more clay in it now. Verholt's ground, starting to reach this far out to meet me. That should comfort me. It doesn't, particularly.

*That catch — was that her catch, or a trick the reeds do with sound, or my own want making a shape out of nothing?*

I don't turn. I fix my eyes forward on the thinning reed-line and let the doubt sit in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Under it, something else sits too. That held breath at the bend, her actual face and not memory's flattened version of it — I have that now. I turn it over the way you turn a coin in a pocket to be sure it's still there. It doesn't cancel the doubt. It sits beside it, both crowded into the same narrow space behind my ribs.

## VI.

Ossory did not often stay at his desk past the last bell, but that night he found himself there anyway, the Passage rolls open to Cal Renwick's case, a cold cup of small beer forgotten at his elbow.

He read back over his own script. *Trial granted, terms standard.* He was moved by the completeness of the boy's Keening, the steadiness in his voice when he'd said her name and the hour, no falter, in a way that suggested either great discipline or a grief too large to trip over its own details.

His hand, resting on the page, went still a moment longer than it needed to.

He noticed the stillness. That was the thing he would not, later, have been able to account for honestly — that he noticed his own hand not moving, and in the noticing there was a fork, brief as a held breath, where he might have asked what exactly in Renwick's case had reached past the ledger-script and touched something that wanted to sit still.

He did not take it. He called the stillness *a beat of attention* — a phrase that let him keep the stillness and set it down at the same time, the way you can hold a coal a moment in a gloved hand and call the warmth pleasant precisely because the glove means you never have to ask how hot it is underneath. Not doubt, he told himself. Some cooler, unlit room in him understood this wasn't the same as doubt actually being absent. It didn't press. Pressing was not a thing his office had ever required.

He closed the rolls, banked the fire himself, and went to bed. He slept well.

## VII.

*I wasn't there for the walking. No one from the parish ever is. I only had it after, secondhand, from the Watchmen who saw the start and end of it and nothing of the middle, which belongs, I suppose, only to the ones inside it.*

*But I know what I taught him. Her whole name. The exact hour. Say it complete or the saying does nothing — I never once thought to soften that for him.*

*My wrists ache tonight the way they always ache, from the pan, from a working life spent turning grain that will cure some griefs and pass clean through others like water through a net. I didn't tell him that. I'm not sure I could have, in words that would have stopped him going.*

## VIII.

The reed-line has thinned enough now that I can feel the different quality of light against my closed eyelids when I blink — brighter, the marsh opening toward the last stretch before firmer ground. My chest has gone tight in a way that has nothing to do with the walking itself. It's the listening that's using me up.

Her step behind me falters.

Not stops. Falters — a half-beat's hesitation in the rhythm I've had by heart these last minutes, and then a gap where the catch doesn't come, or comes late, or comes at all and I only imagined the gap because my own heart stumbled first and dragged the sound of her after it.

I don't know. That's the whole of it. There is no way inside the rule Ossory gave me to know.

My hand goes to my coat pocket without my telling it to. The keeping-salt is there, and my fingers close around it, damp and granular against my palm, and for a held breath I let myself believe an old woman's *in case* might hold some weight it's never held anywhere else.

It does nothing. It sits in my fist inert as sand.

The step behind me hasn't resumed. Or it has, and I've lost the thread of it in my own held breath. Or it never faltered and I broke the rhythm myself by listening too hard.

And underneath even that — that look at the bend. Her face, entire, before any of this walking had to be done at all. I hold it up against the fear the way you'd hold a coal against a wound to see if it still burns. It does.

The ground under my boots is nearly all clay now. Close. I can feel how close.

I turn.

## IX.

There is no drama in it, from where I stand. She is there — she was there, the whole way, heel and drag and catch, exactly as I knew her — and for the smallest sliver of time before anything happens I have her face again, the actual weather of it.

Then the reeds take the shape of her the way water takes the shape of a hand pulled out of it. No violence. No cry. Just an absence arriving where a presence had been, smooth as a held breath finally let go, and I am alone on the causeway with the last stretch of clay in front of me and nothing behind.

I don't fall down. I stay standing. My hand is still closed around the salt, and I open my fingers, and it runs out between them into the mud, indistinguishable from the marsh's own black ground the moment it lands.

## X.

Ossory logged the case the following morning, unhurried, his hand as even as it had ever been. *Renwick, C. — trial voided, terms not met, second petition inadmissible per standing rule.* He did not think this entry unusually sad. It was, statistically, the ordinary outcome.

The pen rested a moment longer than necessary over the closing line. He noticed that, and noticed himself not following it. He wrote the line, blotted it, and reached for the next case on the rota.

*I heard it from Alder Voss before Cal was even back across the clay — that the reeds took her again clean, that he came through the gate alone and didn't sing anything, didn't say her name, just walked past the Watchmen with his coat pockets turned out and empty, salt-grain still caught in the seams.*

*I didn't ask Cal what happened on the causeway. There's a kind of not-asking in this parish that isn't silence, exactly — we have a word for the hush a house takes on after a death, and there should be another word for the hush that settles after a chance like that one closes, but there isn't, not yet, not in all our generations of standing at this Line finding words for everything else.*

*He still keeps her shawl in the chest. Still hasn't rubbed the salt in. I don't ask him to anymore.*

*Some Aprils I sing the Keening at her grave and my mouth still knows every word before I've decided to say them, and it still steadies me exactly as much as it always has, and it still opens the same old seam doing it.*
