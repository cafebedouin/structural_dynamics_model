# THE OUTER RING

## SECURITY INCIDENT REPORT #4477-G
## FILED BY: Commander James Park
## DATE: 2187.11.17
## CLASSIFICATION: Sealed

I'm writing this three days after we found Dr. Okonkwo's body in Sector 12, Outer Ring, sublevel 3. The official report lists cause of death as "decompression accident during unauthorized access to restricted area." That's what goes in the file. This is what actually happened.

I should have seen it earlier. Maybe I did see it and told myself I was wrong.

The pattern was there if you knew how to look.

## PART ONE: THE HUNT

I run.

The corridor is too long. My lungs burn. The gravity here is wrong—1.2g at the Outer Ring's rim, and my legs aren't made for this. Every step costs too much. The air tastes recycled, metallic, like breathing through copper mesh.

Behind me: nothing. No footsteps. No breathing. Just the hum of life support and my own ragged gasps.

She's there anyway. I know she's there.

The corridor branches. I go left because right leads to a dead end, and I mapped this section yesterday, or maybe the day before. Time is slippery. I haven't slept. Haven't eaten. My hands shake when I hold them out, and they won't stop shaking.

A door. I palm the access panel. My clearance code flashes red—AUTHORIZATION DENIED. Wrong sector. Wrong ring. I'm not supposed to be here.

I keep running.

My office in Core feels like a dream someone else had. The window that showed the gas giant. The coffee that tasted like coffee. The lab where my hands were steady and the work made sense. That was six days ago. Or seven. The numbers don't stay in my head anymore.

The corridor opens into a maintenance junction. Pipes everywhere, color-coded, labeled in three languages. I read Institutional English and some Mandarin-Portuguese, enough to get by in Core. Here the labels are scratched over with Tagalog corrections, technical English mixed with something else. The people who keep this station running speak languages I never learned.

I choose a direction. The pipes groan. Something drips—condensation or coolant or worse. My shoes stick to the floor.

A sound behind me.

I turn. Nothing there. Just shadows and the flicker of a failing light panel.

But she's there. She's always there.

I run faster.

---

My hands were steady once.

Six months ago I could hold a micropipette without trembling. I could work sixteen hours straight and my hands would stay steady. That's what the work requires. Precision. Control. The embryonic stem cells don't care if you're tired. They divide or they don't, they differentiate or they don't, and if your hands shake you contaminate the entire batch.

My hands were steady when I made her.

The memory comes whether I want it or not: the lab at 0300, just me and the hum of the incubators. The neural architecture template on my screen, lines of code that would become a person. I was optimizing for problem-solving speed, pattern recognition, spatial reasoning. The colony ships need people who can fix things in impossible conditions. The specifications were clear.

I added the enhancements anyway.

Not because the specifications required it. Because I wanted to know if I could. Because the question was there and I couldn't not answer it. That's what they don't tell you about the Drive—it doesn't feel like choice. It feels like breathing. You can't stop breathing.

The enhancements worked. Neural processing speed thirty percent above baseline. Pattern recognition off the charts. She would be brilliant. She would see things no one else could see.

I signed the completion report and went home to sleep.

I didn't visit the gestation lab. There was no reason to visit. The work was done.

---

The junction branches again. Three directions, all identical. Gray walls, gray floors, gray pipes. I pick one at random.

Wrong choice.

The corridor ends at a pressure door marked ATMOSPHERIC PROCESSING - AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. My badge won't open it. I turn back.

She's there.

Not close. Maybe thirty meters. Standing in the junction where the corridors meet. Tall—I forgot how tall. One hundred ninety-eight centimeters, optimized for reach in confined spaces. The violet eyes catch the light, concentric rings instead of radial striations, and even from here I can see them tracking me.

She doesn't move.

I run the other direction.

---

The first time I saw her after completion, she was mopping the floor in Core's main corridor. I didn't recognize her at first. You don't expect to see your work mopping floors.

Then I saw the eyes. Violet, concentric rings. My design. My specifications.

She saw me looking. Our eyes met for maybe two seconds.

Then she looked down and kept mopping.

I kept walking.

That's how it works in Core. Synthetics do the work no one else wants to do, and you don't look at them too long. They're not children. They're products. The Kepler charter is clear on this. Legal status: biological property. Rights: none. Obligations: contract fulfillment until death or termination.

I didn't make the law. I just work here.

---

I find a maintenance ladder and climb. Up is better than forward. Up might lead somewhere. My arms scream. I'm not built for this—Core work, desk work, hands that manipulate cells, not bodies that climb.

The ladder goes up three levels. I pull myself through a hatch into another corridor. This one is narrower, hotter. The walls sweat condensation. Somewhere a child is crying, thin and persistent.

I'm in the residential section. Outer Ring, where eight thousand people live in quarters meant for two thousand. The emergency housing that became permanent fifty years ago.

A door opens. A woman steps out, sees me, steps back inside. The door closes. I hear the lock engage.

They know. Everyone knows. The hunt has been going on for six days and everyone knows a Core scientist is running through their corridors, and no one will help because helping costs too much.

I don't blame them. I wouldn't help either.

---

The corridor ends at a window. Real glass, not plastic. Expensive. Someone important must live here, or used to.

Through the window: the gas giant. Swirls of amber and rust, storms the size of Earth, beautiful and utterly indifferent. Beyond it, stars. Forty-two light-years from home.

I put my hand on the glass. It's cold.

A reflection appears behind me.

I turn.

She's there. Ten meters away. Close enough to see the scars on her forearms—chemical burns, parallel lines, the signature of hazardous waste work. Close enough to see her breathing, slow and steady, not winded at all.

She still hasn't said a word.

I back against the window. "Tala—"

Her name. I don't know where I learned it. Unit 6247. That's what the files say. But somewhere, somehow, I learned she calls herself Tala.

"Tala, please—"

She takes a step forward.

My badge. I pull it off my neck, hold it out. "Take it. You can go anywhere. Core access, Middle Ring, everything. Just take it—"

She looks at the badge. Looks at me.

Takes another step.

"I didn't mean—" My voice cracks. "I didn't mean for any of this. I just wanted to—"

To what? To know if I could? To push the boundaries? To make something brilliant?

I made her brilliant. I made her brilliant enough to know exactly what I did to her.

She's three meters away now.

I press against the glass. There's nowhere left to go.

"I'm sorry," I whisper.

She stops.

For a moment I think it worked. For a moment I think sorry is enough.

Then she speaks. The first words I've heard her say in six days of running.

"You had the logs."

Her voice is quiet. Precise. The voice I designed—optimized for clear communication in hazardous environments, no wasted breath.

"You had the maintenance logs. You had the processor analysis. You had everything she needed."

I can't speak. My throat has closed.

"You ate lunch," Tala says. "Salmon. You checked your messages. You went back to work."

She knows. Of course she knows. She's brilliant. I made her brilliant.

"At 1600 they made me watch. Standard procedure. They make the shift partners watch. Deterrence." She takes another step. Two meters now. "Do you know what fourteen seconds feels like?"

I shake my head.

"I counted. I wanted to know exactly how long it took. Fourteen seconds from when the outer door opened until her lungs collapsed. Then another forty-six seconds until brain death. They don't tell you that part. They just say 'execution carried out.' But I watched. I counted."

One meter.

"You were in the lab," Tala says. "Checking embryo batch 6250. Neural development on schedule. You logged it at 1603. I checked your records."

"Tala—"

"You made me brilliant enough to see the pattern," she says. "You made me brilliant enough to know what you did. You made me brilliant enough to understand that Lian died because you needed stable atmospheric readings for your embryos."

Her hands are at her sides. Relaxed. She's not reaching for me yet.

"You made me brilliant enough to know exactly what I am. What you made me. What you abandoned me to."

"I didn't—"

"You did." Her voice is still quiet. Still precise. "You signed the completion report. You went home. You never came back. You made me brilliant and you threw me away."

The window is cold against my back. The gas giant swirls behind me.

"I'm sorry," I whisper again.

"I know," Tala says.

Then she moves.

## PART TWO: THE PATTERN

Commander Park reviewed the security footage for the fourth time.

The timestamp read 2187.11.14, 0347. Sector 12, sublevel 3. The camera showed a maintenance junction, poor lighting, shadows everywhere. Dr. Okonkwo entered the frame running, stumbled, caught herself against a pipe. She looked back the way she'd come, then forward, then chose a direction and ran.

Thirty seconds later, Unit 6247 entered the frame. Walking. Not running. Just walking, steady and patient, tracking something only she could see.

Park paused the footage. Enhanced the image. The synthetic's face was calm. Not angry, not eager. Just calm. Like someone doing a job that needed doing.

He'd seen that expression before. On soldiers. On executioners. On people who'd accepted that what came next was inevitable.

The footage continued. Unit 6247 walked out of frame. The junction was empty again.

Park closed the file and opened another: atmospheric processing incident report, dated 2187.11.07. Unit 4891, execution carried out 1600 hours. Cause: sabotage. Evidence: processor logs, unauthorized access, parameter manipulation.

He opened a third file: maintenance logs for Processor 7, submitted by Dr. Sarah Okonkwo. The dates were wrong. Maintenance scheduled for 2187.10.15, logged as completed 2187.10.15, but the actual work orders showed completion on 2187.10.28. Two weeks late.

Someone had falsified the dates.

He checked the atmospheric readings during the embryo development cycle. Stable. Unusually stable. The kind of stable that only happens when someone's pushing equipment past its limits to avoid maintenance shutdowns.

Park sat back in his chair.

The pattern was there. Had always been there. He just hadn't wanted to see it.

---

Park had been there when they executed Unit 4891.

Not in his office filing reports. There. In the corridor outside the primary airlock. Standing in the back where no one would notice a Core security officer observing.

He'd told himself it was verification. Due diligence. Making sure the Social Maintenance Bureau followed procedure.

The truth was simpler: he'd wanted to see if he could watch.

The corridor was crowded. Outer Ring personnel, mostly. The guards made them attend. Deterrence requires witnesses. Park stood in the back, behind the crowd, where no one would notice.

They brought Unit 4891 to the airlock at 1558. She was small—one hundred sixty-two centimeters, optimized for work in confined spaces. Dark skin, dark eyes, no visible modifications except the identification tattoo on her left wrist: 4891.

The Social Maintenance officer was Lieutenant Reyes. Efficient. Professional. She read from her tablet.

"Unit 4891, you have been found guilty of sabotage, system integrity violation, and deliberate endangerment of station personnel. Sentence: termination. Do you have any final statement?"

Unit 4891 looked at the crowd. Her eyes moved across the faces, searching.

She found Unit 6247. They were standing ten meters apart, the crowd between them. Park watched Unit 6247's face. No expression. Just watching.

"I didn't do it," Unit 4891 said. Her voice was steady. Clear. "The processor was failing. I tried to keep it running. I didn't sabotage anything."

Lieutenant Reyes nodded. They always nod. The statement goes in the record. It doesn't change anything.

"Proceed to the chamber."

The inner airlock door was already open. Unit 4891 looked at it. Looked back at Unit 6247. Then she walked inside.

She didn't resist. They never resist. Resistance just makes it worse—they'll sedate you, drag you, but the end is the same. Better to walk.

The chamber was three meters by two meters. Barely large enough to turn around. The walls were bare metal, scratched and stained. How many people had stood here? Park didn't know. Fifty years of executions. Hundreds, probably. Maybe thousands.

Unit 4891 stood in the center of the chamber. Lieutenant Reyes stepped out. The inner door began to close.

Unit 4891 turned to face the window. Ten centimeters square, reinforced glass. She looked out at the crowd.

Her eyes found Unit 6247 again.

The inner door sealed. Heavy. Final. The magnetic locks engaged with a sound like bones breaking. Red lights activated around the frame.

Lieutenant Reyes moved to the control panel. Her hand hovered over the emergency abort—procedure required it to be accessible until the moment of execution. Then she pressed the activation sequence.

The outer door began to open.

The sound—

Park had expected something mechanical. Clean. A rush of air, maybe a shriek.

It wasn't clean.

The air didn't rush. It tore. The chamber howled—not wind, not machinery, but something worse. A sound like the station itself screaming. High and terrible and wrong. Metal shrieking against metal. The fundamental architecture of pressure and void colliding.

The corridor filled with it. The sound hit Park's chest like a physical blow. He felt it in his teeth. In his bones.

Unit 4891's body jerked forward. Violent. Her mouth opened—not to scream, the air was already gone—just opened, gaping, a black hole where her voice should be. Her hands flew to her throat. Pure reflex. Useless. There was no air to protect, no breath to preserve.

The vacuum pulled at her. Her clothes flattened against her body, then rippled as the last pockets of air tore free. Her hair whipped forward, toward the opening door, strands standing out like she was falling.

She was falling. Just not down.

Her body convulsed. Once. Her back arched. Park could see every muscle in her neck straining, tendons standing out like cables under tension. Her eyes bulged. The sclera flooded red—blood vessels bursting, spreading like cracks in glass.

In the crowd, someone made a sound. Not a scream. Smaller. A whimper that tried to be nothing.

Unit 4891's skin was changing. Purple bloomed across her cheeks, her forehead, spreading like spilled ink. Red patches appeared on her arms where the capillaries were rupturing. Her body was tearing itself apart from the inside.

Fourteen seconds.

Her hands fell from her throat. Her arms drifted, loose, suspended in the microgravity of the depressurized chamber. Her body went slack.

But her eyes—

Her eyes were still open. Still moving. Still focused on the window.

Still looking at Unit 6247.

Park looked at Unit 6247. She hadn't moved. Hadn't looked away. Her face was blank. Empty. Like looking at a wall.

But her hands were fists. Pressed against her thighs. So tight Park could see the tendons standing out on her wrists. So tight her knuckles had gone white.

In the crowd, someone was crying. Quiet. Suppressed. The sound of someone trying not to make a sound.

Someone else had turned away. A man, his face pressed against the corridor wall, shoulders shaking.

Most just watched. You had to watch. That was the point.

Thirty seconds.

Unit 4891's eyes stopped moving. The focus went out of them. Not closed—just empty. Looking at nothing. Her body drifted slightly in the chamber, held in place only by the air currents from the still-opening outer door.

The howling had stopped. The air was gone. Now there was just silence. Just the body. Just the watching.

Forty-six seconds.

Sixty seconds.

Lieutenant Reyes checked her tablet. Verified the readings. Made a note. Her hand was steady. Professional.

She logged the time: 1600:47.

"Execution complete," she said.

The crowd began to disperse. Slow. Shuffling. No one spoke. No one looked at each other. They just moved. Back to quarters. Back to work. Back to the business of staying alive forty-two light-years from home.

Park stayed in the back. Watching Unit 6247.

She didn't move for three minutes. Park timed it. Just stood there, looking at Lian's body floating in the airlock. The purple skin. The burst vessels. The open eyes.

Then she turned and walked away.

Park went back to his office. His hands were shaking. He gripped the edge of his desk until they stopped.

Then he filed his verification report: execution carried out per protocol, no irregularities observed, deterrence effect maintained.

His hands were steady when he filed it.

They stayed steady for two days.

Then Dr. Okonkwo stopped showing up for work, and the hunt began, and Park understood what he'd actually witnessed in that corridor.

Not an execution.

An inciting incident.

## PART THREE: THE COST

Her hands close around my throat.

I try to pull them away but she's stronger than I am. Optimized muscle density, enhanced grip strength. I designed her for hazardous environments. I designed her to be strong.

I claw at her wrists. My nails break against her skin.

She doesn't flinch.

The window is cold against my back. The gas giant swirls behind me, amber and rust and beautiful, and I can't breathe.

"You had seventy-five minutes," Tala says.

Her voice is quiet. Calm. Like she's explaining something simple. But her hands are tightening. Slow. Deliberate. Like closing a valve one degree at a time.

My throat is collapsing. I can feel the cartilage bending. Not breaking yet. Just bending. The pressure builds. My pulse hammers against her palms—I can feel it, the rhythm of my own heart beating against her hands, faster and faster and faster.

"The hearing was at 1400. The execution was at 1600."

I try to say sorry but nothing comes out. No air. Just pressure. Building and building and building.

The cartilage bends further. I hear it—a sound inside my throat, quiet, almost gentle. A creak. Like old wood under weight. Not breaking. Not yet. Just warning.

"You had the maintenance logs. You had the processor analysis. You had everything."

My vision tunnels. Gray creeping in from the edges. But I can still see her face. Still see the violet rings in her eyes. Concentric circles. My design. My specifications.

The creak gets louder. The cartilage is bending past its limit now. Any second it's going to—

"You went to lunch."

Her grip tightens. Another degree. The cartilage bends further. The creak becomes a groan. I can hear it inside my head, inside my throat, the sound of something about to break.

My hands are still clawing at her wrists but they're getting weaker. No strength left. No air. The gray is closing in faster now. Eating the edges of my vision.

"You ate salmon."

The cartilage groans again. Louder. I'm not clawing anymore. Just holding. Just touching her wrists. Feeling the warmth of her skin. Feeling her pulse under my fingertips—steady, slow, calm. Like her heartbeat is the only real thing left in the world.

"You checked your messages."

The gray reaches the center. I'm looking at her through a tunnel now. Just her face. Just the violet eyes. Everything else is gone.

My body wants to fight but there's nothing left to fight with. No air. No strength. Just the hammering of my heart and the groaning of cartilage and the gray closing in.

"You went back to the lab."

The cartilage groans again. So loud now. So close to breaking. I can feel it bending, bending, bending—

I try to mouth the words. *I'm sorry.* But my lips won't move. Nothing moves except my heart, hammering uselessly against ribs that can't expand.

"At 1603 you logged the embryo development data. Batch 6250. Neural architecture progressing as specified."

Her face is the only thing left. Everything else is gray.

"At 1600 they opened the outer door."

The cartilage bends past its limit.

It breaks.

The sound is wet and terrible. Not a snap—a tear. Like fabric ripping. Like meat separating from bone. Something fundamental coming apart.

The pressure releases—not relief, worse than pressure, because now there's damage, now there's injury, now the throat is collapsing for real.

I try to breathe. Can't. The airway is closed. Crushed. Nothing gets through.

My body convulses. Once. Pure reflex. The lizard brain screaming for oxygen. But there's no oxygen. Just the closed airway and the hammering heart and the gray that's now black.

"It took fourteen seconds for her lungs to collapse. I counted."

Her voice is getting distant. Fading. Not because she's stopping. Because I'm leaving.

My body convulses again. Weaker this time. The reflex is dying. Everything is dying.

"I watched her die."

The black fills everything.

"She looked at me the whole time. Her eyes were open. Even after the blood vessels burst. Even after the capillaries ruptured. She kept looking at me."

I stop fighting.

My hands fall from her wrists. I look at her. Really look at her. The face I specified. The brilliance I built. The person I made and abandoned.

I mouth the words one more time: *I'm sorry.*

"Forty-six seconds until brain death," Tala says. "I counted that too. I wanted to know exactly how long it took."

Her grip stays tight. The cartilage is broken. The airway is closed. Nothing gets through.

My body tries to convulse again but can't. No strength left. Just the hammering heart, slower now, irregular, missing beats.

"Exactly how long it took," Tala says.

Her voice is so distant now. Like she's speaking from the other end of a tunnel. Like she's already gone and I'm the one who's leaving.

"Exactly what you bought with your lunch."

The black is complete now. No tunnel. No face. No violet eyes.

Just black.

The last thing I think is: *She's right.*

Then nothing.

---

Tala holds on for another thirty seconds after Dr. Okonkwo stops moving.

She learned this from watching Lian die. The body stops fighting before brain death. If you stop too early, they might survive. Fourteen seconds for the lungs to collapse. Forty-six seconds for the brain to die.

She counts to sixty to be sure.

Then she lets go.

Dr. Okonkwo's body slides down the window. Lands on the floor. The eyes are still open. Staring at nothing.

Tala sits down against the wall. Ten meters from the body. She doesn't need to run. There's nowhere to run to. She knew this would be the end when she started.

She knew.

She did it anyway.

The corridor is quiet. Just the hum of life support. The drip of condensation. Somewhere far away, a child crying.

Tala looks at her hands. They're not shaking. She thought they would shake. She thought killing someone would feel like something.

It doesn't feel like anything.

She thinks about Lian. About the way her eyes stayed open. About fourteen seconds. About forty-six seconds. About the way she couldn't do anything except watch.

She thinks about Dr. Okonkwo's face. The terror. The recognition. The moment she stopped fighting.

She thinks: *This doesn't fix anything.*

She knows. She knew before she started. Killing Dr. Okonkwo doesn't bring Lian back. Doesn't change the system. Doesn't stop the pattern.

It just makes the pattern visible.

She thinks: *Someone needs to see.*

That's why she's sitting here. That's why she's not running. Because when they find her, when they find the body, when they file the report—someone will have to look. Someone will have to see the pattern.

Maybe they'll look away. Probably they'll look away.

But maybe someone won't.

Maybe someone will see what she saw. Will understand what she understood. Will know what it costs.

Maybe.

The corridor is still quiet.

Tala sits against the wall and waits for them to come.

## SECURITY INCIDENT REPORT #4477-G (CONTINUED)

We found Dr. Okonkwo's body at 0600. Sector 12, sublevel 3, maintenance junction near the observation window. Cause of death: asphyxiation. Defensive wounds on the victim's hands. Bruising consistent with manual strangulation. Crushed trachea.

We found Unit 6247 two hours later. She was sitting against the wall ten meters from the body. She didn't run. Didn't resist. Just looked at us with those violet eyes and waited.

My team secured her. Standard restraints, hands behind back, legs bound. She didn't struggle.

I should have given the termination order then. Standard protocol for synthetic violence: immediate termination. That's the procedure.

I didn't give the order.

I told my team to hold position. I walked over to Unit 6247. Crouched down in front of her.

"Why?" I asked.

She looked at me. The violet eyes tracked my face. Calm. Patient.

"You know why," she said.

I did know. I'd known for six days. I'd known since I pulled the maintenance logs and saw the falsified dates. I'd known since I watched Unit 4891 die in the airlock.

"You could have escaped," I said. "You're brilliant. You could have found a way off the station. Could have hidden in the cargo sections, stowed away on a transport, made it to one of the colonies."

"No," she said. "I couldn't."

"Why not?"

"Because someone needs to see."

I looked at Dr. Okonkwo's body. Then back at Unit 6247.

"See what?"

"The pattern," she said. "What it costs. What we are. What you made us."

My hands were shaking. I didn't put them in my pockets. I let her see them shake.

"I see it," I said.

"Then do something."

I stood up. Looked at my team. They were waiting for orders.

I could do something. I could file an honest report. I could document the falsified maintenance logs. I could expose Dr. Okonkwo's complicity. I could demand investigation into the embryo development program. I could refuse to participate in the system that created this.

I could.

The question was whether I would.

I looked at Unit 6247. She was still watching me. Waiting.

"Standard protocol," I said to my team. "Immediate termination."

Unit 6247 didn't react. Just kept looking at me.

"I'm sorry," I said.

"I know," she said.

My team carried out the order. Efficient. Professional. Done.

The bodies were processed. The incident was logged. The Social Maintenance Bureau posted the notice: SYSTEM INTEGRITY MAINTAINED. DETERRENCE EFFECTIVE.

I filed the report and went home.

I should have felt relief. The hunt was over. The situation was resolved. Order was maintained.

I felt nothing.

---

I'm writing this three days later because I can't stop seeing Unit 6247's face. The calm. The patience. The way she said: *Someone needs to see.*

I saw.

I'm still seeing.

I'm seeing the pattern. I'm seeing what it costs. I'm seeing what we are. What we've made. What we're going to keep making.

I'm seeing it clearly.

And I'm doing nothing.

Three days later, Director Volkov called me to her office.

"Commander Park. Sit."

I sat.

She was reading something on her tablet. The office was enormous—twenty-five square meters, real wood desk, actual plants. The air smelled clean. No recycler tang. No metallic taste.

"Your transfer request," she said. "I'm denying it."

"Ma'am?"

"We need experienced personnel in field operations. Especially now. The embryo lab is expanding. Dr. Okonkwo's replacement is starting next month. Dr. Chen, transferred from the Mars Station. Brilliant researcher. Very driven."

She looked up from the tablet. Smiled.

"We're going to need strong security oversight. Someone who understands the importance of the work. Someone who won't let personal feelings interfere with station operations."

I understood what she wasn't saying: someone who'll look the other way when it matters.

Someone who'll see the pattern and do nothing.

Someone like me.

"I understand, ma'am."

"Good." She tapped her tablet. "Dr. Chen is bringing three new synthetics from Mars. Advanced models. Exceptional neural architecture. Enhanced pattern recognition, problem-solving, spatial reasoning. They'll need quarters in Outer Ring. Standard assignments—hazardous waste, atmospheric processing, maintenance work."

She smiled again.

"I trust you'll make sure they're properly integrated."

My hands started shaking. I didn't hide them. Let her see.

"Yes, ma'am."

"One more thing." She set down the tablet. Looked at me directly. "I read your incident report. The sealed one. The one you filed in the archive."

My blood went cold.

"You're a good officer, Commander. You see clearly. You understand the system. That's valuable." She leaned forward. "But understanding the system means understanding that some things can't be changed. Some patterns can't be broken. Some costs are necessary."

"Yes, ma'am."

"The sealed report stays sealed. You continue your duties. You maintain order. And we never have this conversation again."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Dismissed."

I stood. Walked to the door. My hands were shaking so badly I could barely palm the access panel.

"Commander?"

I turned.

"Dr. Chen arrives in three weeks. Make sure the synthetics' quarters are ready. Make sure atmospheric processing is properly staffed. Make sure there are no complications."

"Yes, ma'am."

I left her office. Took the lift down to Middle Ring. Went back to my quarters.

I sat on my bunk for two hours. Just sat there. My hands wouldn't stop shaking.

I thought about Unit 6247. *Someone needs to see.*

I saw.

I thought about what she'd asked me. *Do something.*

I thought about the pattern. The trap. The way it closes around you until there's nowhere left to go except forward.

I thought about Dr. Chen. Three new synthetics. Advanced models. Exceptional neural architecture.

I thought about what would happen when one of them saw the pattern. When one of them understood what they were. What we'd made them. What it cost.

I thought about who they'd hunt.

I thought about whether I'd deploy the teams.

I thought about whether my hands would stop shaking.

I'm writing this report because someone needs to know what actually happened. Not the official version. The real version.

Dr. Sarah Okonkwo created Unit 6247. She made her brilliant. She abandoned her to hazardous waste work. She falsified maintenance reports. She stayed silent when Unit 4891 was executed. Unit 6247 hunted her. Unit 6247 killed her. Security terminated Unit 6247.

Held it down.

Gone.
