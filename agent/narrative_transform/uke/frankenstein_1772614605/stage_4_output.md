# THE OUTER RING

## SECURITY INCIDENT REPORT #4477-G
## FILED BY: Commander James Park
## DATE: 2187.11.17
## CLASSIFICATION: Sealed

I'm writing this three days after we found Dr. Okonkwo's body in Sector 12, Outer Ring, sublevel 3. The official report lists cause of death as "decompression accident during unauthorized access to restricted area." That's what goes in the file. This is what actually happened.

I should have seen it earlier. Maybe I did see it and told myself I was wrong.

The pattern was there if you knew how to look.

---

## PART ONE: THE HUNT

I run.

The corridor is too long. My lungs burn. The gravity here is wrong—1.2g at the Outer Ring's rim, and my legs aren't made for this. Every step costs too much. The air tastes recycled, metallic, like breathing through copper mesh.

Behind me: nothing. No footsteps. No breathing. Just the hum of life support and my own ragged gasps.

She's there anyway. I know she's there.

The corridor branches. I go left because right leads to a dead end, and I mapped this section yesterday, or maybe the day before. Time is slippery. I haven't slept. Haven't eaten. My hands shake when I hold them out, and they won't stop shaking.

A door. I palm the access panel. My clearance code flashes red—AUTHORIZATION DENIED. Wrong sector. Wrong ring. I'm not supposed to be here.

I keep running.

My office in Core feels like a dream someone else had. The window that showed the gas giant. The coffee that tasted like coffee. The lab where my hands were steady and the work made sense. That was six days ago. Or seven. The numbers don't stay in my head anymore.

The corridor opens into a maintenance junction. Pipes everywhere, color-coded, labeled in three languages. I read Institutional English and some Mandarin-Portuguese, enough to get by in Core. Here the labels are scratched over with Tagalog corrections, technical English mixed with something else. The people who keep this station running speak languages I never learned.

I choose a direction. The pipes groan. Something drips—condensation or coolant or worse. My shoes stick to the floor.

A sound behind me.

I turn. Nothing there. Just shadows and the flicker of a failing light panel.

But she's there. She's always there.

I run faster.

---

My hands were steady once.

Six months ago I could hold a micropipette without trembling. I could work sixteen hours straight and my hands would stay steady. That's what the work requires. Precision. Control. The embryonic stem cells don't care if you're tired. They divide or they don't, they differentiate or they don't, and if your hands shake you contaminate the entire batch.

My hands were steady when I made her.

The memory comes whether I want it or not: the lab at 0300, just me and the hum of the incubators. The neural architecture template on my screen, lines of code that would become a person. I was optimizing for problem-solving speed, pattern recognition, spatial reasoning. The colony ships need people who can fix things in impossible conditions. The specifications were clear.

I added the enhancements anyway.

Not because the specifications required it. Because I wanted to know if I could. Because the question was there and I couldn't not answer it. That's what they don't tell you about the Drive—it doesn't feel like choice. It feels like breathing. You can't stop breathing.

The enhancements worked. Neural processing speed thirty percent above baseline. Pattern recognition off the charts. She would be brilliant. She would see things no one else could see.

I signed the completion report and went home to sleep.

I didn't visit the gestation lab. There was no reason to visit. The work was done.

---

The junction branches again. Three directions, all identical. Gray walls, gray floors, gray pipes. I pick one at random.

Wrong choice.

The corridor ends at a pressure door marked ATMOSPHERIC PROCESSING - AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. My badge won't open it. I turn back.

She's there.

Not close. Maybe thirty meters. Standing in the junction where the corridors meet. Tall—I forgot how tall. One hundred ninety-eight centimeters, optimized for reach in confined spaces. The violet eyes catch the light, concentric rings instead of radial striations, and even from here I can see them tracking me.

She doesn't move.

I run the other direction.

---

The first time I saw her after completion, she was mopping the floor in Core's main corridor. I didn't recognize her at first. You don't expect to see your work mopping floors.

Then I saw the eyes. Violet, concentric rings. My design. My specifications.

She saw me looking. Our eyes met for maybe two seconds.

Then she looked down and kept mopping.

I kept walking.

That's how it works in Core. Synthetics do the work no one else wants to do, and you don't look at them too long. They're not children. They're products. The Kepler charter is clear on this. Legal status: biological property. Rights: none. Obligations: contract fulfillment until death or termination.

I didn't make the law. I just work here.

---

I find a maintenance ladder and climb. Up is better than forward. Up might lead somewhere. My arms scream. I'm not built for this—Core work, desk work, hands that manipulate cells, not bodies that climb.

The ladder goes up three levels. I pull myself through a hatch into another corridor. This one is narrower, hotter. The walls sweat condensation. Somewhere a child is crying, thin and persistent.

I'm in the residential section. Outer Ring, where eight thousand people live in quarters meant for two thousand. The emergency housing that became permanent fifty years ago.

A door opens. A woman steps out, sees me, steps back inside. The door closes. I hear the lock engage.

They know. Everyone knows. The hunt has been going on for six days and everyone knows a Core scientist is running through their corridors, and no one will help because helping costs too much.

I don't blame them. I wouldn't help either.

---

Lian's face won't leave my head.

Unit 4891. Assigned to atmospheric processing. Accused of sabotage when Processor 7 failed during her shift. The investigation took forty-eight hours. The evidence was clear: processor logs showed unauthorized access, parameter changes, deliberate degradation of filtration efficiency.

I knew it was wrong.

I knew because I'd delayed the maintenance reports for Processor 7. Three times. The processor was running on borrowed time, and I'd borrowed that time because I needed the atmospheric readouts stable for the embryo batch, and maintenance shutdowns would have introduced variables I couldn't control.

The processor failed because I'd pushed it past its limits.

Lian's authorization access was legitimate—she was supposed to monitor the parameters. The "unauthorized" changes were her trying to keep the failing system running.

I knew this. I had the maintenance logs. I had the evidence.

The hearing was scheduled for 1400. I could have submitted the logs. Five minutes of testimony. Lian would have been cleared.

I didn't submit them.

The calculation was simple: Submit the logs, admit I'd falsified maintenance reports, lose my position, lose my research, get sent back to Earth with a black mark that would end my career. Or stay silent, let the system handle it, keep working.

The math was simple.

At 1600 they took Lian to the airlock. Standard procedure for sabotage. The Social Maintenance Bureau posted the execution notice on every public terminal: SYSTEM INTEGRITY MAINTAINED. DETERRENCE EFFECTIVE.

I went back to the lab and worked until 0300.

My hands shook the entire time.

---

The corridor ends at a window. Real glass, not plastic. Expensive. Someone important must live here, or used to.

Through the window: the gas giant. Swirls of amber and rust, storms the size of Earth, beautiful and utterly indifferent. Beyond it, stars. Forty-two light-years from home.

I put my hand on the glass. It's cold.

A reflection appears behind me.

I turn.

She's there. Ten meters away. Close enough to see the scars on her forearms—chemical burns, parallel lines, the signature of hazardous waste work. Close enough to see her breathing, slow and steady, not winded at all.

She still hasn't said a word.

I back against the window. "Tala—"

Her name. I don't know where I learned it. Unit 6247. That's what the files say. But somewhere, somehow, I learned she calls herself Tala.

"Tala, please—"

She takes a step forward.

My badge. I pull it off my neck, hold it out. "Take it. You can go anywhere. Core access, Middle Ring, everything. Just take it—"

She looks at the badge. Looks at me.

Takes another step.

"I didn't mean—" My voice cracks. "I didn't mean for any of this. I just wanted to—"

To what? To know if I could? To push the boundaries? To make something brilliant?

I made her brilliant. I made her brilliant enough to know exactly what I did to her.

She's three meters away now.

I press against the glass. There's nowhere left to go.

"I'm sorry," I whisper.

She stops.

For a moment I think it worked. For a moment I think sorry is enough.

Then she moves, faster than I can track, and her hands close around my throat.

---

## PART TWO: THE PATTERN

Commander Park reviewed the security footage for the fourth time.

The timestamp read 2187.11.14, 0347. Sector 12, sublevel 3. The camera showed a maintenance junction, poor lighting, shadows everywhere. Dr. Okonkwo entered the frame running, stumbled, caught herself against a pipe. She looked back the way she'd come, then forward, then chose a direction and ran.

Thirty seconds later, Unit 6247 entered the frame. Walking. Not running. Just walking, steady and patient, tracking something only she could see.

Park paused the footage. Enhanced the image. The synthetic's face was calm. Not angry, not eager. Just calm. Like someone doing a job that needed doing.

He'd seen that expression before. On soldiers. On executioners. On people who'd accepted that what came next was inevitable.

The footage continued. Unit 6247 walked out of frame. The junction was empty again.

Park closed the file and opened another: atmospheric processing incident report, dated 2187.11.07. Unit 4891, execution carried out 1600 hours. Cause: sabotage. Evidence: processor logs, unauthorized access, parameter manipulation.

He opened a third file: maintenance logs for Processor 7, submitted by Dr. Sarah Okonkwo. The dates were wrong. Maintenance scheduled for 2187.10.15, logged as completed 2187.10.15, but the actual work orders showed completion on 2187.10.28. Two weeks late.

Someone had falsified the dates.

He checked the atmospheric readings during the embryo development cycle. Stable. Unusually stable. The kind of stable that only happens when someone's pushing equipment past its limits to avoid maintenance shutdowns.

Park sat back in his chair.

The pattern was there. Had always been there. He just hadn't wanted to see it.

Dr. Okonkwo delays maintenance to keep her research running. Processor 7 fails during Unit 4891's shift. Unit 4891 is accused, executed. Unit 6247 witnesses the execution—they worked the same shifts, shared quarters, probably shared more than that. Unit 6247 escapes. Unit 6247 hunts the person responsible.

Simple. Logical. Inevitable.

Park should have stopped it. Should have seen it coming. Should have intervened before the hunt began.

He didn't.

He told himself it wasn't his job to investigate Core research practices. Told himself the atmospheric processing investigation was thorough. Told himself the execution was justified.

He told himself right up until Dr. Okonkwo stopped showing up for work and the hunt became impossible to ignore.

Now it was day six, and Park was watching security footage of a brilliant scientist running for her life through Outer Ring, pursued by the synthetic she'd created and abandoned.

He could stop it. He had the authority. He could deploy security teams, flood Sector 12 with personnel, corner Unit 6247, terminate her before she reached her target.

He could.

The question was whether he would.

---

Park's personal log, 2187.11.14, 2200 hours:

*Requested transfer to Middle Ring administrative position. Director Volkov approved. Effective 2187.12.01. Reduced field duties, increased oversight responsibilities. Safer. More stable.*

*The hunt will be over by then.*

---

He didn't deploy the teams.

He told himself it was a resource allocation issue. Told himself that flooding Outer Ring with security would cause panic, disrupt operations, risk civilian casualties. Told himself he was being strategic.

He was lying.

The truth was simpler: he was afraid.

Not of Unit 6247. Not of Dr. Okonkwo.

Of the pattern.

Because if he stopped this hunt, if he intervened, if he saved Dr. Okonkwo's life—then what? She goes back to Core. Back to the lab. Back to the work that consumes sixteen hours a day. Back to the Drive that made her create synthetic humans and then abandon them to hazardous waste work.

She'd do it again. The Drive doesn't stop. It can't stop. That's what makes it the Drive.

And next time, maybe Park would be the one who had to make the choice. Maybe he'd be the one who saw something wrong and had to decide whether to speak or stay silent. Maybe he'd be the one running through Outer Ring with someone hunting him.

The pattern was a trap, and the trap was already closing.

So he didn't deploy the teams.

He filed the reports. He monitored the situation. He maintained order.

He told himself he was doing his job.

---

## PART THREE: THE COST

Her hands are strong.

I can't breathe. I claw at her wrists but she doesn't move, doesn't flinch, just squeezes steadily like she's closing a valve.

The window is cold against my back. The gas giant swirls behind me, amber and rust and beautiful, and I can't breathe.

My vision tunnels. Gray at the edges, closing in.

Her face is calm. That's what I can't understand. She's not angry. She's not crying. She's just calm, like this is something that has to be done, and she's doing it.

I try to say sorry again but nothing comes out.

The gray is closing in faster now.

I think about the lab. The incubators humming. The stem cells dividing. The neural architecture template, lines of code that became a person. I wanted to know if I could. I wanted to push the boundaries. I wanted to make something brilliant.

I made something brilliant.

The gray reaches the center.

---

## SECURITY INCIDENT REPORT #4477-G (CONTINUED)

We found Dr. Okonkwo's body at 0600. Sector 12, sublevel 3, maintenance junction near the observation window. Cause of death: asphyxiation. No signs of struggle beyond defensive wounds on the victim's hands.

We found Unit 6247 two hours later. She was sitting against the wall ten meters from the body. She didn't run. Didn't resist. Just looked at us with those violet eyes and waited.

Standard protocol for synthetic violence: immediate termination. I gave the order. My team carried it out. Efficient. Professional. Done.

The bodies were processed. The incident was logged. The Social Maintenance Bureau posted the notice: SYSTEM INTEGRITY MAINTAINED. DETERRENCE EFFECTIVE.

I filed the report and went home.

I should have felt relief. The hunt was over. The situation was resolved. Order was maintained.

I felt nothing.

---

Three days later, Director Volkov called me to her office.

"Commander Park. Sit."

I sat.

She was reading something on her tablet. The office was enormous—twenty-five square meters, real wood desk, actual plants. The air smelled clean. No recycler tang. No metallic taste.

"Your transfer request," she said. "I'm denying it."

"Ma'am?"

"We need experienced personnel in field operations. Especially now. The embryo lab is expanding. Director Okonkwo's replacement is starting next month. Dr. Chen, transferred from the Mars Station. Brilliant researcher. Very driven."

She looked up from the tablet. Smiled.

"We're going to need strong security oversight. Someone who understands the importance of the work. Someone who won't let personal feelings interfere with station operations."

I understood what she wasn't saying: someone who'll look the other way when it matters.

"I understand, ma'am."

"Good. Dismissed."

I stood. Walked to the door. Put my hand on the panel.

"Commander?"

I turned.

"Dr. Chen is bringing three new synthetics from Mars. Advanced models. Exceptional neural architecture. They'll need quarters in Outer Ring. Standard assignments—hazardous waste, atmospheric processing, maintenance work." She smiled again. "I trust you'll make sure they're properly integrated."

"Yes, ma'am."

I left her office. Took the lift down to Middle Ring. Went back to my quarters.

I should have seen it earlier.

The pattern was always there.

I'm writing this report three days after we found Dr. Okonkwo's body, and I'm writing it because someone needs to know what actually happened. Not the official version. The real version.

Dr. Sarah Okonkwo created Unit 6247. She made her brilliant. She abandoned her to hazardous waste work. She falsified maintenance reports. She stayed silent when Unit 4891 was executed. Unit 6247 hunted her. Unit 6247 killed her. Security terminated Unit 6247.

That's what happened.

Here's what's going to happen:

Dr. Chen will arrive next month. She'll be brilliant. She'll be driven. She'll work sixteen-hour days because the work demands it. She'll create synthetic humans because that's what the station needs. She'll optimize their neural architecture because she wants to know if she can.

She'll abandon them to hazardous waste work because that's what the system requires.

One of them will be brilliant enough to see the pattern.

One of them will be brilliant enough to hunt.

And I'll be the security chief who maintains order. Who files the reports. Who deploys the teams. Who gives the termination orders.

I'll tell myself I'm doing my job.

I'll tell myself right up until I'm the one running through Outer Ring with someone hunting me.

The pattern is a trap.

The trap is already closing.

I'm filing this report in the sealed archive because someone needs to know. Someone needs to see the pattern. Someone needs to understand what we're building here.

Maybe that someone is you, reading this years from now.

Maybe you'll be smarter than I was.

Maybe you'll stop it before it starts.

Or maybe you'll do what I did: see the pattern, understand the trap, and walk into it anyway because the alternative costs too much.

Either way, you'll know what actually happened.

That's all I can give you.

---

Park closed the file. Encrypted it. Buried it in the archive where no one would find it unless they knew exactly where to look.

Then he went back to work.

Dr. Chen's arrival was scheduled for next month. Three new synthetics. Advanced models. Exceptional neural architecture.

He had quarters to assign. Work schedules to arrange. Systems to maintain.

The station kept spinning. The gas giant kept swirling. The stars kept burning, forty-two light-years from home.

And the pattern kept running, inevitable as gravity, beautiful and utterly indifferent.

Park filed his daily report. Attended his meetings. Maintained order.

His hands were steady the entire time.
