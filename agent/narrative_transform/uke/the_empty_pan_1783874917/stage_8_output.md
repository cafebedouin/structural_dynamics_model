# The Interval

The madder root is ready.

I know this before I know I know it. My hands — stained the color of old blood, cracked at the knuckles where the mordant bites deepest — rest on the rim of the vat, and they tell me. Not through thought. Through something older than thought. The interval between breathing out and breathing in, when the root has given everything it has to give and will give no more.

I lift the bolt of silk. Heavy with dye, heavy with water. The red is deep, the color of pomegranate seeds split open. Not the brownish red that comes from steeping too long, when the tannins cloud the color and make it muddy. This is clean. This is right.

The sky through the workshop's high window is still dark. Fourth watch, maybe halfway through. The street below is silent except for the night soil carriers and their carts. This is my time. The time when observation sleeps, when questions die, when *begin* doesn't turn my hands to wood.

I hang the silk on the drying rack. My hands know how to wring it, how much pressure, which direction. I do not think about these things. My hands do them while I am thinking about other things — about whether there is enough millet for breakfast, about whether Master Feng will need me to prepare the indigo bath today, about the examination.

No. I stop thinking about the examination.

I rinse my hands in the bucket of clean water. The water turns pink, then clear again as I scrub. My hands are never clean. The red has soaked into the skin, into the lines of my palms, into the beds of my fingernails. Sometimes I think the red has soaked all the way through to the bone.

The workshop is narrow. Vats on the ground floor, drying racks along the walls, the smell of mordants so thick you can taste it. Alum, iron, urine left to ferment in clay jars. Upstairs, more racks. Above that, the sleeping room I share with the younger apprentices. They are still asleep. They will wake when the bell at the ward gate rings for the fifth watch.

I have maybe half an hour before Master Feng comes down.

I sit on the stool by the smallest vat and rest my hands on my knees. They are shaking. Just a little. Just enough that I can see it if I look.

The examination is soon.

No. I stop thinking about it.

But my hands keep shaking.

---

Master Feng Qiao had seen it happen before. The hands that knew, that had always known, becoming uncertain. The interval — *shoujue*, his own master had called it, though the term was not in any manual — disappearing like morning frost when the sun rose.

He stood in the doorway of the workshop, watching Chen Shu's hands shake on her knees. She did not know he was there. The silk on the drying rack behind her was perfect. Deep red, even color, no streaking. She had lifted it at exactly the right moment. Her hands had known.

But now her hands shook.

Feng remembered his own examination. He had been the same age Chen Shu was now. He had sat for it and passed — demonstration competent, verbal adequate, portfolio strong. Second-Tier certification. Enough to inherit his master's workshop, enough to take apprentices, enough to bid on palace commissions when they came.

But in the examination hall, with the officials watching, with the lamplight bright and the time limit pressing, his hands had hesitated. Not much. Just enough. Just that half-breath of uncertainty that meant he had to think about what he was doing instead of letting his hands do it.

He had passed anyway. The demonstration had been competent. Competent was enough.

But it had not been *shoujue*. It had been performance.

Chen Shu's hands on the drying rack, just now, lifting the silk — that had been *shoujue*. The real thing. The interval arriving in her body before thought, her hands moving with the certainty that came from repetitions too numerous to name, mornings alone in the workshop stretching back through seasons and years.

But her hands shook now.

Feng stepped into the workshop. Chen Shu looked up, startled, and immediately stood and bowed.

"Shifu," she said. "I did not hear you."

"The silk is good," Feng said. He walked to the drying rack, touched the edge of the fabric. Still damp, but the color was set. "Very good."

"Thank you, shifu."

"Your hands knew the interval."

Chen Shu looked down at her hands. They had stopped shaking. "Yes, shifu."

"But you are afraid they will not know it in the examination."

She did not answer. She did not need to. Her hands, clasped in front of her, told him everything.

"Come," Feng said. "We will practice the verbal examination again. The demonstration you know. The verbal is where you lose points."

---

The Directorate's announcement came days before the examination.

Master Feng read it aloud in the workshop while I stood with the younger apprentices, listening. His voice was steady, but I saw his hands tighten on the paper.

"*By order of the Directorate of Imperial Workshops, under the authority of the Ministry of Revenue: Beginning the first month of the fourth year of Chongning, all persons practicing crafts subject to certification who have not obtained official verification must do so within the prescribed period. After the prescribed period, practice without certification will be prohibited. Violators will be subject to fine and confiscation of tools.*"

The younger apprentices whispered to each other. They were too young to sit for the examination — you had to be at least of age, with years of apprenticeship already behind you. This did not affect them yet.

It affected me.

I had sat for the examination before. Failed before. I had another chance — this examination, days from now — and then I would have to wait. But if I failed this time, the prescribed period would narrow. Fewer chances remaining.

And if I failed those too...

Master Feng folded the paper and set it on the workbench. He looked at me. His face was very calm.

"Chen Shu," he said. "Come with me."

I followed him up the stairs to the third floor, to his small private room. He closed the door. The room smelled like sandalwood and old paper. There was a desk, a sleeping mat, a small chest for his clothes. On the wall, his certification seal, carved from jade, hanging from a red cord.

He sat at the desk. I stood.

"Sit," he said.

I sat on the floor. He did not tell me to sit on the stool. That would have been improper.

"The examination fee," he said. "Do you have it?"

"No, shifu."

"I will loan it to you."

I looked up at him. "Shifu, I cannot—"

"You can," he said. "Your hands know. The examination will see it."

I wanted to believe him. I wanted to believe that my hands, which knew the interval in the pre-dawn workshop, would know it in the examination hall. That the officials would see what Master Feng saw. That the demonstration would be more than competent.

But my hands, resting on my knees, were shaking again.

"Thank you, shifu," I said.

He opened the chest and took out coins wrapped in cloth. He unwrapped them and laid them on the desk between us. Strings of cash, more than I had ever held at once. He separated them — I watched his fingers move from string to string, touching each — then wrapped them again and gave them to me.

"Your hands know," he said again.

I took the coins. They were heavy. Heavier than silk, heavier than water, heavier than anything I had ever held.

---

I practice.

Every morning, after the pre-dawn work is done, after the interval has come and gone and my hands have lifted the silk at exactly the right moment, I practice the demonstration.

Master Feng sets the timer — a water clock, the kind they use in the examination hall. He gives me a bolt of undyed silk, a measure of madder root, the mordant in the proportions specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*. He sits on the stool and watches.

"Begin," he says.

And my hands become wood.

Not immediately. For the first few minutes, they remember. They prepare the mordant, they steep the root, they test the dye on a scrap of fabric. But then I become aware that Master Feng is watching. I become aware of the water clock dripping, dripping, marking the time. I become aware that I am performing, not inhabiting.

And my hands stop knowing.

The sensation is physical. My fingers, which moved with their own intelligence moments ago, suddenly need instruction. The interval — that space between perceiving and acting where the body knows what to do — collapses. What remains is thought, and thought is too slow. By the time I decide to lift the silk, the moment has passed. The root has steeped too long or not long enough. The color is acceptable but not right.

I finish the demonstration. I always finish. The silk comes out competent. Even color, no streaking, acceptable shade of red. Master Feng nods.

"Good," he says. "Now the verbal examination."

I stand in front of him. He becomes the examiner. His voice changes — becomes formal, distant, the voice of an official reading from a manual.

"Recite the contingency factors affecting madder dye quality as specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*."

I have memorized this. I have recited it every day for weeks. But standing in front of Master Feng, with his face formal and his voice distant, the words tangle in my mouth.

"The contingency factors are... uh... root quality... fiber preparation... uh..."

"Continue."

"If the root is old, then the steeping duration should be... longer? No, shorter? I..."

Master Feng's face softens. He is not the examiner anymore. He is my master again.

"Chen Shu," he says gently. "You know this. When you are working, your hands know when the root is old. They know how long to steep. Why can you not say it?"

"I do not know, shifu."

But I do know. My hands know in the doing. They do not know in the saying. The words in the manual — *contingency factors*, *optimal duration*, *fiber preparation* — these are not my words. These are the words of officials who have never held a piece of undyed silk, who have never smelled the mordant, who have never felt the interval.

My words are: *The root is ready, ne?* My words are: *Let it steep longer, ba.* My words are: *The fiber at the bottom did not take.*

But these are not the examination's words.

Master Feng sighs. "Again," he says. "From the beginning."

We practice until the water clock empties. Then we practice again. And again. Each time, my hands become wood when he says "begin." Each time, my mouth tangles on the manual's vocabulary. Each time, I finish with work that is competent but not *shoujue*.

On another practice, Master Feng stops me halfway through the verbal examination.

"Chen Shu," he says. His voice is quiet. "Why do you think the examination measures mastery?"

I do not understand the question. "Because... that is what it is for, shifu. To verify that we know our craft."

"No," he says. "That is not what it is for."

I wait. He looks at his hands — stained like mine, marked by decades of work.

"The examination measures whether you can produce acceptable work under observation," he says. "Whether you can recite the manual's vocabulary. Whether your work matches the standards when officials are watching. This is not the same as mastery."

"But shifu, you passed—"

"I passed," he says. "My work was competent. The officials nodded. I received certification. But in the examination hall, my hands did not know *shoujue*. They knew performance."

I look down at my own hands. They are not shaking now. They are still.

"Then how do I pass?" I ask.

"You learn to perform," he says. "You practice until your hands can produce acceptable work even when the interval collapses. You memorize the manual's words even though they are not your words. You become competent at being watched."

"But that is not—"

"No," he says. "It is not mastery. But it is what the examination measures."

We sit in silence. Outside, the afternoon market sounds drift up through the window. Vendors calling, customers bargaining, the city continuing.

"I do not know if I can do that, shifu," I say finally.

"I know," he says. "Neither did I."

But he passed. And I have failed before.

---

Days before the examination, I meet Old Dyer Wu.

I am at the market, buying more madder root. Master Feng has given me money for practice materials. The root seller knows me — I come here every week — and he gives me a good price.

Old Dyer Wu is at the next stall, buying alum. He is small, bent, his hands as stained as mine. He nods at me.

"You are Feng's apprentice," he says.

"Yes, *lao*." I use the respectful term for an elder.

"You sit for the examination soon, ne?"

"Yes, *lao*. Soon."

He grunts. "I sat for it. Failed. The verbal examination — I could never remember the manual's words. My hands knew, but my mouth did not."

I do not know what to say. Failed. Examination fees paid. Time spent.

"But you still work," I say.

"I still work." He gestures at the alum. "The merchants who know, they know. The ones who need a seal, let them buy from the certified ones."

"But the regulation—"

"The regulation says many things." He picks up his alum, then stops. Looks at me directly. "The examination fee was months of wages. I borrowed. From family, from merchants I worked for, from a moneylender the last time. I am still paying the moneylender."

He shifts the alum to his other arm. "My workshop is the size of a storage closet. I share it with a cobbler — he works days, I work nights. My wife left after I failed. Said I should have given up, found other work. Maybe she was right."

I stand very still, holding my madder root.

"But my hands still know," he says. "In the night, alone, when I lift the silk — they know. The examination never saw it. But it is real."

He starts to walk away, then turns back. "Your hands know, *xiao*. I have seen your work. The examination..." He shakes his head. "The examination sees other things."

After he leaves, I stand in the market for a long time. Around me, people buy and sell, bargain and laugh. The city continues. But I am thinking about Wu's wife who left. About the moneylender he still pays. About the workshop the size of a storage closet, shared with a cobbler.

About what it costs to keep working when the examination does not see what your hands know.

I walk back to Master Feng's workshop slowly. The coins for the examination fee are hidden in my room, wrapped in cloth, heavy with borrowed weight. When I arrive, Master Feng is at the vat, his hands in the dye. He does not look up, but I know he sees me.

"We practice again tomorrow," he says.

"Yes, shifu," I say.

But that night, I lie awake in the sleeping room, listening to the younger apprentices breathe. I think about Wu's workshop. About his wife. About the moneylender. About Master Feng's words: *You learn to perform.*

I think about my hands, which know *shoujue* here, in the dark, in the pre-dawn workshop where observation sleeps.

I think about what they will know in the examination hall.

---

The examination hall is in the Directorate compound, near the palace walls. I have been here before. I know the way.

Master Feng walks with me. He does not speak. Neither do I. What is there to say? My hands know, or they do not. The examination will see it, or it will not.

The compound is crowded. Dozens of artisans waiting for their examinations — potters, weavers, embroiderers, dyers. I see other dyers I know, both sitting for Second-Tier certification like me. They nod at me. I nod back.

Master Feng hands me the examination fee. The wrapped coins, heavy with borrowed weight. I take them to the registration table. The clerk unwraps them, separates them — I watch his fingers move from string to string, the same gesture Master Feng made when he loaned them to me — marks my name in the register, gives me a wooden token with a number on it.

"Dyeing examination, demonstration component," the clerk says. "Third hall, east wing. Wait for your number to be called."

I take the token. My hands are shaking.

Master Feng touches my shoulder. "Your hands know," he says once more.

Then he is gone, and I am alone in the crowd of artisans, holding my wooden token, waiting.

---

They call my number.

I follow a junior inspector down a long corridor to the third hall. The hall is large, with high ceilings and tall windows. Morning light falls across the workbenches. There are multiple workbenches, multiple sets of materials, multiple water clocks. Multiple artisans will be examined at once.

I am assigned to the middle bench. The inspector gives me my materials: a bolt of undyed silk, a measure of madder root, the mordant in the specified proportions, a vat of hot water. Everything I need.

The officials sit at a table at the front of the hall. The scholar-administrator in the middle, reading from the *Yingzao Fashi*. The senior certified dyer on the left, his face impassive. The junior inspector on the right, holding the measuring instruments.

I recognize the scholar-administrator. Supervisor Liu Han. He presided over my previous examination. He does not recognize me. Why would he? I am among many dyers he examines.

I stand at my bench. The other artisans — both men, both older than me — stand at their benches. We wait.

Supervisor Liu looks up from the manual. His voice is formal, distant, the voice of an official performing a duty.

"You will produce a bolt of silk dyed to the standard specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*. Madder red, even color, no streaking. You have the allotted time. Begin."

The water clocks start dripping.

And my hands become wood.

---

I know what to do. I have done it more mornings than I can name. I have done it every morning in the pre-dawn workshop for years. I have done it in the practice demonstrations with Master Feng watching.

But now, with the officials watching, with the water clock dripping, with the other artisans working at their benches beside me, I do not know.

My hands do not know.

I prepare. The mordant. The proportions. Specified. In the manual. Alum. And iron. Mixed. With water.

My hands. Measure. Mix. Pour.

They move. Through the steps.

But they do not move. With certainty.

They move. With thought. With effort. With the awareness. That I am performing.

The interval is gone. That space between perceiving and acting — it has collapsed. My hands, which moved with their own intelligence in the pre-dawn workshop, now wait for instruction. I tell them: measure the alum. I tell them: add the iron. I tell them: mix slowly.

But telling is too slow. By the time the instruction travels from thought to hand, the moment has shifted. The mixture is wrong. Too thin, maybe. Or too thick. I cannot tell which. I add more alum. Now it might be too thick. I add water. Now I do not know what it is.

I steep the madder root. The manual says optimal duration is contingent on root quality and fiber preparation. My hands should know when the root is ready. They should feel the interval, the moment when the root has given everything and will give no more.

But I do not feel it.

I wait. I test the dye on a scrap of fabric. The color is too light. I steep longer. I test again. Still too light. I steep longer.

The water clock drips. The sound fills the hall. Drip. Drip. Drip. Each drop a measurement of time passing, of the interval not arriving, of my hands not knowing.

I test again. The color is darker now. Maybe dark enough? I do not know. I cannot tell. My hands, which knew in the pre-dawn workshop, do not know here.

I lift the bolt of silk and submerge it in the dye. I work it through the bath, making sure every part of the fabric is covered. I lift it, wring it, submerge it again. The manual says this should be repeated.

I repeat it.

My hands move. They wring the fabric. But the pressure is wrong. Too much, and the color will streak. Too little, and the dye will pool. In the workshop, my hands know the right pressure without thinking. Here, I think about it. And thinking makes it wrong.

I lift the silk and hang it on the drying rack. The color is... acceptable. Even, no streaking. Maybe a little light, but within the acceptable range.

Maybe.

The water clock stops dripping. Time is finished.

Supervisor Liu stands and walks to my bench. He examines the silk. His face shows nothing. The senior certified dyer joins him, touches the fabric, nods once. The junior inspector measures the color against the standard samples.

They do not speak to me. They move to the next bench, examine that artisan's work, then the third.

Then they return to their table and write in their ledgers.

"Dismissed," Supervisor Liu says. "Scores will be announced shortly."

---

I wait in the courtyard with the other artisans. Master Feng is there, sitting on a stone bench near the gate. I sit beside him. He does not ask how it went. He does not need to ask.

"The silk was competent," I say.

"Good."

"But it was not..."

I do not finish. Master Feng knows what I mean.

Time passes. The junior inspector comes out and reads from a list.

"Dyeing examination, demonstration component. Chen Shu, adequate."

I close my eyes.

Adequate. Competent. Acceptable. Not failing, but not passing either. The demonstration is only part of the total score. I still have the verbal examination and the portfolio.

But I know already. I know from the shaking in my hands, from the interval that did not come, from the silk that was acceptable but not right.

I know.

---

The verbal examination is worse.

Supervisor Liu asks me to recite the contingency factors affecting madder dye quality. I open my mouth. The words tangle.

"The contingency factors are... root quality... fiber preparation..."

"Continue."

"If the root is old, then... uh... *ruo gen lao*..." I slip into the classical register, then back into vernacular, then neither. "The duration should be... I mean, the optimal duration is... uh..."

"Specify the optimal duration for aged root material."

My mouth opens. Nothing comes out. I know the answer — my hands know the answer — but the words the manual uses are not the words I know. The manual says: *optimal duration inversely correlates with root age and directly correlates with fiber preparation completeness.* But my hands say: steep it longer if the root is old, unless the fiber is very clean, then not as long.

These are not the same language.

"I... I do not remember, *daren*."

Supervisor Liu writes in his ledger. His face shows nothing.

The examination continues. More questions, more tangled words, more silences where there should be answers. My mouth does not know what my hands know. The manual's vocabulary is not my vocabulary.

I try. I try to translate what my hands know into the manual's language. But the translation always fails. The manual speaks in abstractions — *contingency factors*, *optimal parameters*, *quality variance*. My hands speak in specifics — *this root*, *this water*, *this moment*.

When it is over, Supervisor Liu reads my score aloud.

"Verbal examination. Chen Shu, inadequate."

I bow and leave.

---

The portfolio component is the only part I pass. Strong. The pieces I submitted — silk dyed in my master's workshop, marked with my unregistered seal — are good. They are better than good. They are *shoujue*.

But the portfolio is only part of the total score.

Supervisor Liu calculates the final score and reads it aloud in the main hall, where all the examinees are gathered.

"Chen Shu. Demonstration adequate, verbal inadequate, portfolio strong. Overall: fail."

I bow. My hands are steady now. They do not shake anymore. There is nothing to shake for.

I walk out of the Directorate compound into the afternoon sunlight. Master Feng is waiting by the gate.

"I am sorry, shifu," I say.

"I know," he says.

We walk back to the workshop together. Neither of us speaks. What is there to say?

I have time remaining. Another examination. Another chance.

And if I fail again, the regulation says I must cease practice.

But Master Feng does not say this. Neither do I. We both know it already.

---

After the examination, I move to Old Dyer Wu's workshop.

Not immediately. First there are weeks of silence in Master Feng's workshop. I work in the pre-dawn hours as always. My hands know the interval as they always have. But now there is a weight in the air, a knowledge that sits between Master Feng and me like a third person in the room.

He does not ask if I will sit for the examination again. I do not tell him I cannot afford another loan.

Wu finds me at the market. "I heard," he says. "Failed, ne?"

"Yes, *lao*."

"Come work with me," he says. "The cobbler leaves at dusk. You can use the workshop nights. I take mornings. We split the vat time."

I look at him. His bent back, his stained hands, his face marked by years of working in the margins.

"The regulation—" I start.

"The regulation gives you time," he says. "Use it. Build your clients now, while you still can. When the deadline comes..." He shrugs. "We decide then."

So I move to Wu's workshop. Master Feng does not try to stop me. He helps me carry my things — my small bundle of clothes, my unregistered seal, the practice pieces I have saved. When we arrive at Wu's door, he sets down the bundle and looks at me.

"The debt," he says. "I forgive it."

"Shifu—"

"All of it," he says. "You owe me nothing."

I want to argue. I want to say I will repay him. But we both know I cannot. Not without certification. Not working nights in a workshop the size of a storage closet.

But the debt does not disappear. It transforms. Into the knowledge that he spent months of income on my examinations and will never recover it. Into the weight of his generosity, which I can never repay and which he will never mention again. Into the fact that my continued practice costs him money he has already lost. The forgiveness makes the debt heavier, not lighter. It changes from something I might someday repay into something I will carry forever.

"Thank you, shifu," I say.

He nods once, then leaves. He does not look back.

Wu shows me the workshop. It is smaller than I expected. A single vat, barely large enough for a bolt of silk. A rope strung across the room for drying. The smell of leather from the cobbler's work mixing with the smell of mordants. Barely enough room for us both to stand.

"The cobbler is Zhao," Wu says. "Good man. Keeps to himself. Doesn't ask questions about the regulation."

"And your clients?"

"Merchants who have known me for years," Wu says. "They know my work. They don't need certification to tell them what their eyes can see. But there are not many. Most buyers want the seal."

He shows me the vat, the mordants, where he keeps the madder root. Everything is organized, efficient, compressed into the small space.

"You work nights," he says. "I work mornings. We don't overlap. The vat is shared — you clean it after each use. The cobbler doesn't touch it."

"And the regulation?"

Wu's face hardens. "The regulation says I should have stopped years ago. But my hands still know. So I work. If they catch me, I pay the fine. If I cannot pay the fine..." He shrugs. "Then they take the tools. But until then, I work."

That night, I dye my first bolt of silk in Wu's workshop. Alone, in the dark, with observation sleeping. My hands remember. The interval returns. The silk comes out perfect — deep red, even color, no streaking.

I hang it on the rope to dry and sit on the stool by the vat. My hands are steady. They do not shake.

This is *shoujue*. The examination never saw it. But it is real.

---

Wu's clients come at night.

The first is Merchant Gao. He arrives after the cobbler has left, knocks twice on the door. Wu lets him in.

"This is Chen Shu," Wu says. "She works with me now. Her hands know."

Merchant Gao looks at me. His face is careful, assessing. "You have certification?"

"No, *daren*."

"But your work is good?"

"Yes, *daren*."

He looks at Wu. Wu nods.

"Show me," Merchant Gao says.

I show him the silk I dyed the night before. He examines it — holds it up to the lamplight, rubs the fabric between his fingers, smells it. His face shows nothing.

Then he nods. "Good. Very good. I will buy this bolt. And I will bring you more work."

He pays Wu. Not as much as a certified dyer would charge, but enough. Wu gives me half.

After Merchant Gao leaves, Wu says, "He takes a risk buying from us. If the palace inspectors find uncertified work in his inventory, he pays a fine. But he knows our work is better than what the certified dyers produce. So he takes the risk."

"How many clients like him?" I ask.

"A few," Wu says. "Enough to survive. Not enough to thrive."

Over the following weeks, I meet Wu's other clients. Merchant Zhao, who buys silk for his daughter's wedding. Merchant Sun, who supplies a small shop in the market. Merchant Li, who exports to the south. All of them know Wu's work. All of them take the risk of buying uncertified.

And all of them, when they see my work, nod and say: "Good. Very good."

But there are not many of them. Most buyers want the seal. They want the official verification. They want to know what they are getting, and certification is how they know.

Wu and I work in the margins. We survive. But we do not thrive.

And Wu still pays the moneylender. I see him set aside coins every month, his face tight, the stack never quite enough. The debt from his examination fees, still unpaid after all these years. His wife left because of that debt. His workshop is the size of a storage closet because of that debt.

Surviving in the margins is not stable. It is not an alternative to the examination. It is a slow collapse that has not finished collapsing yet. Wu's bent back, his tight face, his missing wife — these are not the price he paid. These are the price he is still paying. And will keep paying. And I have chosen the same path.

---

Months after I move to Wu's workshop, Master Feng comes to visit.

I am at the vat, my hands in the dye. Wu is in the back room, preparing indigo. Master Feng stands in the doorway, watching.

I see him and straighten. "Shifu," I say.

"I came to see how you are," he says.

"I am well, shifu."

I hang the silk on the rope. The color is deep red, even, perfect. Master Feng recognizes *shoujue* when he sees it.

"Wu's clients are good?" he asks.

"They are enough," I say. "They know his work. They do not ask for certification."

Master Feng nods. He looks around the small workshop. It smells like mordants and damp wool, like his own workshop but more concentrated. More desperate.

"The regulation," he says. "You have time remaining."

"I know, shifu."

"Will you sit for the examination again?"

I look at my hands. They are steady. The interval has returned. Here, in this small workshop, with the officials sleeping, my hands know again.

"I do not know, shifu," I say. "The examination fee..."

I do not finish. Master Feng knows what I mean. I already owe him for the previous examination — or I did, before he forgave the debt. Another loan would add months more. And if I failed again — when I failed again — I would have no way to repay it. Not if the regulation forced me to stop working.

"The debt," Master Feng says. "I meant what I said. You owe me nothing."

But that is not true. The debt is still there. It has just changed form. It is the knowledge that he spent months of income on my examination and will never recover it. It is the weight of his generosity, which I can never repay. It is the fact that my failure cost him money he has already lost.

"Shifu," I say quietly. "You knew I would not pass."

It is not a question.

Master Feng does not answer immediately. He looks at the silk hanging on the rope, at my stained hands, at the small workshop where *shoujue* persists in the absence of certification.

"Yes," he says finally. "I knew."

"Then why—"

"Because I did not know what else to do," he says. "The regulation says you must be certified. I thought... I thought if you tried enough times, if you practiced enough, you would learn to perform. You would learn to make your hands move even when the interval collapsed. You would pass, and you would inherit the workshop, and everything would continue."

"But that is not what happened," I say.

"No," he says. "That is not what happened."

We stand in silence. Outside, the afternoon market sounds drift through the walls. The city continuing.

"I should have told you," Master Feng says. "I should have told you that the examination measures the wrong thing. That passing it would not make you more skilled, would not make your hands know more than they already know. I should have told you that *shoujue* exists only in the unobserved practice, and that the examination would never see it."

"You did tell me, shifu," I say. "Before the examination. You said the examination measures performance, not mastery."

"But I still sent you," Master Feng says. "I still loaned you the money. I still made you believe that passing was possible, that it was necessary."

"It was necessary," I say. "Without certification, I cannot inherit your workshop. Without the workshop, I have nothing."

"You have this," Master Feng says, gesturing at the small room, the vat, the silk drying on the rope. "You have your hands, which know. You have clients who see what the examination cannot see."

"I have a room the size of a storage closet," I say. "Shared with a cobbler. I have a few clients who know Wu's work, who are willing to risk buying from an uncertified dyer. I have time remaining before the regulation says I must stop working."

My voice is calm, but Master Feng hears the weight underneath it.

"And I have hands that know *shoujue*," I continue. "That lift the silk at exactly the right moment, that feel the interval before thought arrives. But the examination never saw it. And when the regulation's deadline comes, it will not matter that my hands know. I will have to stop anyway."

Master Feng does not know what to say. I am right. All of it is true.

"I am sorry," he says finally.

"I know, shifu," I say.

He stands there a moment longer, then turns to leave. At the door, he stops.

"I will retire next year," he says. "The workshop... I will sell it to a certified dyer. It is a good workshop. It deserves to continue."

"Yes, shifu," I say.

He leaves. Wu comes out from the back room. We do not speak. What is there to say?

---

The regulation's deadline approaches.

I track it in my body. Not with numbers, not with calendars, but with the weight that grows heavier each morning when I wake. The prescribed period. The time when I must cease practice or work illegally.

Wu and I do not talk about it. But I see him looking at me sometimes, when he thinks I am not watching. His face is careful, assessing. He is wondering what I will choose.

I am wondering too.

I could sit for the examination again. Master Feng would loan me the fee — he has already said he forgives the debt, which means he would loan me more. I could practice the demonstration, memorize the manual's vocabulary, stand in the examination hall and let my hands become wood once more.

And I would fail again. I know this. The examination measures performance, not mastery. My hands know *shoujue*, but *shoujue* is not what the examination sees.

Or I could stop. Find other work. Marry, perhaps. Leave the craft that my hands know but the examination cannot measure.

Or I could continue. Work illegally. Risk the fine, risk confiscation. Survive in the margins like Wu has survived — except Wu's survival is not stable. His wife left. He still pays the moneylender. His workshop is the size of a storage closet. This is not thriving. This is collapsing slowly.

I do not know which choice to make.

But the deadline is coming. And I must choose.

---

The deadline arrives on a morning like any other.

I wake in the fourth watch, as I always have. The workshop is dark, silent except for the dripping of water from the vat. I light a lamp and go downstairs. The madder root is steeping in the small vat. I prepared it yesterday evening, and now it is ready.

I know this before I know I know it.

My hands rest on the rim of the vat. They tell me. The interval between breathing out and breathing in. The moment when the root has given everything and will give no more.

I lift the bolt of silk. Heavy with dye, heavy with water. The red is deep, clean, right.

I hang it on the rope to dry. My hands know how to wring it, how much pressure, which direction. I do not think about these things. My hands do them.

The regulation says I should have ceased practice by now. The prescribed period has ended. I am working illegally.

But my hands still know.

I rinse them in the bucket of clean water. The water turns pink, then clear. My hands are never clean. The red has soaked into the skin, into the lines of my palms, into the beds of my fingernails.

I do not mind.

Outside, the sky is beginning to lighten. The night soil carriers are finishing their rounds. The vegetable sellers will arrive soon. The city is waking up.

I sit on the stool by the vat and rest my hands on my knees. They are not shaking.

The interval is here. It has always been here. The examination could not measure it, could not see it, could not certify it.

But it is mine.

Wu comes down the stairs. He sees the silk on the rope, sees me sitting by the vat. His face shows nothing, but I see the question in his eyes.

"I am staying," I say.

He nods once. "Good."

"The fine—"

"We pay it if they catch us," he says. "I have been paying it for years. You will too."

"And if I cannot pay?"

"Then they take the tools," Wu says. "But until then, we work."

He goes to the back room to prepare his morning batch. I stand and begin cleaning the vat. The work continues.

But I know what Wu is not saying. The fine grows. The moneylender still waits. His wife is still gone. The workshop is still the size of a storage closet. This is not a sustainable alternative. This is working until you cannot work anymore. And I have chosen it anyway. Not because it is stable. Not because it will work out. But because my hands still know, and I do not know how to make them stop.

---

Weeks after the deadline, the inspector comes.

I am at the market, buying madder root. The root seller knows me now — I come here every week. He is wrapping my purchase when I see the inspector approaching.

I recognize him from the examination hall. Junior inspector, the man who measured the color against the standard samples during my demonstration. He does not recognize me. Why would he? I am among many failed examinees he has seen.

But he sees the madder root in my hands. He sees the stains on my fingers. He knows what they mean.

"You are a dyer," he says.

"Yes, *daren*."

"You have certification?"

My mouth is dry. "No, *daren*."

He takes out his ledger. "Name."

"Chen Shu."

He writes it down. "The regulation requires certification. You are in violation. The fine is..." He consults the ledger. "...payable within the month. If you cannot pay, your tools will be confiscated."

He tears a page from the ledger and gives it to me. The fine is written there. Months of wages. More than I have saved.

"You have been notified," he says. Then he leaves.

I stand in the market, holding the madder root and the fine notice. Around me, people buy and sell, bargain and laugh. The city continues.

I walk back to Wu's workshop slowly. When I arrive, Wu is at the vat. I show him the notice.

He reads it, nods. "I have received many of these. We will pay what we can. The rest..." He shrugs. "We negotiate."

"And if they will not negotiate?"

"Then they take the tools," Wu says. "And we find other tools. Or we work with our hands alone. Or we stop." He looks at me. "But we do not stop easily."

That night, I dye silk as I always have. My hands know the interval. The silk comes out perfect.

The examination never saw this. The inspector who wrote the fine notice never saw this. Supervisor Liu, with his manual and his measuring instruments, never saw this.

But it is real.

---

I pay the fine in installments.

Wu helps me negotiate with the inspector. We pay what we can each month. It is not enough — the debt grows faster than we can pay it — but it is something. The inspector accepts it. For now.

"Eventually they will demand full payment," Wu says. "When that happens, we decide. Pay in full, or let them take the tools."

"And if they take the tools?"

"Then we find other tools," Wu says. "Or we work with our hands alone."

But I know what he is not saying. If they take the tools, we cannot work. Not really. Not the way our hands know how to work.

The debt grows. The inspector comes every month. I pay what I can. It is never enough.

Wu still pays his moneylender. I see him set aside coins, his face tight, the stack never quite enough. Years of debt, still unpaid. His wife still gone. His workshop still the size of a storage closet.

This is what surviving in the margins looks like. Not stable. Not sustainable. Just continuing until it cannot continue anymore.

But I keep working.

---

Months after the deadline, Master Feng sells his workshop.

I hear about it from Merchant Gao. "Feng is retiring," he says. "Sold the workshop to a certified dyer from the north. Good price, I heard."

I do not go to see Master Feng. What would I say? The workshop was his to sell. He needed to retire. The certified dyer from the north will continue the work, will train the younger apprentices, will bid on palace commissions.

Everything will continue. Just without me.

But weeks later, Master Feng comes to Wu's workshop. I am at the vat, my hands in the dye. He stands in the doorway, watching.

"Shifu," I say.

"I came to say goodbye," he says. "I am leaving the city. Going back to my village."

"I heard you sold the workshop," I say.

"Yes." He looks around Wu's small room. "This is where you will stay?"

"Yes, shifu."

"The fine—"

"I am paying it," I say. "In installments. It is not enough, but it is something."

Master Feng reaches into his robe and takes out a small pouch. He sets it on the workbench. "This will help."

I look at the pouch. I know what it contains. Coins. Enough to pay several months of the fine.

"Shifu, I cannot—"

"You can," he says. "I sold the workshop for a good price. I do not need all of it. Take this. Pay the fine. Keep working."

I want to refuse. I want to say I have taken enough from him already. But I know he will not accept my refusal. And I know what this means. More debt. More money he has spent on me that I will never repay. More weight added to the weight I already carry. The debt that was forgiven now growing again, heavier than before, compounding with interest that has nothing to do with money.

"Thank you, shifu," I say.

He nods. Then he looks at the silk on the rope, at my stained hands, at the small workshop where *shoujue* persists.

"Your hands still know," he says.

"Yes, shifu."

"Good." He turns to leave, then stops. "The examination was wrong. I should have told you that from the beginning. It measures the wrong thing. Your failures were not failures of skill. They were failures of the examination."

"I know, shifu," I say.

"But I sent you anyway," he says. "Because I did not know what else to do. I am sorry for that."

"I know, shifu," I say again.

He leaves. I do not see him again.

But the pouch of coins remains on the workbench. I use it to pay the fine. It buys me time. Not much. But something.

And the debt to Master Feng grows larger. Money I will never repay. Generosity I can never match. The knowledge that my continued practice costs him more than I can ever give back. The knowledge that he knows this too, and gives anyway, which makes it worse.

---

The work continues.

I wake in the fourth watch. I go down to the workshop. My hands know the interval. I lift the silk at exactly the right moment. The red is deep, clean, right.

Wu's clients come at night. They buy my work. They do not ask for certification. They know what their eyes can see.

The inspector comes every month. I pay what I can. The debt grows.

Master Feng's coins run out. The fine grows faster than I can pay it. Wu's moneylender still waits for his payments. The workshop is still the size of a storage closet.

But my hands still know.

How long can I continue? I do not know. Wu has been working without certification for years. He pays the fines when he can. He negotiates when he cannot. He survives.

But his wife is gone. His debt is unpaid. His workshop is a storage closet. This is not thriving. This is not even stable survival. This is collapsing slowly, and the collapse has not finished yet.

I have chosen the same path. I know what it costs. I see it in Wu's bent back, in his tight face when he sets aside coins for the moneylender, in the smallness of the room we share with a cobbler. I see it in the growing stack of fine notices, in Master Feng's coins running out, in the inspector's monthly visits.

The debt grows. The inspector comes. The fine mounts. The tools may be confiscated. The work may become impossible.

But my hands still know the interval. And that is real.

The examination never saw this. The officials never saw this. Supervisor Liu, with his manual and his measuring instruments, never saw this.

But it is mine.

The silk on the rope, drying in the darkness, is real. The merchants who buy it know. They do not need certification to tell them what their eyes can see.

And my hands, which became wood in the examination hall, are steady here.

I rinse them in the bucket of clean water. The water turns pink, then clear. My hands are never clean. The red has soaked into the skin, into the lines of my palms, into the beds of my fingernails.

I do not mind.

Outside, the sky is beginning to lighten. The city is waking up.

I sit on the stool by the vat and rest my hands on my knees. They are not shaking.

The interval is here. It has always been here.

The debt grows. The inspector comes. The fine mounts. The tools may be confiscated. The work may become impossible.

But for now, in this moment, in the pre-dawn darkness, my hands know.

And the work continues.
