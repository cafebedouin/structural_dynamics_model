# The Interval

The madder root is ready.

I know this before I know I know it. My hands — stained the color of old blood, cracked at the knuckles where the mordant bites deepest — rest on the rim of the vat, and they tell me. Not through thought. Through something older than thought. The interval between one breath and the next, when the root has given everything it has to give and will give no more.

I lift the bolt of silk. Heavy with dye, heavy with water. The red is deep, the color of pomegranate seeds split open. Not the brownish red that comes from steeping too long, when the tannins cloud the color and make it muddy. This is clean. This is right.

The sky through the workshop's high window is still dark. Fourth watch, maybe halfway through. The street below is silent except for the night soil carriers and their carts. This is my time. The time when no one watches, no one asks questions, no one says *begin* and makes my hands turn to wood.

I hang the silk on the drying rack. My hands know how to wring it, how much pressure, which direction. I do not think about these things. My hands do them while I am thinking about other things — about whether there is enough millet for breakfast, about whether Master Feng will need me to prepare the indigo bath today, about the examination.

No. I stop thinking about the examination.

I rinse my hands in the bucket of clean water. The water turns pink, then clear again as I scrub. My hands are never clean. The red has soaked into the skin, into the lines of my palms, into the beds of my fingernails. Sometimes I think the red has soaked all the way through to the bone.

The workshop is narrow. Three vats on the ground floor, drying racks along the walls, the smell of mordants so thick you can taste it. Alum, iron, urine left to ferment in clay jars. Upstairs, more racks. Upstairs above that, the sleeping room I share with the two younger apprentices. They are still asleep. They will wake when the bell at the ward gate rings for the fifth watch.

I have maybe half an hour before Master Feng comes down.

I sit on the stool by the smallest vat and rest my hands on my knees. They are shaking. Just a little. Just enough that I can see it if I look.

The examination is in six days.

No. I stop thinking about it.

But my hands keep shaking.

---

Master Feng Qiao had seen it happen before. The hands that knew, that had always known, becoming uncertain. The interval — *shoujue*, his own master had called it, though the term was not in any manual — disappearing like morning frost when the sun rose.

He stood in the doorway of the workshop, watching Chen Shu's hands shake on her knees. She did not know he was there. The silk on the drying rack behind her was perfect. Deep red, even color, no streaking. She had lifted it at exactly the right moment. Her hands had known.

But now her hands shook.

Feng remembered his own examination, thirty-two years ago. He had been twenty-six, the same age Chen Shu was now. He had sat for it once and passed — demonstration 75%, verbal 68%, portfolio 81%. Second-Tier certification. Enough to inherit his master's workshop, enough to take apprentices, enough to bid on palace commissions when they came.

He remembered the demonstration component. Four hours to produce a bolt of silk dyed to the *Yingzao Fashi* standards: madder red, even color, specific mordant proportions documented in section twelve, procedure four. He remembered the three officials watching him. The scholar-administrator who had never held a piece of undyed silk in his life, reading from the manual. The senior certified dyer who had not dyed anything in fifteen years, nodding or shaking his head. The junior inspector with his measuring instruments, checking the color against the standard samples.

He remembered his hands. They had known the interval in the practice sessions, in his master's workshop, in the pre-dawn hours when no one was watching. But in the examination hall, with the officials watching, with the lamplight bright and the time limit pressing, his hands had hesitated. Not much. Just enough. Just that half-breath of uncertainty that meant he had to think about what he was doing instead of letting his hands do it.

He had passed anyway. The demonstration had been competent. Competent was enough.

But it had not been *shoujue*. It had been performance.

Chen Shu's hands on the drying rack, just now, lifting the silk — that had been *shoujue*. The real thing. The interval arriving in her body before thought, her hands moving with the certainty that came from ten thousand repetitions, ten thousand mornings alone in the workshop.

But her hands shook now.

Feng stepped into the workshop. Chen Shu looked up, startled, and immediately stood and bowed.

"Shifu," she said. "I did not hear you."

"The silk is good," Feng said. He walked to the drying rack, touched the edge of the fabric. Still damp, but the color was set. "Very good."

"Thank you, shifu."

"Your hands knew the interval."

Chen Shu looked down at her hands. They had stopped shaking. "Yes, shifu."

"But you are afraid they will not know it in the examination."

She did not answer. She did not need to. Her hands, clasped in front of her, told him everything.

"Come," Feng said. "We will practice the verbal examination again. The demonstration you know. The verbal is where you lose points."

---

The Directorate's announcement came three days before the examination.

Master Feng read it aloud in the workshop while I stood with the two younger apprentices, listening. His voice was steady, but I saw his hands tighten on the paper.

"*By order of the Directorate of Imperial Workshops, under the authority of the Ministry of Revenue: Beginning the first month of the fourth year of Chongning, all persons practicing crafts subject to certification who have not obtained official verification must do so within two years. After two years, practice without certification will be prohibited. Violators will be subject to fine and confiscation of tools.*"

The younger apprentices whispered to each other. They were too young to sit for the examination — you had to be at least twenty, with at least five years of apprenticeship. This did not affect them yet.

It affected me.

I had sat for the examination twice. Failed twice. I had one more chance — this examination, six days from now — and then I would have to wait another year. But if I failed this time, I would have only one year left. One more examination. One more chance.

And if I failed that one...

Master Feng folded the paper and set it on the workbench. He looked at me. His face was very calm.

"Chen Shu," he said. "Come with me."

I followed him up the stairs to the third floor, to his small private room. He closed the door. The room smelled like sandalwood and old paper. There was a desk, a sleeping mat, a small chest for his clothes. On the wall, his certification seal, carved from jade, hanging from a red cord.

He sat at the desk. I stood.

"Sit," he said.

I sat on the floor. He did not tell me to sit on the stool. That would have been improper.

"The examination fee," he said. "Do you have it?"

"No, shifu."

"I will loan it to you."

I looked up at him. "Shifu, I cannot—"

"You can," he said. "Your hands know. The examination will see it."

I wanted to believe him. I wanted to believe that my hands, which knew the interval in the pre-dawn workshop, would know it in the examination hall. That the officials would see what Master Feng saw. That the demonstration would be more than competent.

But my hands, resting on my knees, were shaking again.

"Thank you, shifu," I said.

He counted out the coins from the chest. Twelve strings of one hundred wen each. Three months of my wages. He wrapped them in cloth and gave them to me.

"Your hands know," he said again.

I took the coins. They were heavy. Heavier than silk, heavier than water, heavier than anything I had ever held.

---

Feng watched her leave the room, the wrapped coins clutched against her chest. He knew what she was thinking. He had thought the same thing, thirty-two years ago, when his own master had loaned him the examination fee.

*My hands know. The examination will see it.*

But the examination did not see *shoujue*. It saw performance. And performance was not the same thing.

He had not told her this. What good would it do? She needed to sit for the examination. She needed to pass. Without certification, she could not inherit the workshop when he retired. Without the workshop, she had nothing. She would work as an uncertified dyer at half wages for the rest of her life, if the new regulation allowed even that.

He stood and looked out the small window. The street below was filling with morning traffic. Vegetable sellers, water carriers, a man leading a donkey loaded with bolts of raw silk. The city waking up.

There were other uncertified dyers in the ward. Old Dyer Wu, three streets over. He had sat for the examination four times, failed four times, and now worked out of a room barely large enough for one vat. He had clients — merchants who had known him for twenty years, who knew his work was good, who did not care about certification. But those clients were few. Most buyers wanted the seal, the official verification. They wanted to know what they were getting.

That was what the system provided. Coordination. A legible hierarchy. Second-Tier Certified Dyer meant something. It meant the work would match the standards, would be competent, would be acceptable to the palace procurement officers.

It did not mean *shoujue*. But *shoujue* was not what the system measured.

Feng turned from the window. The room was dim, the morning light not yet reaching this high. He looked at his certification seal on the wall. Jade, carved with his name and the Directorate's mark. He had hung it there thirty-two years ago and had not taken it down since.

He wondered if Chen Shu would ever hang her own seal on this wall.

He wondered if the examination would see what he saw.

---

I practice.

Every morning, after the pre-dawn work is done, after the interval has come and gone and my hands have lifted the silk at exactly the right moment, I practice the demonstration.

Master Feng sets the timer — a water clock, the kind they use in the examination hall. Four hours. He gives me a bolt of undyed silk, a measure of madder root, the mordant in the proportions specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*. He sits on the stool and watches.

"Begin," he says.

And my hands become wood.

Not immediately. For the first few minutes, they remember. They prepare the mordant, they steep the root, they test the dye on a scrap of fabric. But then I become aware that Master Feng is watching. I become aware of the water clock dripping, dripping, marking the time. I become aware that I am performing, not inhabiting.

And my hands stop knowing.

I finish the demonstration. I always finish. The silk comes out competent. Even color, no streaking, acceptable shade of red. Master Feng nods.

"Good," he says. "Now the verbal examination."

I stand in front of him. He becomes the examiner. His voice changes — becomes formal, distant, the voice of an official reading from a manual.

"Recite the contingency factors affecting madder dye quality as specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*, section twelve, procedure four."

I have memorized this. I have recited it every day for three weeks. But standing in front of Master Feng, with his face formal and his voice distant, the words tangle in my mouth.

"The contingency factors are... uh... root quality... fiber preparation... uh..."

"Continue."

"If the root is old, then the steeping duration should be... longer? No, shorter? I..."

Master Feng's face softens. He is not the examiner anymore. He is my master again.

"Chen Shu," he says gently. "You know this. When you are working, your hands know when the root is old. They know how long to steep. Why can you not say it?"

"I do not know, shifu."

But I do know. My hands know in the doing. They do not know in the saying. The words in the manual — *contingency factors*, *optimal duration*, *fiber preparation* — these are not my words. These are the words of officials who have never held a piece of undyed silk, who have never smelled the mordant, who have never felt the interval.

My words are: *The root is ready, ne?* My words are: *Let it steep longer, ba.* My words are: *The fiber at the bottom did not take.*

But these are not the examination's words.

Master Feng sighs. "Again," he says. "From the beginning."

---

Two days before the examination, I meet Old Dyer Wu.

I am at the market, buying more madder root. Master Feng has given me money for practice materials. The root seller knows me — I come here every week — and he gives me a good price.

Old Dyer Wu is at the next stall, buying alum. He is small, bent, his hands as stained as mine. He nods at me.

"You are Feng's apprentice," he says.

"Yes, *lao*." I use the respectful term for an elder.

"You sit for the examination soon, ne?"

"Yes, *lao*. Two days."

He grunts. "I sat four times. Failed four times. The verbal examination — I could never remember the manual's words. My hands knew, but my mouth did not."

I do not know what to say. Four times. Four examination fees. Four years of waiting.

"But you still work," I say.

"I still work." He gestures at the alum. "The merchants who know, they know. The ones who need a seal, let them buy from the certified ones."

"But the regulation—"

"The regulation says many things." He picks up his alum and turns to go, then stops. "Your hands know, *xiao*. I have seen your work. The examination..." He shakes his head. "The examination sees other things."

He walks away.

I stand in the market with my madder root, thinking about what he said. *The merchants who know, they know.* But how many merchants know? How many clients does Old Dyer Wu have? Enough to survive, clearly. But enough to live well? Enough to take apprentices, to inherit a workshop, to hang a certification seal on the wall?

I do not think so.

I walk back to the workshop. My hands, carrying the madder root, are steady. But inside, something is shaking.

---

The night before the examination, Feng could not sleep.

He lay on his mat in the small room, listening to the night sounds of the city. Dogs barking, a baby crying in a nearby building, the watch drum marking the hours. He thought about Chen Shu, sleeping upstairs. He thought about her hands, which knew *shoujue* in the pre-dawn workshop but became wood in the practice demonstrations.

He thought about the examination he would never sit for again. First-Tier certification. Higher wages, better commissions, the palace's most prestigious work. He had thought about it, years ago. He had even saved the examination fee. But he had never sat for it.

Why?

Because he knew what the examination measured. Performance under observation. The ability to produce work matching the standards in four hours with three officials watching. The ability to recite the manual's vocabulary without stumbling.

He could do these things. He had done them once, thirty-two years ago, and passed. He could probably do them again.

But he would not do them with *shoujue*. He would do them with competence, with experience, with the muscle memory of forty years of practice. His hands would move through the demonstration, and the officials would nod, and he would pass.

And it would mean nothing.

The First-Tier seal would hang on his wall next to the Second-Tier seal, and clients would pay him more, and the palace would send him more prestigious commissions, and his hands would still know the interval in the pre-dawn workshop when no one was watching.

But the examination would never see it.

He had made his peace with this. The system provided coordination. Buyers knew what Second-Tier Certified meant. The standards were arbitrary — he knew this, had known it since his apprenticeship, when his master's practices diverged from the *Yingzao Fashi* and he had to learn both — but arbitrary standards were better than no standards at all. The palace needed a way to evaluate artisans. The examination was that way.

It was inaccurate. But it was stable.

Chen Shu did not understand this yet. She thought the examination measured mastery. She thought her failures meant her hands were inadequate. She thought if she practiced enough, memorized enough, performed well enough, the examination would see what he saw.

He wanted to tell her: *The examination will never see it. Shoujue exists only in the unobserved practice. The moment the examiner says 'begin,' the interval collapses. What remains is performance, and performance is not the same thing.*

But he did not tell her this. What good would it do? She needed to pass. She needed certification. Without it, she had nothing.

He closed his eyes and tried to sleep. Outside, the watch drum marked the third watch. Three hours until dawn. Three hours until Chen Shu would wake and go down to the workshop, alone, and her hands would know the interval one more time before the examination took it away.

---

The examination hall is in the Directorate compound, near the palace walls. I have been here twice before. I know the way.

Master Feng walks with me. He does not speak. Neither do I. What is there to say? My hands know, or they do not. The examination will see it, or it will not.

The compound is crowded. Dozens of artisans waiting for their examinations — potters, weavers, embroiderers, dyers. I see two other dyers I know, both sitting for Second-Tier certification like me. They nod at me. I nod back.

Master Feng hands me the examination fee. Twelve strings of coins, wrapped in cloth. I take them to the registration table. The clerk counts them, marks my name in the register, gives me a wooden token with a number on it.

"Dyeing examination, demonstration component," the clerk says. "Third hall, east wing. Wait for your number to be called."

I take the token. My hands are shaking.

Master Feng touches my shoulder. "Your hands know," he says one more time.

Then he is gone, and I am alone in the crowd of artisans, holding my wooden token, waiting.

---

They call my number.

I follow a junior inspector down a long corridor to the third hall. The hall is large, with high ceilings and tall windows. Morning light falls across the workbenches. There are three workbenches, three sets of materials, three water clocks. Three artisans will be examined at once.

I am assigned to the middle bench. The inspector gives me my materials: one bolt of undyed silk, one measure of madder root, the mordant in the specified proportions, a vat of hot water. Everything I need.

The three officials sit at a table at the front of the hall. The scholar-administrator in the middle, reading from the *Yingzao Fashi*. The senior certified dyer on the left, his face impassive. The junior inspector on the right, holding the measuring instruments.

I recognize the scholar-administrator. Supervisor Liu Han. He presided over my first examination, two years ago. He does not recognize me. Why would he? I am one of dozens of dyers he examines every year.

I stand at my bench. The other two artisans — both men, both older than me — stand at their benches. We wait.

Supervisor Liu looks up from the manual. His voice is formal, distant, the voice of an official performing a duty.

"You will produce one bolt of silk dyed to the standard specified in the *Yingzao Fashi*, section twelve, procedure four. Madder red, even color, no streaking. You have four hours. Begin."

The water clocks start dripping.

And my hands become wood.

---

I know what to do. I have done it a thousand times. I have done it every morning in the pre-dawn workshop for ten years. I have done it in the practice demonstrations with Master Feng watching.

But now, with the officials watching, with the water clock dripping, with the other two artisans working at their benches beside me, I do not know.

My hands do not know.

I prepare the mordant. The proportions are specified in the manual: two parts alum, one part iron, mixed with water. My hands measure, mix, pour. They move through the steps. But they do not move with certainty. They move with thought, with effort, with the awareness that I am performing.

I steep the madder root. The manual says optimal duration is contingent on root quality and fiber preparation. My hands should know when the root is ready. They should feel the interval, the moment when the root has given everything and will give no more.

But I do not feel it.

I wait. I test the dye on a scrap of fabric. The color is too light. I steep longer. I test again. Still too light. I steep longer.

The water clock drips.

I test again. The color is darker now. Maybe dark enough? I do not know. I cannot tell. My hands, which knew in the pre-dawn workshop, do not know here.

I lift the bolt of silk and submerge it in the dye. I work it through the bath, making sure every part of the fabric is covered. I lift it, wring it, submerge it again. The manual says this should be repeated three times.

I repeat it three times.

I lift the silk and hang it on the drying rack. The color is... acceptable. Even, no streaking. Maybe a little light, but within the acceptable range.

Maybe.

The water clock stops dripping. Four hours.

Supervisor Liu stands and walks to my bench. He examines the silk. His face shows nothing. The senior certified dyer joins him, touches the fabric, nods once. The junior inspector measures the color against the standard samples.

They do not speak to me. They move to the next bench, examine that artisan's work, then the third.

Then they return to their table and write in their ledgers.

"Dismissed," Supervisor Liu says. "Scores will be announced in one hour."

---

I wait in the courtyard with the other artisans. Master Feng is there, sitting on a stone bench near the gate. I sit beside him. He does not ask how it went. He does not need to ask.

"The silk was competent," I say.

"Good."

"But it was not..."

I do not finish. Master Feng knows what I mean.

One hour passes. The junior inspector comes out and reads from a list.

"Dyeing examination, demonstration component. Chen Shu, sixty-two percent."

I close my eyes.

Sixty-two percent. Competent. Acceptable. Not failing, but not passing either. The demonstration is only forty percent of the total score. I still have the verbal examination and the portfolio.

But I know already. I know from the shaking in my hands, from the interval that did not come, from the silk that was acceptable but not right.

I know.

---

The verbal examination is worse.

Supervisor Liu asks me to recite the contingency factors affecting madder dye quality. I open my mouth. The words tangle.

"The contingency factors are... root quality... fiber preparation..."

"Continue."

"If the root is old, then... uh... *ruo gen lao*..." I slip into the classical register, then back into vernacular, then neither. "The duration should be... I mean, the optimal duration is... uh..."

"Specify the optimal duration for aged root material."

"I... I do not remember, *daren*."

Supervisor Liu writes in his ledger. His face shows nothing.

The examination continues. More questions, more tangled words, more silences where there should be answers. My mouth does not know what my hands know. The manual's vocabulary is not my vocabulary.

When it is over, Supervisor Liu reads my score aloud.

"Verbal examination. Chen Shu, forty-five percent."

I bow and leave.

---

The portfolio component is the only one I pass. Seventy percent. The three pieces I submitted — silk dyed in my master's workshop, marked with my unregistered seal — are good. They are better than good. They are *shoujue*.

But the portfolio is only thirty percent of the total score.

Supervisor Liu calculates the final score and reads it aloud in the main hall, where all the examinees are gathered.

"Chen Shu. Demonstration sixty-two percent, verbal forty-five percent, portfolio seventy percent. Overall: fail."

I bow. My hands are steady now. They do not shake anymore. There is nothing to shake for.

I walk out of the Directorate compound into the afternoon sunlight. Master Feng is waiting by the gate.

"I am sorry, shifu," I say.

"I know," he says.

We walk back to the workshop together. Neither of us speaks. What is there to say?

I have one year left. One more examination. One more chance.

And if I fail again, the regulation says I must cease practice.

---

Three months after the examination, Feng stood in the doorway of Old Dyer Wu's workshop and watched Chen Shu work.

The workshop was smaller than his. One vat instead of three, no drying racks, barely enough room for two people to stand. But Chen Shu's hands, lifting a bolt of silk from the vat, moved with certainty.

She saw him and straightened. "Shifu," she said.

"I came to see how you are," Feng said.

"I am well, shifu."

She hung the silk on a rope strung across the room. The color was deep red, even, perfect. Feng recognized *shoujue* when he saw it.

"Wu's clients are good?" he asked.

"They are enough," Chen Shu said. "They know his work. They do not ask for certification."

Feng nodded. He looked around the small workshop. It smelled like mordants and damp wool, like his own workshop but more concentrated. Wu was in the back room, preparing indigo. He nodded at Feng through the doorway.

"The regulation," Feng said. "You have one year left."

"I know, shifu."

"Will you sit for the examination again?"

Chen Shu looked at her hands. They were steady. The interval had returned, Feng saw. Here, in this small workshop, with no officials watching, her hands knew again.

"I do not know, shifu," she said.

Feng understood. The examination would measure performance. It would not measure this — the silk she had just lifted, the certainty in her hands, the interval arriving before thought.

And the clients who knew Wu's work, who knew Chen Shu's work now — they did not need the examination to tell them what they already knew.

The system persisted. Supervisor Liu and his officials continued to certify artisans, continued to measure performance and call it mastery, continued to provide coordination for the palace buyers who needed a legible hierarchy.

But here, in this small workshop, in the pre-dawn hours when no one was watching, *shoujue* persisted too.

Feng did not know if this was victory or defeat. Perhaps it was neither. Perhaps it was simply survival.

"I will retire next year," he said. "The workshop..."

"I know, shifu." Chen Shu's voice was quiet. "You should sell it to a certified dyer. It is a good workshop. It deserves to continue."

"Yes," Feng said.

He did not say what he was thinking: that the workshop would continue without *shoujue*, that the new owner would produce competent work that matched the standards, that the clients would be satisfied and the palace would be satisfied and everything would be coordinated and legible and acceptable.

And that somewhere, in small workshops like this one, in the pre-dawn hours, the interval would continue to arrive in hands that knew, even if the examination never saw it.

He bowed to Chen Shu and left.

---

I wake in the fourth watch, as I always have.

Wu's workshop is dark, silent except for the dripping of water from the vat. I light a lamp and go downstairs. The madder root is steeping in the small vat. I prepared it yesterday evening, and now it is ready.

I know this before I know I know it.

My hands rest on the rim of the vat. They tell me. The interval between one breath and the next. The moment when the root has given everything and will give no more.

I lift the bolt of silk. Heavy with dye, heavy with water. The red is deep, clean, right.

I hang it on the rope to dry. My hands know how to wring it, how much pressure, which direction. I do not think about these things. My hands do them while I am thinking about other things.

I am thinking about the examination I will not sit for again. About the regulation that says I must cease practice in nine months. About the clients who know Wu's work, who know my work now, who do not ask for certification.

I am thinking about Master Feng's workshop, which he will sell to a certified dyer next year. About the younger apprentices who will learn from the new master, who will sit for the examination when they are old enough, who will pass or fail and either way will lose *shoujue* the moment the examiner says *begin*.

I am thinking about my hands, which know the interval here, in this small workshop, in the dark, alone.

The examination never saw this. The officials never saw this. Supervisor Liu, with his manual and his measuring instruments and his formal voice, never saw this.

But it is real.

The silk on the rope, drying in the pre-dawn darkness, is real. The red is deep and even and perfect. The merchants who buy it will know. They will not ask for my certification seal, because I do not have one. They will ask for my work, because my work is good.

And my hands, which became wood in the examination hall, are steady here.

I rinse them in the bucket of clean water. The water turns pink, then clear. My hands are never clean. The red has soaked into the skin, into the lines of my palms, into the beds of my fingernails.

I do not mind.

Outside, the sky is beginning to lighten. The night soil carriers are finishing their rounds. The vegetable sellers will arrive soon. The city is waking up.

I sit on the stool by the vat and rest my hands on my knees. They are not shaking.

The interval is here. It has always been here. The examination could not measure it, could not see it, could not certify it.

But it is mine.

I stand and prepare for the day's work. There is silk to dye, mordant to mix, clients to serve. The work continues. The interval continues.

And my hands know.
