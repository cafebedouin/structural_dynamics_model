# The Weight of the Cord

## I.

The splice-knot sat at the back of Ostrun Vey's neck like a second spine, and he had learned, over twenty-two years, exactly how it lay against his collar in cold weather and in warm, exactly how much it thickened the braid where the Table's braiders had worked two cords into one. He found it now without looking, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. Still there. Still fused. Still warm from his own skin, because it never left him, because there was nowhere for it to go.

He climbed to the third gallery with the petition folded twice in his coat, though he had made this climb enough times that the paper hardly mattered anymore; the Table's clerks had his claim memorized before he unfolded it, could probably recite it back to him in the same dry cadence they used to refuse it. Not refuse. He knew better than to think refuse. Refuse implied someone had looked at the thing and said no. What happened in the Reading-chamber was quieter than that, and worse, in a way he had never found the words for and had stopped trying to find words for around the third time.

This made four.

The chamber door stood ajar when he arrived — he noted this the way he noted everything about doors and thresholds, an old habit from lock-warden work where an inch of give in a door told you more than a foot of shouting — and an ajar door meant the Reading was between petitioners, meant he might not have to wait among the others on the cold bench with their own foldered griefs. He went in.

Notary Ceth Ambrail sat at the long table with the ledger open before them, hands resting flat and easy on either side of the binding, and looked up at Ostrun with an expression of complete, professional welcome that contained no memory of having seen him three times before. Or perhaps it contained the memory and simply didn't let it show. Ostrun had never been able to tell which, and had stopped trying to tell.

"By what tier do you address the Table," Ceth said, which was the opening every petitioner heard, filed or not, because the question itself was part of what made the answer legible.

"Fifth," Ostrun said. "Fused-fifth. Lock-Warden Ostrun Vey, cording reference —" and he gave the long string of numbers and letters that identified the marriage-fusion entry, numbers he had memorized the way a man memorizes a wound's exact dimensions, because the dimensions were the only part of the wound anyone else could be made to look at.

Ceth's hands moved without hurry to the correct ledger-tab, found it without looking, the way Ostrun found his own splice-knot without looking. "This is the matter of the separable identity."

"It is."

"You bring the same claim as before."

"I bring it again because it was never seated," Ostrun said, and heard in his own voice the flatness of a man reciting liturgy he no longer believed would be answered, only performed, because performing it was the only motion left available to him. "My wife is four years gone. The fusion was hers and mine together. I am asking the Table to recognize that a thing joined can be shown, in principle, to have once been separate."

"The Table does not dispute that it was once separate," Ceth said, and this was true, and Ceth said it kindly, in the particular kind way of someone who has said the same true thing many times and has not yet grown to resent its repetition. "The Table requires a witnessed anomaly-event to process a claim of this class. You have brought testimony. Testimony is not witness."

"I was there when we were spliced. I remember the splice."

"Your memory is not doubted. Your memory is not admissible in the shape this claim requires." Ceth's eyes went to the ledger's index of categories, the long columns of what could be entered and what could not, and Ostrun watched their gaze travel down the page the way a woman's eyes might travel down a lock-mechanism, checking each tooth of it for the one that would catch. "There is no category presently seated for 'testimony of a fusion's original separateness, uncorroborated.' I am sorry. I would enter it if I could find where it goes."

"Then where does it go."

It was not, by the time he asked it, really a question. Ceth did not answer it as one. Ceth closed the ledger's tab with the soft sound of a page returning to its resting fold, and said, in the voice reserved for the end of a Reading, "The Table thanks you for bringing this forward. It has not been seated at this time."

Not denied. Not seated. Ostrun had learned the difference the first time, had spent a year believing the difference meant there was still a door somewhere, a fifth try or a sixth that would finally find the room where someone was permitted to say yes. He no longer believed that, exactly. He also had not stopped bringing the petition. These two facts sat in him without resolving each other, the way the splice-knot sat at his neck without resolving into either a wound fully healed or a wound fully open.

He left the way he had come in, past the bench of other petitioners with their own foldered patience, out through the gallery's wet stone air, and he did not, on his way down, notice that the small unfiled girl who sometimes sat against the third gallery's outer wall — the one the residents called by a name that was not a name so much as an accounting, Nine-and-Nothing, because she'd been born on the ninth gallery and held no tier at all — was gone from her usual place against the shadow-line where the lamp-bracket didn't reach. He did not notice this because he had never, in any conscious sense, noticed her being there in the first place. He would say, if asked, that of course he'd seen the child around; everyone had. He would not be able to say when he had last actually looked at her.

That evening, alone in his sleeping-berth on the fifth gallery, he took off his coat and felt for the splice-knot again, pressing it between two fingers the way he had every evening for twenty-two years, checking that it still held, that the braid had not begun to fray at the join, as if the only variable left in his life worth managing was whether the rope his dead wife's rank was tied into still lay flat against his own.

It did. It always did. That was the whole of the problem.

## II.

Notary Ceth Ambrail's morning went the way Ceth's mornings generally went, which was to say: correctly.

There was a particular satisfaction — Ceth did not call it that, in the private grammar Ceth used even alone, which was the Table's grammar and no other, but it functioned as satisfaction — in watching a Reading-day open onto its full bench of petitioners and knowing, category by category, where each of them would land. A dispute over a contested water-right between two third-tier households: that had a page. A ninth-tier engineer's request to certify an apprentice for lock-work ahead of schedule: that had a page, several pages, an entire procedure with its own appendix. Even Ostrun Vey's petition, four times returned unseated, had a *page* — the page simply described a category that could not receive what he brought it. This distinction mattered to Ceth more than most petitioners ever understood. An unseatable claim was not a claim the Table had failed. It was a claim shaped wrong for any table at all, and the kindest true thing anyone could do for such a claim was say so plainly, rather than pretend a door existed where none had ever been cut.

Instructor-Engineer Prel Kastade arrived a little before the eighth hour, required by quorum rule to witness any Reading involving lock-adjacent certification, and settled into the observer's chair with the loose, unhurried ease of a man for whom quorum duty was a mild interruption to an otherwise full and well-fed day. He greeted Ceth warmly — Prel greeted everyone warmly; it was one of the genuinely likable things about him — and asked, while the bench outside finished filling, whether the pressure-lock recertification on gallery nine's western seal had been logged yet, because he wanted the apprentice cleared before the seep-season made the gallery too wet to train safely in.

"Not yet logged. I'll have it by midday."

"No rush on my account," Prel said, which was true, because nothing in Prel's day carried the kind of weight that made rush necessary, and this too was simply a fact about Prel, not a fault in him. He was good at the locks. He had been good at the locks for eleven years, had probably saved forty lives with that goodness, in the specific, countable way that mattered to the Cistern's survival more than almost anything else the Table adjudicated. Ceth respected this without reservation.

The second petitioner of the morning was brought forward not by herself but by a woman named Hara Dost, unfiled, gaunt in the particular way of someone who had spent a winter stretching chits meant for one across two, who stood at the bench's edge with a child's hand held loosely in hers — not gripping, Ceth noted, the way you'd hold a hand you were presenting as evidence, but the way you'd hold a hand you were simply keeping near you in a cold room.

"By what tier do you address the Table," Ceth said, to Hara, because Hara was filed, marginally, at the second tier, low enough to sit near the unfiled without quite being one of them, high enough to be permitted to speak in this room on someone else's behalf.

"Second," Hara said. "I bring forward a matter for the child. Her cording was never entered. Her mother's tier was under dispute at the birth-hour and the birth itself fell into no window the Table had open. She has gone nine years without a page."

Ceth looked, for the length of one breath, at the child — who did not meet Ceth's eyes, who had positioned herself half a step behind Hara's skirts in the particular geometry of someone who had learned, very young, exactly how far a room's attention could reach and how to stand outside that reach — and found the ledger-tab without needing to search for it, because Ceth had encountered the shape of this claim before, more than once, and knew precisely which page described it.

"This is the matter of the uncontested-but-unentered birth."

"It is not uncontested," Hara said. "That's the trouble of it. It was contested at the time, between two tier-claims, and the contest itself was never resolved, so the birth never landed in either column, and nine years on there's no one left with cause to press the contest to a resolution, because both households that were disputing it have since —" she stopped, chose a different word than the one she'd started toward — "have since stopped disputing it. There's no one contesting anymore. There's just no one who ever un-contested it either. She's fallen through the gap between two claims that both went quiet."

This was, Ceth recognized immediately, a genuinely difficult shape — not the ordinary unfiled case, where a birth simply hadn't been brought forward in time, which had its own tired but functional page. This was a claim caught between two expired contests, belonging to neither, and Ceth's mind ran, with real and unfeigned diligence, down the index of categories the ledger held for exactly this kind of orphaned procedural matter, searching — genuinely searching, not performing a search — for the page that would receive it.

There was no such page.

"The Table requires the original contest to be formally withdrawn by at least one party of record before a birth-cording can be re-opened," Ceth said. "If both parties have, as you say, ceased pursuing the matter without formally withdrawing —"

"They're dead," Hara said, flatly. "Both households. Flood-fever, the year before last. There's no one left to withdraw anything."

Ceth held very still.

This was the crack, and it lasted exactly as long as a held breath lasts — one beat longer than the beat Ceth would have held for Ostrun Vey's petition, or for the water-right dispute, or for any of the morning's ordinary unseatable claims. One beat, in which Ceth looked at the child again, at the bare unbraided throat above her collar, cold-reddened in the gallery's wet air, and thought — in the Table's own grammar, because Ceth had no other grammar left to think in even for a single private beat — *there is no procedural mechanism by which a dead party's silence can stand in for a living party's withdrawal, and I do not know whether that is an oversight or a design, and it does not matter which, because I cannot enter what has nowhere to go.*

Then the breath released, and procedure resumed, because procedure was the only shape Ceth's certainty had ever needed to take.

"I am sorry," Ceth said, and meant it, in the specific and limited way the Table's grammar permitted meaning things — sorry as a true feeling occupying exactly the space the ledger left open for feeling, no more. "The claim cannot presently be seated. There is no page for a contest resolved by the death of both parties rather than formal withdrawal. I would enter it if a page existed to enter it into."

"She's nine years old," Hara said, not pleading, simply stating the fact as though its bareness might do work the procedure couldn't. "She's some of the unfiled, and she's going to stay some of the unfiled, because two dead households can't sign a withdrawal."

"I understand the weight of what you're describing," Ceth said, and did — understood it fully, felt no impulse to minimize it, and still could not make the ledger's architecture bend around a shape it had never been built to hold. "The Table thanks you for bringing this forward. It has not been seated at this time."

Hara did not argue further. People rarely did, past a certain point; there was nothing to argue with, no adversary in the room, only an absence of a slot, and you cannot argue a slot into existing by force of feeling. She took the child's hand again — the same loose, keeping-near hold — and turned from the bench, and the child went with her, walking close along the chamber's wall rather than out across its open floor, out of some old instinct about where a room's attention fell and where it didn't, out through the door that someone else had left ajar.

Prel, from the observer's chair, said, mildly, to no one in particular, "Sad business, that. Shame there's no way round it." He said it the way a man says something true and regrettable about weather — a flood-season, a bad crop of lamp-oil — and then he asked Ceth, without any pause at all, whether the recertification paperwork for the apprentice might be ready before midday after all, since he'd like to get the boy onto the western seal by week's end if the seep allowed it.

Ceth told him it would be ready. Prel thanked Ceth, gathered his coat, and left in good humor, having witnessed the Reading fulfill its quorum requirement and having, in the specific and total way that cost him nothing to have, already begun thinking about locks.

Ceth sat a moment longer with the ledger open to the page that described what could not receive Hara Dost's claim, running one thumb along its edge, and thought, in the Table's grammar, the only thought the grammar permitted: *an unfiltered Table would drown in every grief that wanted hearing, and I have made myself the wall that keeps it from drowning, and the wall is a true and necessary thing, and I have never once had to stand on the other side of it to know that it is.*

Then the thumb stilled. The page turned. The next petitioner was called.

## III.

Ostrun went down three evenings later, though he could not afterward have said what specifically sent him — no new refusal, no fresh wound, only the accumulated weight of the old one settling a little further into its groove, the way a rope worn against the same stone edge for enough seasons finally stops fraying and simply flattens, taking the shape of the wear.

He passed gallery after gallery of lamp-bracket light, the mineral-wet stone going darker and colder the further down he descended, past the ninth gallery's last certified lock, past the point where any cord's tier meant anything at all to any door, until the spiral stair gave out onto the bare stone of the aquifer's original floor — granular, ungoverned, holding the cold of four hundred years in it like a held breath that never had to be released for anyone's benefit.

There was no gate. There had never been a gate. That was, he understood without being able to say it as an understanding, the entire nature of the place: nothing here asked him for a tier before it let him stand on it.

He had come here after the burial. He had come here in the year after, more than he had ever admitted to anyone at the Table, sitting on the cold floor with his coat drawn tight, not praying exactly, not grieving exactly either by the end of it — just present, in the one location the fused cord did not weigh any differently than an unfused one would have, because the bedrock did not weigh cords at all.

Someone was already there.

He saw her before he understood what he was seeing — a small shape settled against the base of the old stone where no lamp-bracket threw light, doing something patient and unhurried with a length of frayed cordage, working her fingers along its knots the way a person works at anything with no deadline attached to it. She did not startle at his approach, though she went still in the particular way of someone accustomed to gauging whether stillness or movement made her less visible, and decided, apparently, that stillness would do.

He knew who she was, in the loose way everyone on the lower galleries knew who she was — the girl they called by the accounting instead of a name, the one who slept where the wet ledge stayed dry enough, who ate what the unfiled managed to pass hand to hand in their own warm, unranked grammar that the Table's ledger had no ear for. He had seen her before, certainly. He would have said, if asked, that of course he had.

He looked at her now, properly, for what he understood — dimly, without being able to hold the understanding in a shape he could examine — was perhaps the first time.

Her throat was bare.

No braid. No dye, no knot, no weight at the collarbone. Just skin, cold-reddened same as any exposed skin in this air, catching no lamp-light because there was no lamp-light down here to catch, and something in Ostrun's chest went suddenly, wordlessly cold at the sight of it — not the cold of the gallery air but a colder cold, a wrongness his hand moved to answer before his mind had found any thought to send it. His fingers went, without his deciding they should, to the back of his own neck, to the splice-knot, the double-thickness of a cord that held too much rather than a throat that held nothing at all, and he did not think *she has none and I have twice*, because that thought required an arithmetic he had no procedure in him for performing. He only felt, all at once, the specific vertigo of two absences that were not the same absence, sitting three feet apart on bedrock old enough to have never once been asked to care about either of them.

She did not ask why he was staring. She went back to her knots, in no hurry, because there was nothing here that required her to hurry, no gate closing, no Reading-hour ending, no bench filling behind her with others waiting their turn.

He sat, some distance off, because it seemed right to leave that distance, and did not speak, because he found — reaching for something to say, some greeting fit for the moment — that the Cistern-formal address had no shape for this room and never had, and the only other register he owned, the stripped and warm and rank-less one, belonged to a dead woman's mouth and not his own, and he was not yet — might never be — able to borrow it for anyone else.

So he simply sat. The cold came up through the stone the same for both of them, asked nothing of either cord, gave nothing back that either of them could carry up the stair and use. Above them, nine galleries up, a Reading-chamber's ledger sat closed for the night on two pages that described, in the Table's exact and careful language, everything that could not be entered — his fused splice, her orphaned birth — each page perfectly correct, perfectly kind, perfectly unable to find the other, though anyone standing far enough back from both of them at once might have seen, in the shape of what they lacked, that the two pages were, in every way that mattered to whoever might one day be permitted to say so, describing the same kind of wound.

No one was standing that far back. Not tonight. The lamp-brackets did not reach this deep, and the two of them sat on in the bare dark a while longer, unfiled and fused, neither closer to being seen by the Table than they had been an hour before, held only by the bedrock, which had never once, in four hundred years, asked either of them what they were owed.
