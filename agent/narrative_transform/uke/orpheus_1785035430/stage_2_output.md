# SECTION 0: INVARIANT CONTRACT

**The invariant, as commitment + falsifier:**
The world contains a real — the force of a single person's devotion to another — that no ledger, no border-office, no sorting instrument can read, own, or reduce to a value. It is not measured because it is not the kind of thing that survives being measured; the instant it is entered into a register, what's recorded is not it. **Falsifier:** if the finished story contains a recoverable "true value" of the devotion — a number the office got wrong, a rank it should have granted — the invariant was lost.

**The missing-floor invariant + falsifier:**
Beneath the visible boundary between the country of the living and the country of the dead there is no natural seam — only an old settlement, cut once by force and kept since by watchmen, and dressed in the grammar of geography so long that even honest people call it weather. **Falsifier:** if the story frames the boundary as a fact of nature that a kinder god or a better law could not have drawn differently, the floor invariant was lost.

**The substrate that holds it, in the world's own terms:**
The *keeping-salt* — a black, granular deposit found only along the Silt Line, the marsh-border between the riverside parishes of the living and the reed-flats where the dead are ferried. Salt-panners have worked the Line for six generations. It is common knowledge, never questioned, that keeping-salt cures grief the way brine cures meat — you rub it into the collar of a garment left behind by the dead, and the ache dulls, manageable, workable-around. What is not written anywhere, because there is no instrument for it, is that keeping-salt only cures manageable grief. Against the specific unbearable devotion of one person for one other, irreplaceable, unfungible, it does nothing at all — it passes through the hand like sand. The customs officers who weigh cargo at the Line's checkpoints can certify salt by grain, grade, and sack-weight. No scale in the parish has ever registered a difference between a sack that will comfort a widow and a sack that a man carried forty miles because he could not stop carrying it. The weighmasters have never noticed the gap in their own instrument. That is the invariant, and it is not a secret — it is simply not the kind of fact a checkpoint can hold.

**The substrate inhabitation sentence:**
You press your palm into a barrel of keeping-salt gone damp from the marsh air, and you already know — before the weighmaster's brass scale confirms the sack's legal weight to the ounce — that nothing in this parish has ever been built to weigh what you are actually carrying.

# SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

**Setting.**
The Silt Line, 1690s, a tidal marsh-border between two river parishes on a coastline never named after any real one. To the east: Verholt, a working parish of eel-fishers, salt-panners, and small tenant farms, governed nominally by a distant crown but really by its own vestry and by the river's seasons. To the west, across two miles of reed-flat and brackish channel: Ashmere, the reed-country, where by long-unquestioned custom the dead are ferried at death and are said to go on living a duller, colder version of life among the reeds until the reeds themselves finally take them. No one in Verholt has ever seen an Ashmere soul return. It is not a religious claim so much as a bureaucratic one: there is a Crossing House at the Line, staffed by Watchmen of the Passage, who process every body that goes west and who — this is the detail everyone in Verholt half-knows and no one says aloud — occasionally process petitions to bring one back. Occasionally is a generation, maybe two. It has happened. It is spoken of the way a village speaks of a man once struck by lightning and lived: proof the thing is *possible*, offered as comfort, never as expectation.

The Watchmen are drawn from Verholt families themselves — this is the detail that keeps the Crossing House from feeling like an occupying force. A Watchman's cousin panning salt on the Line is entirely ordinary. The Watchmen do not think of themselves as gatekeepers of a border; they think of themselves as the parish's most trusted clerks, custodians of an old and necessary arrangement that keeps the dead from wandering back hungry and keeps the living from wearing themselves out on hope. The arrangement works. Bodies go west, grief is panned into keeping-salt, life resumes. No one in six generations has needed to ask what the Crossing House actually *is*, structurally — a jurisdiction, drawn once by a founding Passage-Warden whose name is now a street, enforced since by rota and rifle and the simple fact that no one has ever tried to walk the reed-flats without a Watchman's leave and lived to report on it.

**Constraint reference table.**

*C1 — The Passage Boundary (the line between Verholt and the reed-country of the dead)*
Naturalized as: the Crossing itself — the tidal channel and reed-flat that separates the living parish from Ashmere, patrolled and administered by the Watchmen of the Passage.
- **Agent_A** (powerless, biographical, trapped, universal) experiences it as *the wall the world is built against*. A young eel-fisher whose wife was ferried west three winters ago meets it as absolute: there is no boat, no bribe, no season in which a living man simply walks into the reeds and walks back out with her. It is spoken of in Verholt exactly as weather is spoken of — "the reeds don't give back what they take," said with the same flatness as "the tide doesn't wait."
- **Agent_B** (institutional, civilizational, arbitrage, universal) — the Passage-Warden who commands the Crossing House — experiences the identical boundary as *routine jurisdiction he administers*. Petitions cross his desk. Most are refused as a matter of form. A very few, for reasons the Warden alone weighs, are granted. To him the Line is not a wall; it is a filing system with an unusually solemn caseload.
- Local term: **"the Reed-Law."** Never called a wall, a law, or a border in conversation — always "the way of the reeds," which does the same work as calling gravity "the way of things."

*C2 — The Salt-Song*
Naturalized as: a mourning practice unique to Verholt — a keening, half-sung recitation performed at the graveside and, crucially, repeatable afterward, alone, over a fistful of keeping-salt, that visibly steadies a griever and is also the *only* channel by which an ordinary parish soul can make the Passage-Warden notice them at all. The song is taught mother to daughter, father to son; everyone in Verholt can perform it adequately. It is understood, vaguely, that a Salt-Song sung loudly and long enough near the Crossing House sometimes moves a Watchman to actually *read* a petition rather than stamp it.
- **Agent_A** (moderate, biographical, constrained, universal) uses the Salt-Song as a genuine tool — sings it to calm his own grief, and, calculating, sings it *at* the Crossing House gate because he has learned it is the only lever that produces a hearing rather than a form-letter refusal. To him it is an instrument he wields, imperfect but real.
- **Agent_C** (powerless, civilizational, trapped, regional) — an elder aunt of the dead wife, who has sung the Salt-Song at every anniversary for longer than Agent_A has been alive — experiences the same practice as something that both genuinely holds the parish's grief in shared ritual *and* quietly reopens a wound that never fully scars, because the song requires reciting the dead's whole name and the exact hour of loss, every single time, forever. It comforts and it extracts, in the same breath, and she cannot get the comfort without paying the reopening.
- Local term: **"the Keening"** or "singing the salt." Never called ritual, mechanism, or leverage.

*C3 — The Warden's Condition*
Naturalized as: the specific bargain the Passage-Warden extends to Agent_A after the Keening moves him — not a full pardon of the Reed-Law, but a conditional trial: Agent_A may cross to the reed-flats and walk his wife back to the Crossing House gate himself, but he must not look back to confirm she is following until they have both passed fully beyond the reeds, onto Verholt clay. One glance behind, at any point before the boundary, and the arrangement voids — not as punishment exactly, the Warden would say, but because the Reed-Law simply resets itself when its terms aren't met. No appeal. No second trial. The Warden sets these terms without softening them, without building in any margin, without preserving any fallback channel of coordination if the condition fails.
- **Agent_A** (powerless, immediate, trapped, local) experiences the condition as an iron trap disguised as mercy — a test that both grief and love make almost impossible to pass, since the entire unbearable ache of six years is a doubt that whispers *check, check, she is not really there* every step of the walk.
- **Agent_B**, from the Warden's chair, experiences granting this condition as costless, routine administrative generosity — a case resolved, a precedent maintained, nothing risked on his side of the ledger.
- Local term: **"the Backward Glance"** or "the Warden's one rule." Villagers who know the old stories call it simply "the rule that's never once been kept."

**Character positions.**

- **Agent_A → Cal Renwick**, a Verholt eel-fisher, late twenties, widower. Owns a flat-bottomed skiff, a one-room cottage above the tideline, and a wife's wool shawl he has kept folded in a chest and never rubbed with keeping-salt, because he has never wanted the ache dulled — a fact the parish finds strange and slightly shameful, the way it regards any grief that refuses to be managed.
- **Agent_B → Warden Ossory**, Passage-Warden of the Crossing House, sixties, third generation in the post. Lives in the only stone building at the Line, keeps the Passage rolls in his own hand, genuinely believes he runs the fairest system his family's office has ever run. Grants Cal's petition partly out of something he will not examine too closely — pity, precedent, boredom with refusal — and does not adjust the Backward Glance condition's severity in light of what it will actually cost a grieving man to hold his nerve for two miles of reed-flat.
- **Agent_C → Ness Renwick**, Cal's aunt by marriage, sixties, keeper of the family's Keening tradition, the one who taught Cal the Salt-Song as a boy and now watches him deploy it as a lever rather than a comfort, unsure whether to be proud or afraid of what that means.

**Linguistic strategy: Direct Translation, with a code-switched register at the Crossing House.**

Verholt speech is built around agricultural/tidal metaphor for anything to do with loss and time — grief is "salted," not "processed"; a petition is "put to the tide," not "filed." Concepts with no clean English equivalent: *lang-hush* (the specific silence a house takes on in the year after a death, distinct from ordinary quiet — a noun, not an adjective), *reed-owed* (adjective describing a person the parish considers to have "unfinished debt" with the dead — carries no shame, just fact), *keep-salt-shy* (a person who deliberately refuses to dull their grief, as Cal is).

At the Crossing House itself, characters shift into a clipped administrative register borrowed from crown bureaucratic English — "petition," "rota," "the Warden will hear the case at second bell" — signaling the Reed-Law's institutional face. The switch between marsh-vernacular and Crossing-formal is how the story marks who is speaking as neighbor versus who is speaking as office.

*Example:*
> "She's reed-owed three winters now," Ness said, not looking up from the salt-pan. "You going to keep-salt-shy the whole of your life, or you going to sing the thing properly?"
>
> At the gate, Cal straightened his collar before he spoke. "I've come to put a petition to the Warden. Renwick, Cal, of the eastern flats. Third bell, if he'll hear it."

**Inhabitation sentences.**

- **Cal:** "You are a man who has refused every comfort the parish offers for grief because the comfort would mean admitting she is not coming back, and now the one door that might actually return her opens only on the condition that you never once turn to check that she is really behind you."
- **Ossory:** "You are the third Warden in your family to sit this desk, and you have never once had to walk the reeds yourself to know your rules are fair, because fairness, from where you sit, has never cost you anything."
- **Ness:** "You are the woman who taught the boy the song that saved him and is now also the trap that will not let him rest, and you cannot tell him to stop singing it because it is the only thing that has ever made the Warden look up."
- **Substrate (Cal, at the barrel):** "You press your palm into a barrel of keeping-salt gone damp from the marsh air, and you already know — before the weighmaster's brass scale confirms the sack's legal weight to the ounce — that nothing in this parish has ever been built to weigh what you are actually carrying."

# SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

**RESOLVED**

- **Ω_E01:** Does keeping-salt need an internally consistent extraction/production process (who pans it, how it's sold) to avoid reading as arbitrary magic? → Resolved: keeping-salt is a real marsh mineral, panned like any other trade good, sold in Verholt markets for ordinary mundane comfort-uses (rubbed into mourning clothes, sold to travelers, taxed by the vestry). Its *failure* against unfungible grief is never advertised or tested by anyone — it simply isn't the kind of failure that shows up in a market. → Impact: keeps the invariant's substrate mundane and untheorized in-world, per Step 0's non-quantitative naming requirement.
- **Ω_E02:** Should the Crossing House's petition-granting history include enough precedent that Ossory's leniency doesn't read as arbitrary favoritism? → Resolved: yes — establish early that petitions are granted "a generation, maybe two," giving Ossory institutional cover and making his choice feel like ordinary casework rather than special pleading for Cal. → Impact: preserves PIR reading — Ossory is not bending rules for Cal specifically, he's exercising ordinary discretionary latitude that was always part of his office.
- **Ω_C03:** Should the Backward Glance's mechanism (why looking back voids it) be explained in physical/magical terms? → Resolved: no — it is presented exactly as Reed-Law is presented throughout, as bare unexplained condition, "the way of the reeds." Explaining it would convert Mountain-adjacent boundary logic into a legible mechanism, inviting a "correct the error" reading. → Impact: preserves direct-realism rejection; the rule is a fact of the Warden's office, not a puzzle with a right answer.

**UNRESOLVED**

Verholt's tradition being secure and generations-deep makes the extraction-within-comfort more painful (it's not a dying practice being exploited, it's a healthy one that still costs). Awaiting confirmation this reads correctly at Stage 4 draft review.

Flagging for Stage 4 discretion since it affects pacing of a tightly-scoped story.
