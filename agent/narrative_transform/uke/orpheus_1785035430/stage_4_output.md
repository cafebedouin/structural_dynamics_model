# The Backward Glance

## I.

*I taught him the words before he had the grief to put in them. That's the way of it in Verholt — you learn the Keening the way you learn to swim, before there's any water that could take you, so that when the water comes you already know what your mouth is meant to do.*

*He sang it clean the first time, at nine years old, over his grandfather's collar. Clean and empty, a boy's voice going through motions. I remember thinking: good, let it stay empty as long as it can.*

*It didn't stay empty.*

---

The wool of her shawl has gone stiff in the chest three winters now and I have never once rubbed salt into it. Ness asks me about this the way she asks about everything, sideways, over the pan.

"You going to keep-salt-shy the whole of your life," she says, not looking up, working the black grain between her palms, "or you going to sing the thing properly and let it dull?"

"It doesn't need dulling."

"Everything needs dulling, Cal. That's what dulling's for."

I don't answer that. I fold the shawl back into the chest the way I always do, the way I've done every month since the ferrying, and I go back down to the skiff because the eels don't care what a man is or isn't carrying.

The reeds don't give back what they take. I have heard this said in Verholt the way I've heard men say the tide doesn't wait for a slow hand, the way my father said frost doesn't ask permission. Weather-talk. Nobody in this parish says wall. Nobody says law. You'd think, to hear us, that Mara had walked into a season rather than a jurisdiction — that she'd been taken up by something as blameless as rain.

For three years I believed that too. I went to the Crossing House gate exactly once, the first spring, unbidden, no petition prepared, no bell rung, and I stood at the reed-line looking at the stone house with its one lit window and I understood, the way you understand a locked door before you've tried the handle, that there was nothing behind that window that would open for a man with nothing to offer but wanting. I turned around. I didn't go back for two years.

Then Ness sang the anniversary Keening at the graveside, the way she does every year, the way she's done since before I was born into this grief, and something in me that I'd kept folded shut like the chest came open, and I heard my own voice under hers, and I understood — coldly, the way you understand where the eels have gone to ground in low water — that the song wasn't only for comfort. That it was a key as much as a wound.

I have used it as a key since. I am not ashamed of this, though I notice Ness watching my hands when I sing now, the way she used to watch a boy's hands to see if he'd learned the shape of the words right, and I think she is not sure anymore whether she taught me a comfort or a lever.

Both, maybe. I don't have room to be delicate about which.

---

At the gate I straighten my collar the way you straighten it for anyone with authority, though I have told myself for three years this isn't authority, this is only weather with a desk.

"I've come to put a petition to the Warden," I say. "Renwick, Cal, of the eastern flats. I'll sing the Keening if he'll hear it."

The Watchman at the gate is Alder Voss, whose mother pans salt two stretches down from Ness's stretch, and he looks at me the way a neighbor looks at a neighbor doing something a little shameful in public.

"Third bell," he says. "If you can hold the whole song without breaking, he'll hear the case."

I hold the whole song. Her whole name, the exact hour, the way the water took the ferry-boat's line taut when it left the eastern dock. My voice doesn't break, though something behind my ribs does, quietly, in a way no one at the gate can see.

The Warden hears the case.

## II.

Warden Ossory kept the Passage rolls in his own hand, as his father had, and his father's father before that, and he took a particular satisfaction — not pride, precisely, pride implied too much heat — in the evenness of his own ledger-script. A man's whole petition, reduced to a clean line: *name, parish, cause, disposition.* It comforted him the way a well-swept hearth comforts a man who has never had to sleep cold.

He had refused nine petitions that season already. This was not cruelty. It was arithmetic of a kind that didn't need to call itself arithmetic — most griefs, however sung, however sincere, did not meet the threshold his family's office had maintained for three generations, a threshold that existed precisely so the reed-country would not be worn thin with traffic, so the dead could keep their dulled, colder living undisturbed by hope arriving on every tide.

He remembered granting one, seventeen years back — a fisherman's widow, and before her his own father had granted two in a career of forty years, and his grandfather one in fifty. A generation, maybe two. This was not a rule written anywhere. It was simply the rhythm the office had always kept, and Ossory trusted rhythm the way other men trusted scripture.

Cal Renwick's Keening, when it came, was — Ossory noted, setting down his pen — unusually complete. The boy had not faltered on the hour. Most men faltered on the hour; grief blurred time before it blurred anything else. This one hadn't.

He granted the case a hearing on that basis alone, which was, he felt, entirely fair. The rules of his office did not require him to feel anything at all about a petitioner. They required him only to determine whether the case met the form. This one met the form.

He did not consider, setting the terms of the trial that afternoon — *the petitioner may walk the reed-flats and bring the deceased to the Crossing House gate; he must not look back to confirm her presence until both have passed fully onto Verholt clay; the condition is absolute; there is no second trial* — whether the condition was severe. Severity was not a category his office measured. Fairness was: had every petitioner in living memory been offered the same unadorned terms? They had. The rule that's never once been kept had never once been softened either, and Ossory understood this symmetry to be the proof of its justice, not a flaw in it.

He signed the case *Renwick, C. — trial granted, terms standard* and set down his pen and felt, for a moment, the particular warmth of a well-made decision, the kind that asks nothing further of the man who made it.

## III.

*He came to me the night before with the fistful of salt still ungranted in his coat, and asked if I'd sing over him one more time before he went. Not the anniversary Keening — something smaller, just for the walking.*

*I sang it. My mouth knew the shape before my mind caught up, the way it always does now — I could sing that girl's name asleep, I think, I could sing it dead myself and my mouth would keep going.*

*I pressed a fistful of the black grain into his palm after. In case, I said, and didn't finish the sentence, because I didn't know what the rest of it was. In case what. In case the reeds are kinder to salted men. I don't believe that. I've panned that grain forty years and it's never once done anything but cure the manageable.*

*But my hands wanted to give him something and that was the only something my hands know how to make.*

## IV.

The reeds close over the causeway path almost at once, taller than I expected, taller than they look from the Verholt bank, and the light through them goes the color of weak tea. I do not look back at the Crossing House gate, though I want to, though every animal part of me wants one clean look at Ossory's stone house before it's out of reach, some proof I haven't walked off the edge of the known world into nothing. I don't look. That isn't the rule yet — the rule doesn't start until she's behind me — but I am already practicing not looking, the way you practice not touching a wound before someone tells you not to touch it.

The ground gives underfoot in a way Verholt clay never gives — a wet, sucking give, each step drawing a small sound out of the mud like something reluctant to let go of my foot. I go on. The reed-hiss is constant, a dry papery weather-sound that never resolves into wind exactly, just a continuous shifting like the marsh breathing through its teeth.

I find her at the third bend, where Ossory said I would, standing exactly where a living woman would stand if she'd been waiting for me a long while without impatience. I don't look at her long. I'm not permitted long — the moment I turn to walk her back is the moment the rule begins, and something in Ossory's flat voice when he explained the terms told me the beginning matters, that whatever grace period exists between seeing her and turning away is thin and I shouldn't spend it.

"Come on, then," I say, the way I'd say it to her living, easy, unceremonious, because ceremony would break something in my throat I need to keep whole for two miles.

I hear her step onto the causeway behind me. That's the last time I let myself register it as sight. After that it's only sound.

## V.

Six years married teaches a man things he doesn't know he's learned until they're all he has left. I could pick Mara's tread out of a room of ten women blindfolded — the particular unhurried set-down of her heel, never quite matching another woman's rhythm, a walk that favored the left slightly from an old fall on the eel-dock steps when she was a girl.

I hear it behind me now. The reed-hiss makes everything harder to be sure of, and the mud-suck of my own steps threatens constantly to swallow the smaller sound of hers, but underneath both, if I hold very still inside my own walking, there it is: heel, then the soft drag, then the small catch that isn't quite a limp. I know that sound. I have known that sound eleven years.

The path takes a long bend around a stand of taller reed and the ground firms slightly, the mud giving way to something with more clay in it, more purchase, and I feel the change through my boots before I think anything about it. Verholt's ground, starting to reach this far out to meet me. That should comfort me. It doesn't, particularly. Comfort isn't available to me right now the way water isn't available to a man mid-drowning who happens to be surrounded by it.

The doubt starts small, the way doubts do, a needle-prick rather than a wound. *That catch — was that her catch, or a trick the reeds do with sound, or my own want making a shape out of nothing?*

I don't turn. I fix my eyes forward on the thinning reed-line and I let the doubt sit in my chest like a swallowed stone.

## VI.

Ossory did not often stay at his desk past the last bell, but that night he found himself there anyway, the Passage rolls open to the page bearing Cal Renwick's case, a cold cup of small beer forgotten at his elbow. The room held its usual warmth — a low fire, the particular good smell of dry paper and beeswax that Ossory associated, without ever naming it as such, with a life properly and safely conducted.

He read back over his own script. *Trial granted, terms standard.* He found himself, oddly, moved by it — by the completeness of the boy's Keening that afternoon, by the steadiness in his voice when he'd said her name and the hour, no falter, none at all, in a way that suggested either great discipline or a grief too large to trip over its own details.

His hand, resting on the page, went still a moment longer than it needed to. Not doubt. He would not have called it doubt, and it was not doubt — only a beat of attention, the kind a fair man permits himself before closing a ledger for the night, a private acknowledgment that this case had moved him slightly more than the nine he'd refused that season.

He did not, in that beat, reconsider the terms. It did not occur to him to reconsider them, any more than it occurred to him to reconsider the tide. The terms were the terms his office had always set — no margin, no fallback, the Reed-Law reset on its own if its one condition failed, and this was not a policy he had invented to be harsh; it was simply how the arrangement had always worked, inherited whole, unquestioned, the way a man inherits the shape of a doorway and doesn't ask why it's that height.

He closed the rolls. He banked the fire himself, an old habit from a house he'd grown up in before it became an office, and went to bed with the particular untroubled sleep of a man who has done, by his own lights, a kind and proper day's work.

## VII.

*I wasn't there for the walking, of course. No one from the parish is ever there. I only had it after, the way we always have everything after — secondhand, from the Watchmen who saw the start and end of it and nothing of the middle, which belongs, I suppose, only to the ones inside it.*

*But I know what I taught him. I know what my mouth put in him when he was nine years old and didn't need it yet. Her whole name. The exact hour. Say it complete or the saying does nothing — that's the rule of the Keening, the rule I never once thought to soften for him either, because a Keening sung halfway has never steadied anybody, not once in six generations, not for lack of trying.*

*My wrists ache tonight the way they always ache, from the pan, from forty years of turning grain that will cure some griefs and pass clean through others like water through a net. I didn't tell him that. I'm not sure I could have, in words that would have stopped him going.*

## VIII.

The reed-line has thinned enough now that I can feel, without looking, the different quality of light against my closed eyelids when I blink — brighter, the marsh opening toward the last stretch before the firmer ground. My chest has gone tight in a way that has nothing to do with the walking itself; the walking is easy enough, my legs know this ground's cousin ground from a life of working the flats. It's the listening that's using me up.

Her step behind me falters.

Not stops. Falters — a half-beat's hesitation in the rhythm I've had by heart these last long minutes, heel-drag-catch, heel-drag-catch, and then a gap where the catch doesn't come, or comes late, or comes at all and I only imagined the gap because my own heart stumbled first and dragged the sound of her after it.

I don't know. That's the whole of it — I don't know, and there is no way inside the rule Ossory gave me to know, and the not-knowing sits in my chest exactly the size and shape of the doubt that has lived there three years, the one that whispers *she is not really there, she was never really coming, check, check.*

My hand goes to my coat pocket without my telling it to. The keeping-salt is there, the fistful Ness pressed on me, and my fingers close around it, the grain shifting damp and granular against my palm the way it does in the barrels at the weigh-house, and for one held breath I let myself believe an old woman's *in case* might hold some weight here that it's never held anywhere else.

It does nothing. Of course it does nothing. It sits in my fist inert as sand, exactly the way it sits inert against every grief too large and too particular to be the kind grief the parish knows how to cure, and I understand this without words for it, the way you understand cold water is cold before you've thought the word *cold* at all.

The step behind me hasn't resumed. Or it has, and I've lost the thread of it in my own held breath. Or it never faltered and I broke the rhythm myself by listening too hard, the way staring too long at a still object makes it seem to shudder.

The ground under my boots is nearly all clay now. Close. I can feel how close.

I turn.

## IX.

There is no drama in it, from where I stand. She is there — she was there, the whole way, heel and drag and catch, exactly as I knew her — and for the smallest sliver of time before anything happens I have her face again, the actual weather of it, not memory's flattened version, and it is worth — I think this, clearly, in the instant it costs me everything — it is worth more than the six years I have left to grieve it.

Then the reeds take the shape of her the way water takes the shape of a hand pulled out of it. No violence. No cry. Just an absence arriving where a presence had been, smooth as a held breath finally let go, and I am alone on the causeway with the last hundred yards of clay in front of me and nothing behind.

I don't fall down. I want to record that, though there's no one to record it to. I stay standing. My hand is still closed around the salt in my pocket, and I open my fingers, and it runs out between them into the mud, indistinguishable from the marsh's own black ground the moment it lands.

## X.

Ossory logged the case the following morning, unhurried, his hand as even as it had ever been. *Renwick, C. — trial voided, terms not met, second petition inadmissible per standing rule.* He did not think of this entry as unusually sad. It was, statistically, the ordinary outcome; most trials voided; that was, if anything, the proof the terms were sound — a rule so easily kept would hardly be worth calling a rule.

He felt, briefly, the same beat of attention he'd felt two nights before, the pen resting a moment longer than strictly necessary over the closing line. Then he wrote the line, and blotted it, and reached for the next case on the rota, which concerned a dispute over eel-weir rights three farms up the eastern flats and required no particular feeling at all.

*I heard it from Alder Voss before Cal was even back across the clay — that the reeds took her again clean, that he came through the gate alone and didn't sing anything, didn't say her name, just walked past the Watchmen with his coat pockets turned out and empty, salt-grain still caught in the seams.*

*I didn't ask him what happened on the causeway. There's a kind of not-asking in this parish that isn't silence, exactly — we have a word for the hush a house takes on after a death, and there should be another word, I think, for the hush that settles after a chance like that one closes, but there isn't, not yet, not in six generations of us standing at this Line finding words for everything else.*

*He still keeps her shawl in the chest. Still hasn't rubbed the salt in. I don't ask him to anymore.*

*Some Aprils I sing the Keening at her grave and my mouth still knows every word before I've decided to say them, her whole name, the hour, and it still steadies me exactly as much as it always has, and it still opens the same old seam doing it, and both those things are true in the same breath, the way they always were, the way I taught him they would be before he had any grief of his own to test it on.*
