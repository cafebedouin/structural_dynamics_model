# The Board and the Stair

## I.

The draft came up through the seam where the shelf met the wall, and Ines had learned, over nine winters, exactly how far she could shift her hip before the cold found the gap in her blanket again. She budgeted it the way she budgeted everything: an inch of warmth here, a held breath there, enough left over to get through the fourth bell without her hands shaking when it mattered that they didn't.

Mireille slept two shelf-widths down, close enough that Ines could hear when her breathing changed in the night — the hitch that meant a dream about the corridor, the long slow exhale that meant she'd found her way out of it without waking. Neither of them had ever discussed this. There was nothing to discuss. They had the same draft, the same shelf, the same quarter coming due, and between them an understanding so old it had never needed to become words: *you don't ask me if I'm ready, because neither of us is ever going to be ready, and asking would waste breath we might need later.*

Posting was two days off. The dormitory had the particular tightness it got in the last week before a quarter closed — girls practicing scales under their breath at the wash-basin, a boy near the far wall running his tongue over a chip in his tooth like he could file it smooth by will alone. Ines did not practice. She had found, across enough quarters, that practicing in the last week only told her body it was afraid, and her body already knew.

She noticed, instead, the small architecture of the room. Which proctor walked the gallery above the shelves at which hour. That Sannat had taken the morning rounds again this week instead of the evening ones, which meant either he'd asked for the change or someone had made it for him, and either way it meant something about how he was sleeping, though she couldn't have said what and didn't try. She noticed these things without deciding to, the way she noticed which way the wind came through the seam. It was simply what the room was made of, along with stone and cold and the sound of forty people trying to breathe quietly enough to be forgotten by whoever was listening.

Dov's old place on the shelf had been given to a new boy two winters back, small and dark-haired and always cold, who slept curled the way Dov used to curl, though Ines doubted anyone had told him to. She still, some nights, put her hand flat against the wood where Dov's shoulder used to be, not reaching for anything, just checking that the wood was still wood.

The silent hour came after the evening meal and before lights, an hour with no bell attached to it, no purpose the Cantorate had ever bothered to name, in which Vessels were permitted — not encouraged, not forbidden, simply permitted, the way weather is permitted — to sit with their own thoughts before sleep took the choice away from them. It was in this hour, on the second-to-last night before posting, that Dov came down from the scriptorium stair with a look on his face Ines didn't have a category for yet.

He didn't speak. He sat on the end of Mireille's shelf, because there was no room left on Ines's, and Mireille moved without comment to give him the space, the way she'd have moved for weather.

Ines reached across the gap between the shelves — not far, an old, worn distance, the exact width her arm had learned at nine years old and never forgotten — and found the inside of his wrist with two fingers. Pressed the rhythm there that had no name because it had never needed one, the same rhythm from a hundred nights before this one, back when the wrist under her fingers had been three feet away every night instead of a floor and a stair and a locked door away.

He didn't press back at first. When he finally did, his fingers were unsteady in a way she didn't recognize, and it worried her more than his silence had.

Sannat, walking the gallery above with his hands folded into his sleeves against the evening chill, looked down once at the shelf-line, the way proctors did, cataloguing nothing in particular — heads down, no talking, no infractions — and moved on. There was no column on any slate he carried for what he had just failed to see. It did not occur to him that he had failed to see anything at all.

---

Posting day arrived the way weather arrives — not sudden, exactly, but total once it was fully there. The corridor outside the dormitory hall filled before the bell, a long crooked queue of Vessels not looking at each other, all of them looking instead at the board at the far end, chalked that morning by a proctor's clerk in handwriting too neat to belong to anyone who had ever been cold in this building.

Ines had learned to read the board with her whole body before her eyes ever reached her own name. How close to the front of the queue the proctor let her stand told her something. How long the queue took to move told her something. The exact temperature of the corridor — always colder here than the dormitory, some trick of the stonework nobody had ever explained — told her how long she would have to hold herself steady before she was close enough to see anything at all.

Mireille stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, both of them at the same distance from the board, both of them having walked the same number of years toward this exact patch of cold stone. Neither said anything. There was nothing to say that the shape of their standing there didn't already say better.

When Ines's turn came she did not let her hand shake reaching up to trace the mark beside her name. She had trained that stillness the way she'd trained breath control, an act of engineering as deliberate as anything the proctors tested for, except that no proctor would ever know to test for it, because it did not occur to any of them that a Vessel's hand not shaking was itself an achievement requiring years.

She had held. The number chalked beside her name put her exactly where she had stood the quarter before — not higher, not lower, in the wide grey country of the ranked mass that was neither elevation nor release, the country she had lived in her entire remembered life. Mireille, beside her, had held too, in the same country, at the same cold distance from the board.

Neither of them wept, or laughed, or said anything the proctor standing three feet off could have written down as an event. They simply turned from the board together and walked back toward the dormitory, and somewhere behind them, high up near the gallery stair, Ines was aware — the way she was aware of weather — that Dov had been watching from above, and had not come down.

## II.

The scriptorium was the warmest room Dov had ever been permitted to work in, which was not the same as warm. Heat rose from the kitchens two floors below and arrived at his desk as a suggestion rather than a fact, enough to keep the ink from freezing in its well, no more. He had stopped minding the cold for its own sake within the first winter. What he minded now was different: the particular way his eyes had learned to find the grain of a page, the slight give of good vellum against bad, the exact slant with which a tired proctor's hand slipped sideways at the end of a long entry. He had spent twelve years learning to hold breath and pitch. He was surprised, still, some mornings, at how fast the body found new things to be good at once the old ones stopped being asked of it.

He copied interview transcripts. That was the whole of his work, most days — the Adjunct's spoken assessments of Resonant Integrity, rendered from Sannat's own scrawled notes into the fair, permanent hand the Cantorate's archive required, so that in a hundred years some registrar could pull the record of a child none of them had ever met and know, officially, whether that child's voice had once been judged to carry the sacred weight the Concert required.

Nobody had ever told Dov what Resonant Integrity was, precisely. He had assumed, for years, standing in the testing corridor waiting for his own turn at it, that this was a failure of his understanding — that somewhere above him, someone held the definition the way you'd hold a coin, whole and countable. It took him the better part of a year at the scriptorium desk to notice that no such definition existed anywhere in the archive he was copying from. Sannat's notes did not define the term. They applied it. *Resonant Integrity: uncertain, recommend retest.* *Resonant Integrity: present, elevation candidate.* *Resonant Integrity: absent.* Absent from what, present in what measure, scored against what fixed point — none of it was there. It had never been there. It was a word that did the work of a number without ever having to submit to being wrong the way a number could be shown to be wrong.

He found this knowledge did something to his chest the first few times, a kind of vertigo, like a floor he'd been standing on turning out to be a plank over water he hadn't known was there. After some months it became merely a fact he carried, the way he carried the grain of the vellum, the slant of the hand.

He told himself this was power, of a kind. Not the power to change anything — he understood, distantly, that understanding a lock did not open it — but a different order of thing. He had spent his whole childhood inside a machine that told him what he was worth without explaining its workings. Now he could see the workings. Surely that had to count for something, even if he could not yet say what.

It was in this state of mind, three days before posting, copying a stack of interview notes from the last quarter's retest round, that he found Ines's name.

Not a full interview — a marginal note, the kind Sannat sometimes left when a score sat close to a line and needed a second look before it went to the board. *I. — endurance sound, pitch retention sound, Resonant Integrity uncertain — recommend standard hold.* Beneath it, in different ink, added later, a single further line in Sannat's smaller, more tired hand: *no cause found to alter. hold stands.*

Dov sat with the page a long time.

He knew — had known for two years now, the way you know the shape of a room you've lived in — that "uncertain" beside Resonant Integrity was not a measurement of anything real. It was the word Sannat reached for when he had run out of other words and still had to write something. He had seen it beside a dozen names. He had seen, twice now, an "uncertain" become an "absent" a season later with no new evidence attached, only a differently-worded note and a name that stopped appearing on any board at all.

He thought: *I could change this.*

Not the interview. He had no authority to alter Sannat's judgment, and would not have known how to forge the Adjunct's specific tired slant convincingly even if he'd wanted to try. But the fair copy — the permanent record, the one that would sit in the archive for the hundred years, the one nobody but another scriptorium clerk would ever check against the original — that copy was made by his hand, and his hand alone.

He took up the pen. Where Sannat's marginal note read *uncertain*, Dov's fair copy, when he finally set it down in the clean permanent hand the archive required, read *present.*

He sat afterward with his palms flat on the cold desk, breathing carefully, the way he used to breathe in the testing corridor before his voice was required to do something difficult. He had done a real thing. An actual mark existed now on an actual page that had not existed an hour before, and it said something kinder about Ines than the truth had said.

He did not let himself think, yet, about what the fair copy was *for* — that it existed to be consulted later, in the case of an appeal, a review, a dispute over an elevation years down the line; that it did nothing at all to the number already chalked, already posted, already read by Ines's own hand tracing her own name in the cold corridor two days from now. He let himself, for the length of one evening, believe he had done something that reached her.

---

He went down the stair on the evening before posting meaning to tell her — not the whole of it, he had no words yet for the whole of it, but enough. *I found your name. I did something.* He got as far as the gallery above the dormitory hall and stood there in the half-dark, looking down at the shelf-line, at the small dark heads bent over private nothing, at Ines's shoulder turned toward the wall the way it always was, and found that he could not make himself go the rest of the way down.

It was not fear of being seen. Proctors passed through that gallery every hour; nobody would have questioned a scriptorium clerk with legitimate business in the dormitory wing. It was something else, a thing he did not have a name for and would not have used the word *shame* for even if he had. He had a warm bed. He ate meat, now, more often than the others did, on the days the scriptorium staff shared the clerks' table. He had climbed a stair two years ago that none of them would ever climb, and what he had brought back down it, tonight, was a fair copy in an archive drawer that would not warm anyone, would not feed anyone, would not move a single chalk mark on a board that had already, by tomorrow morning, been posted.

He stood at the gallery rail a long time. Below him, once, he saw Ines's hand cross the gap between two shelves, saw Mireille shift without being asked to make room, and something in his chest that he had been calling clarity for two years turned over and showed him, briefly, its underside.

He went back up the stair without going down.

## III.

Posting had come and gone the way weather comes and goes, leaving behind it the particular exhaustion that had no name in Cantor-speech and needed none in shelf-speech either, because everyone on the shelf-line knew it by feel: the specific ache of having held a body rigid and still in a cold corridor for the length of time it takes forty names to be read off one at a time, multiplied by the number of times you had done this before, back to when you were seven and didn't yet know what you were bracing for.

Ines lay on her shelf that evening with her spare shift folded under her head for warmth, listening to the dormitory settle. Down the row, Mireille's breathing had already gone slow and even — she'd learned, over the years, to fall asleep fast on posting nights, before her body had time to remember the whole day at once. Ines envied this without resenting it. It was simply a different way of budgeting the same scarce thing.

She had held. That was the whole of the news, and the whole of the news was also nothing, because holding changed nothing about tomorrow, or the quarter after, or the year in which she would turn seventeen and then eighteen and would either be lifted at last into the warm rooms with the meat twice a week or be sent down-mountain with no name that meant anything outside these walls. Holding only meant she got to keep standing exactly where she had always stood, in the exact cold she had always known, for three more months.

She thought, without deciding to think it, of Sannat's face at the interview stage of testing — not this quarter's interview, she hadn't drawn one this time, but the memory of past ones, his eyes tired in a way that had nothing to do with unkindness and everything to do with something she had no word for and had stopped trying to find one for years ago. She had seen him once, in a corridor, stop mid-stride at a name on a list and stand there a moment too long before moving on. She did not know what the name had been. She had filed the moment away the way she filed everything about this building — not as sympathy, exactly, more as information, a fact about the weather of this place that told her nothing about whether the cold would ever let up.

The stair creaked, once, faint, from the direction of the scriptorium wing. She did not sit up. She had learned the sound of that particular stair years ago, back when it had meant something different than it meant now.

Dov came down into the dormitory hall in the last hour before the final bell, when the proctor on gallery duty had gone to the far end to warm his hands at the stove there, and crossed the floor between the shelves without a lamp, the way he used to when this had been simply where he lived rather than a place he visited. He did not say anything about the copy, in the end — had rehearsed a dozen ways to tell her, on the stair coming down, and found every one of them collapsing under its own weight the moment he was actually standing beside her shelf in the dark, close enough to see that she had held, and close enough to understand, finally and completely, that his hand on a page in an archive drawer had not been in the room with her when she traced her name in the cold.

He sat on the edge of the shelf where the new boy slept, careful not to wake him, and reached across the old worn distance his arm still remembered.

Ines found his wrist before he found hers.

She pressed the rhythm there — the one that had outlasted nine years of shared cold, outlasted his transfer, outlasted the two winters of him sleeping somewhere she could not follow — and felt him go still under her fingers the way he used to, the old stillness, not the unsteady thing from before posting. He pressed back. Not an answer to anything she had asked in words, because she had asked nothing in words; simply the fact of his fingers finding the same place on her wrist that hers had found on his, in the same rhythm, unteachable, uncertified, carrying no information any proctor's slate had a column for.

On the gallery above, having returned from the stove sooner than either of them had accounted for, Sannat paused at the rail and looked down at the shelf-line — a boy from the scriptorium sitting where he had no posted business sitting, a girl's hand at his wrist in the dark. He watched a moment. There was nothing in the picture that resembled an infraction; nothing in it resembled anything the Cantorate's law had ever needed a word for, because the law had never once been asked to look for it. He noted, distantly, the way he noted most things now, that the boy had been a difficult case once, testing corridor, breath-collapse, and that someone — himself, as it happened, though he did not dwell on this — had found a place for him instead of a release notice. He thought, without any particular weight to the thought, that it was good the two of them still had this, whatever it was he was seeing. He did not write it down. There was nothing to write.

He walked on toward the far stair, and the dormitory settled back into its ordinary dark, and on the shelf below, in a language with no letters, Ines and Dov went on saying to each other the only true thing either of them had ever been allowed to own.
