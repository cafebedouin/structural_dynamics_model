# The Board and the Stair

## I.

The draft came up through the seam where the shelf met the wall, and Ines had learned, over years of winters, exactly how far she could shift her hip before the cold found the gap in her blanket again. She budgeted it the way she budgeted everything: warmth here, a held breath there, enough left over that her hands wouldn't shake when it mattered that they didn't.

Mireille slept a shelf-width down, close enough that Ines could hear when her breathing changed in the night — the hitch that meant a dream about the corridor, the long slow exhale that meant she'd found her way out of it without waking. Neither of them had ever discussed this. There was nothing to discuss. They had the same draft, the same shelf, the same quarter coming due.

Posting was close now. The dormitory had the particular tightness it got in the last week before a quarter closed — girls practicing scales under their breath at the wash-basin, a boy near the far wall running his tongue over a chip in his tooth like he could file it smooth by will alone. Ines did not practice. She had found, across enough quarters, that practicing in the last week only told her body it was afraid, and her body already knew.

She noticed, instead, the small architecture of the room. Which proctor walked the gallery above the shelves at which hour. That Sannat had taken the morning rounds again instead of the evening ones, which meant something about how he was sleeping, though she couldn't have said what and didn't try. She noticed the kitchens starting up before light — the clatter that meant barley, the thinner clatter that meant only water moved from one vessel to another. She had learned to tell, three floors up and through stone, which morning it would be before the tray ever reached the hall, and had learned to be neither glad nor sorry either way, because gladness and sorrow both cost something she needed for later.

Dov's old place on the shelf had been given to a new boy, small and dark-haired and always cold, who slept curled the way Dov used to curl, though Ines doubted anyone had told him to. She still, some nights, put her hand flat against the wood where Dov's shoulder used to be, not reaching for anything, just checking the wood was still wood. The new boy had started, this past week, waking at the same hour Dov used to wake, a small animal alarm going off in him for no reason he could have named. Ines had begun, without meaning to, listening for it the way she listened for Mireille's breath.

The silent hour came after the evening meal and before lights — no bell attached to it, no purpose the Cantorate had ever bothered to name, in which Vessels were permitted, not encouraged, simply permitted, to sit with their own thoughts before sleep took the choice away. It was in this hour, close to posting, that Dov came down from the scriptorium stair with a look on his face Ines didn't have a category for yet.

He didn't speak. He sat on the end of Mireille's shelf, and Mireille moved without comment to give him the space, the way she'd have moved for weather.

Ines reached across the gap between the shelves and found the inside of his wrist with two fingers. Pressed the rhythm there. It had no name — hadn't needed one, back when the wrist under her fingers had been close every night instead of a floor and a stair and a locked door away.

He didn't press back at first. When he finally did, his fingers were unsteady in a way she didn't recognize, and it worried her more than his silence had.

Sannat, walking the gallery above with his hands folded into his sleeves, looked down once at the shelf-line — heads down, no talking, no infractions — and moved on. There was no column on any slate he carried for what he had just failed to see. It did not occur to him that he had failed to see anything at all.

---

Posting day arrived the way weather arrives — not sudden, but total once it was fully there. The corridor filled before the bell, a long crooked queue of Vessels not looking at each other, all of them looking instead at the board, chalked that morning in handwriting too neat to belong to anyone who had ever been cold in this building.

Ines had learned to read the board with her whole body before her eyes ever reached her own name. How close to the front the proctor let her stand told her something. How long the queue took to move told her something. The corridor's cold — always worse here than the dormitory — told her how long she'd have to hold herself steady before she was close enough to see anything at all.

Mireille stood shoulder to shoulder with her, the same distance from the board, both of them having walked the same years toward this exact patch of stone. Neither said anything. There was nothing the shape of their standing there didn't already say better.

When Ines's turn came she did not let her hand shake reaching up to trace the mark beside her name. She had trained that stillness the way she'd trained breath control — an act of engineering as deliberate as anything the proctors tested for, except no proctor would ever know to test for it, because it did not occur to any of them that a hand not shaking was itself an achievement requiring years.

She had held. The mark put her exactly where she'd stood the quarter before — not higher, not lower, in the wide grey country of the ranked mass that was neither elevation nor release, the country she had lived in her entire remembered life. Mireille had held too, in the same country, at the same cold distance from the board.

Neither of them wept, or laughed, or said anything the proctor standing close by could have written down as an event. Ines wondered, walking back, whether the stillness in her hand was a thing she owned or only a thing the corridor had made her into, and found, as always, that the question closed over itself without an answer. She let it close. There would be another quarter to ask it in.

Somewhere behind them, high up near the gallery stair, Ines was aware — the way she was aware of weather — that Dov had been watching from above, and had not come down.

## II.

The scriptorium was the warmest room Dov had ever been permitted to work in, which was not the same as warm. Heat rose from the kitchens below and arrived at his desk as a suggestion rather than a fact, enough to keep the ink from freezing, no more. He had stopped minding the cold for its own sake within the first winter. What he minded now was different: the way his eyes had learned to find the grain of a page, the slight give of good vellum against bad, the exact slant with which a tired proctor's hand slipped sideways at the end of a long entry.

He copied interview transcripts — the Adjunct's spoken assessments of Resonant Integrity, rendered from Sannat's scrawled notes into the fair, permanent hand the archive required, so that some future registrar could pull the record of a child none of them had ever met and know, officially, whether that child's voice had once been judged to carry the weight the Concert required.

Nobody had ever told Dov what Resonant Integrity was, precisely. He had assumed, waiting in the testing corridor for his own turn at it, that this was a failure of his understanding — that someone above him held the definition whole, like a coin. It took him a long stretch at the desk to notice that no such definition existed anywhere in the archive. Sannat's notes did not define the term. They applied it. *Resonant Integrity: uncertain, recommend retest. Resonant Integrity: present, elevation candidate. Resonant Integrity: absent.* He had watched an "uncertain" beside a name become an "absent" a season later with no new evidence attached — only a different note, and a name that stopped appearing on any board at all.

It only sat in him wrong, the way a note sits wrong in the ear before the singer knows which note it should have been.

He told himself this was power, of a kind. Not the power to change anything — understanding a lock did not open it — but a different order of thing. Some days he thought he could see further into the room than anyone had ever let him see before. Other days the seeing amounted to nothing he could hold in his hand, and he did not examine the difference too closely.

It was in this state of mind, days before posting, that he found Ines's name.

Not a full interview — a marginal note, the kind Sannat left when a score sat close to a line. *I. — endurance sound, pitch retention sound, Resonant Integrity uncertain — recommend standard hold.* Beneath it, in different ink, added later: *no cause found to alter. hold stands.*

Dov sat with the page a long time.

He thought: *I could change this.*

Not the interview — he had no authority to alter Sannat's judgment. But the fair copy, the one nobody but another clerk would ever check against the original, was made by his hand alone.

He took up the pen. Where Sannat's note read *uncertain*, Dov's fair copy, in the clean permanent hand the archive required, read *present.*

He sat afterward with his palms flat on the cold desk, breathing carefully. An actual mark existed now that had not existed before, and it said something kinder about Ines than the note he'd copied it from had said.

He let himself believe, for a while, that he had done something that reached her. The belief had a warmth to it that he did not examine.

He filed the completed page into the drawer where such pages went — a drawer he had opened many times and would open many more, opened, as far as he had ever seen, by no one else at all. He closed it. The sound it made was the small sound it always made, wood on wood. He stood with his hand on the edge a moment before he let it go, not because he understood anything in that moment but because his hand, for a moment, did not want to.

---

He went down the stair the evening before posting meaning to tell her — not the whole of it, he had no words yet for the whole of it, but enough. *I found your name. I did something.* He got as far as the gallery above the dormitory hall and stood there in the half-dark, looking down at the shelf-line, at the small dark heads bent over private nothing, at Ines's shoulder turned toward the wall the way it always was, and found he could not make himself go the rest of the way down.

It was not fear of being seen. Proctors passed through that gallery every hour; nobody would have questioned a scriptorium clerk with legitimate business in the dormitory wing. It was something he did not have a name for either. He had a warm bed. He ate meat, now, more often than the others did. He had climbed a stair none of them would ever climb, and what he had brought back down it, tonight, was a fair copy in a drawer that would not warm anyone, would not feed anyone, would not move a single chalk mark on a board already, by tomorrow morning, posted.

He stood at the rail a long time. Below him, he saw Ines's hand cross the gap between the shelves, saw Mireille shift without being asked to make room, and something in his chest that he had been calling clarity turned over and showed him, briefly, an underside he did not have a word for.

He went back up the stair without going down.

## III.

Posting had come and gone the way weather comes and goes, leaving behind it the particular exhaustion that had no name in Cantor-speech and needed none in shelf-speech either — the specific ache of having held a body rigid in a cold corridor for as long as it takes a queue of names to be read off, back to when you were small and didn't yet know what you were bracing for.

Ines lay on her shelf that evening with her spare shift folded under her head for warmth, listening to the dormitory settle. Down the row, Mireille's breathing had already gone slow and even — she'd learned to fall asleep fast on posting nights, before her body had time to remember the whole day at once. Ines envied this without resenting it. A different way of holding the same weight.

She had held. That was the whole of the news, and the whole of the news was also nothing, because holding changed nothing about tomorrow, or the quarter after, or the year in which she would turn old enough to be lifted at last into the warm rooms with the meat, or sent down-mountain with no name that meant anything outside these walls.

She thought of Sannat's face at past interviews, tired in a way that had nothing to do with unkindness. She had seen him once, in a corridor, stop mid-stride at a name on a list, his hand hovering at the slate he carried, pen not quite touching it, the way a hand hovers over a wound it doesn't know how to dress. He stood like that longer than she expected. Then he closed the slate against his chest — not sharply, not gently, just closed it — and walked on. Ines, watching from a few shelves off, understood only that something had gathered in him and not broken, the way weather sometimes gathers and does not break, and that she would never know which name it had been, or whether what she'd seen in him was a kind of seeing or only a kind of tiredness that wore seeing's face. She turned the question the way she'd turn a stone in a pocket, not expecting it to become anything else, and put it away.

The stair creaked, once, faint, from the direction of the scriptorium wing. She did not sit up. She had learned the sound of that particular stair years ago, back when it had meant something different than it meant now.

Dov came down into the dormitory hall in the hour before the final bell, when the proctor on gallery duty had gone to warm his hands at the stove, and crossed the floor between the shelves without a lamp, the way he used to when this had simply been where he lived. He had rehearsed a dozen ways to tell her, coming down the stair, and found every one of them collapsing the moment he was actually standing beside her shelf in the dark, close enough to see that she had held, and close enough to feel, without being able to finish the thought, that his hand on a page in a drawer had not been in the room with her when she traced her name in the cold.

He sat on the edge of the shelf where the new boy slept, careful not to wake him, and reached across the old worn distance his arm still remembered.

Ines found his wrist before he found hers.

She pressed the rhythm there — outlasting the cold, outlasting his transfer, outlasting the winters of him sleeping somewhere she could not follow — and felt him go still under her fingers, the old stillness, not the unsteady thing from before posting. He pressed back. His fingers found the same place on her wrist that hers had found on his, in the same rhythm, carrying no information any slate had a column for, changing nothing about what the board would say in another season, or the one after that.

On the gallery above, having returned from the stove sooner than either of them had accounted for, Sannat paused at the rail and looked down at the shelf-line — a boy from the scriptorium sitting where he had no posted business sitting, a girl's hand at his wrist in the dark. He watched a moment. His hand found the slate at his side out of old habit, fingers closing on its edge. There was nothing in the picture below that resembled an infraction; nothing in it resembled anything the law had ever needed a word for. He stood at the rail longer than the moment required, the slate closed under his fingers, and did not write anything down, and did not walk on right away either, and whatever crossed his face in that stretch of not-moving, Ines, below, in the dark, with Dov's pulse under her fingers, did not see, and would never know to wonder about. He walked on toward the far stair. The dormitory settled back into its ordinary dark, and on the shelf below, Ines and Dov went on holding to each other in the only language neither of them had ever been taught and neither would ever be asked to prove.
