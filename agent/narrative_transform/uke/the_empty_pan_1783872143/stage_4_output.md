# The Platform Knows

## I.

Mirae stands barefoot on the cold platform, toes curled against the metal. The morning air through the Registry's open windows carries the smell of someone's breakfast — buckwheat porridge, butter tea. Her stomach tightens. She should have eaten.

The Officiant — Davin, she knows his name though she's never used it — watches the screen mounted on the wall. Not her. Never her. Just the numbers the platform generates from the pressure of her feet, the distribution of her weight, the small corrections her body makes without thought.

Her left hip aches where her daughter's weight should be. Yuna is with her mother this morning, but Mirae's body hasn't forgotten the three-year-old's mass, the way she settles on the left side, head against Mirae's ribs. The platform reads this. It reads everything — the slight forward tilt from decades of bent fieldwork her mother did before her, the way her right foot takes more weight to compensate, the micro-adjustments her ankles make that she doesn't control, has never controlled.

She breathes. Counts. One, two, three. A calming technique the last Officiant taught her. It doesn't calm.

The screen blinks. Davin's face doesn't change, but something around his eyes tightens. "Six point one," he says.

The same. The same as last year, and the year before that. Borderline. Not quite failing, but not safe either. Not the kind of score that lets you cross alone.

"Borderline," Davin confirms, and his voice is careful, neutral, the voice of someone who has said these words many times. "You're still favoring your left side. The asymmetry is—" He pauses. "I'm prescribing the same exercises. Ten minutes of wall-standing in the morning, weight distribution drills before bed. The core strengthening sequence I showed you last year."

Mirae nods. She has done them. Every morning before dawn, standing with her back pressed to the cold stone wall of her house, her daughter watching with sleepy confusion. Every night after Yuna is asleep, shifting her weight from foot to foot, trying to feel what the platform feels, trying to correct what her body insists is already correct.

"I do them," she says in Margravian, the language of the Registry, of official things. Her accent is thick, the tones imprecise. "Every day."

"Then you need to do them more carefully." Davin's voice is not unkind. "Your body isn't responding. That means you're not doing them correctly, or not consistently enough."

She wants to say: My body knows how to stand. I have never fallen. I carry fifty-pound sacks across the practice bridge behind my house every week, and I have never stumbled, never felt the lurch of actual falling, never lost my grounding.

But she doesn't have the words. Not in Margravian, and not in Creole either. The words for what her body knows don't exist in the language of scores.

"I'll try harder," she says instead.

Davin nods. He prints the remediation plan on thin paper that feels expensive between her fingers. "Next year," he says. "Same time."

She steps off the platform. Her feet find the floor — solid, unmoving, real — and the relief is immediate and sharp. She puts on her shoes, old canvas work boots with the toes wearing through, and walks out into the morning.

The bridge is a fifteen-minute walk from the Registry. She could see it from here if the buildings weren't in the way — the suspension cables, the narrow walkway swaying slightly in the wind that comes up from the Sorting Gulf. Two hundred meters deep, the gulf. She has looked down into it exactly once, when she was twelve and her mother first brought her to the Registry for assessment. She hasn't looked since.

The turnstile at the bridge entrance reads her chip when she holds her wrist to the scanner. The screen blinks red. **ACCOMPANIED ACCESS ONLY.**

She waits. Her neighbor, Tenzin, is supposed to meet her here. He works at the grain cooperative across the bridge, crosses every morning, has a score of seven point three. High enough that the favor doesn't cost him anything except time.

He arrives five minutes late, breathless, apologetic. "Sorry, sorry. My daughter was sick, I had to—"

"It's fine," Mirae says, switching to Creole without thinking. *Mi gnod.* It's fine. "Thank you."

He swipes his wrist. The turnstile unlocks with a soft click. They walk through together, and the bridge takes their weight.

It sways. It always sways. The wind comes up from the gulf and catches the cables and the whole structure moves, a slow pendulum rhythm that Mirae's body knows how to read, how to match. She has crossed this bridge a thousand times. She has never fallen.

But Tenzin is here, and the turnstile knows it, and that's what makes it safe.

---

Her mother is in the kitchen when Mirae gets home, stirring a pot of rice porridge. Yuna sits on the floor playing with wooden blocks, building towers and knocking them down.

"How did it go?" her mother asks. In Creole, always Creole at home. The question carries the weight of the question beneath it: *Did you pass?*

"Same as last year," Mirae says. She hangs her coat on the hook by the door, slips off her boots. "Six point one."

Her mother's hands still on the spoon. She doesn't look up. "The exercises?"

"He prescribed them again."

"You've been doing them."

"I know."

Yuna looks up from her blocks. "Mama, why do you stand against the wall?"

Mirae crouches down, pulls her daughter into her lap. The weight settles on her left hip, exactly where it belongs. "To make my standing better," she says. "So the platform will read me right."

"But you don't fall down."

"No, I don't."

"Then why does the platform say you do?"

Mirae's mother turns from the stove. Her face is lined, her back curved from thirty years of fieldwork before her hips gave out and she had to stop. Her score is five point four. Has been five point four for six years. She can't cross the bridge at all anymore without accompaniment, and most days she doesn't bother. She stays on this side, in the house, in the kitchen, in the small radius of her own failing body.

"The platform knows better than we do," Mirae's mother says. She says it in Margravian, the language of official truth. Then switches back to Creole: "Even when it doesn't feel like it knows."

Yuna squirms out of Mirae's lap, goes back to her blocks. Mirae stands, her hip aching, her body settling into the stance it has learned over three years of carrying this child: left hip out, right foot planted, spine curved just slightly to compensate.

The stance the platform reads as wrong.

---

At dawn, before Yuna wakes, Mirae stands against the wall. Her shoulder blades press into the cold stone. Her heels are six inches out, her knees slightly bent, her core engaged the way the diagram on the remediation plan shows. She holds it for ten minutes, counting breaths because she doesn't have a clock.

Her body knows this is not how she stands. This is not her grounding. But maybe if she does it long enough, the platform will stop reading her as deviant. Maybe her body will learn.

She counts. One hundred breaths, two hundred. Her thighs start to burn. Her lower back aches. This is not the ache of fieldwork, of useful labor. This is the ache of forcing her body into a shape it doesn't want to hold.

At three hundred breaths, she stops. Yuna is awake, calling for her. Mirae pushes off the wall and her body floods with relief, settling back into its own knowledge of where it belongs in space.

She makes breakfast. Rice porridge with dried apricots, butter tea with salt. Yuna eats slowly, asking questions about everything — why is the sky blue, why do birds fly, why does the bridge sway. Mirae answers what she can. The sky is blue because that's the color light makes when it comes through air. Birds fly because they have wings. The bridge sways because the wind pushes it.

"Why can't we cross the bridge alone?"

Mirae's hands still on the teacup. "Because the platform says I don't stand right."

"But you don't fall down."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because the platform says so."

Yuna accepts this. She is three. The world is full of rules she doesn't understand yet, rules that adults follow because adults say so. The platform is just one more.

Mirae finishes her tea. She needs to get Yuna to her mother's house, then walk to the field. The quinoa needs weeding, and if she doesn't get there by mid-morning, the rows will be taken by someone else.

She gathers Yuna, walks the narrow path between houses. Her mother is awake, sitting in the sun outside her door, mending a shirt. She takes Yuna without comment, and Mirae walks to the field.

The work is the same as always. Bent over the rows, hands in the soil, pulling weeds that grow faster than the quinoa. Her back aches by midday. Her spine curves, her weight shifts to her left side because her right knee is weaker, has always been weaker since she fell off a wall when she was nine.

Around her, other workers move through the rows. Mostly women, mostly older. She knows some of their names, knows most of their scores. Borderline, failing, chronic fail. The ones who cross the bridge do it accompanied, asking favors from neighbors, from family, from anyone with a passing score who will spare the time.

At lunch, they sit in the shade of the tool shed. Someone has brought dried yak meat to share. Mirae eats slowly, listening to the conversation drift around her.

"My assessment is next month," one woman says. Her name is Dolma, and she is fifty-three, and her score has been six point zero for four years. "I've been doing the exercises."

"They don't work," another woman says. Pema, sixty, five point two. "I did them for three years. My back won't straighten."

"You have to do them right," Dolma insists. "You have to be consistent."

"I was consistent."

"Then you weren't doing them right."

Pema is silent. Mirae watches her face, the way it closes, the way she turns back to her food. The conversation moves on.

Later, when they're back in the rows, Mirae works next to Dolma. She wants to ask: Do you really believe the exercises work? Do you really think it's about doing them right?

But she doesn't. Because Dolma needs to believe. Because if the exercises don't work, if standing straighter isn't the answer, then there's nothing she can do. And that's worse than believing she's just not trying hard enough.

So Mirae pulls weeds, and her back aches, and the sun moves across the sky, and she says nothing.

---

## II.

Kiran arrives at the Margraviate on a cold morning in late spring, when the plateau is still brown but the snow on the mountains is starting to melt. The bus from the valley takes four hours, winding up roads that switch back and forth across the face of the cliffs, and by the time it reaches the plateau Kiran's ears are ringing from the altitude.

The research fellowship is for one year. Kinesiological study of high-altitude populations. Gait patterns, load-carrying biomechanics, adaptation to thin air. Kiran has done similar work in Nepal, in Tibet, in the Andes. This should be straightforward.

The university provides housing — a small apartment near the main campus, two rooms and a kitchenette, heated by a radiator that clanks and hisses. Kiran unpacks methodically. Laptop, recording equipment, pressure sensors, books. By evening, the apartment feels less like a temporary space and more like a place where work can happen.

The next morning, Kiran walks to the Central Registry for onboarding. The building is stone, old, with narrow windows that let in slanted light. Inside, it smells like paper and floor wax and something else, something metallic that Kiran can't identify.

The Officiant at the desk is young, maybe thirty, with a face that suggests he smiles often but isn't smiling now. "Name?" he asks in Margravian.

"Kiran Shrestha."

"Purpose of visit?"

"Research fellowship. Kinesiological Institute."

The Officiant types something into his terminal. "You'll need an assessment. Standard procedure for all residents and visitors."

Kiran nods. This was mentioned in the fellowship paperwork, though not explained in detail. "What kind of assessment?"

"Equilibrium. Step onto the platform, please."

The platform is in the center of the room, a square of metal two meters on each side, slightly raised from the floor. Kiran steps onto it barefoot — the Officiant gestured at the shoes, so shoes come off first. The metal is cold. Kiran stands, weight distributed evenly, or as evenly as possible given the slight scoliosis that pulls the spine into a curve, the left leg that's half an inch shorter than the right.

The Officiant watches a screen. His expression doesn't change, but something in his posture relaxes slightly. "Seven point two," he says. "Well above threshold. You're cleared for unrestricted bridge access." He swipes a card through a machine, hands it to Kiran. "This is your chip band. Wear it at all times. The turnstiles will read it when you cross."

Kiran looks at the band — a simple strip of woven fabric with a small chip embedded in the center — and then at the Officiant. "What does the assessment measure?"

"Standing stability."

"Against what reference?"

The Officiant pauses. "Against correct posture."

"Whose?"

He doesn't answer. After a moment, he says, "If you have questions about the methodology, you can submit a request to the Equilibrium Registry's administrative office. They handle all research inquiries."

Kiran nods slowly. "Thank you."

Outside, Kiran stands in the plaza and looks at the bridge. It's visible from here — a narrow suspension bridge stretching across the gulf, cables gleaming in the morning light. People cross it in a steady stream, some alone, some in pairs. At the near end, there's a turnstile, and everyone who passes through it holds their wrist to a scanner.

Kiran walks closer. Watches. Most people cross alone, their wrist-swipe quick and automatic. But some — not many, but some — wait at the turnstile until another person arrives. They swipe together. The turnstile unlocks. They cross side by side.

Kiran makes a note in the small notebook that lives in every pocket: *Accompanied access. Why?*

---

The Kinesiological Institute is a low building on the edge of campus, with labs full of equipment that looks expensive and slightly outdated. Kiran's supervisor, Dr. Yangchen, is a woman in her sixties with close-cropped gray hair and hands that move constantly while she talks.

"Your focus is gait analysis," she says, spreading a map across her desk. "We're particularly interested in load-carrying patterns. The fieldworkers here carry significant weight over uneven terrain, and we want to understand the biomechanical adaptations."

Kiran nods. "What about the bridge crossings? I noticed some people need accompaniment."

Dr. Yangchen's hands still for a moment. "That's related to the Posture Index. Not your research area."

"But it's a kinesiological question, isn't it? If the Index measures standing stability—"

"It measures standing posture. Not the same thing." Dr. Yangchen's voice is careful, neutral. "The Index is a public health measure. It's administered by the Equilibrium Registry, not by us. If you want to study it, you'll need separate approval."

"From whom?"

"The Registry. And the Margrave's office of Public Health."

Kiran makes another note. "Is that difficult to get?"

"It's not usually granted. The Index is considered... sensitive. Politically."

"Why?"

Dr. Yangchen looks at Kiran for a long moment. "Because it works," she says finally. "Bridge accidents are down sixty percent since it was implemented. The Margrave is very proud of it. So are most residents. Questioning it is..." She pauses. "Not encouraged."

Kiran closes the notebook. "I understand."

But later, alone in the apartment, Kiran opens the laptop and searches for information about the Posture Index. The official documentation is sparse — a public health bulletin from forty years ago, some accident statistics, a brief description of the assessment procedure. Nothing about the reference template. Nothing about how "correct posture" was defined.

Kiran makes a list of questions:
- What is the reference template based on?
- Who fails the assessment, and why?
- Is there a correlation between PI scores and actual fall risk?

The questions sit on the screen, unanswered. Kiran stares at them for a long time, then closes the laptop and goes to bed.

---

The first month of fieldwork is straightforward. Kiran spends long days observing workers in the quinoa fields, recording gait patterns, measuring load distribution. The workers are polite, curious, willing to participate. They ask questions about the research, about where Kiran is from, about what it's like to study bodies for a living.

Kiran asks questions too. Casual ones, woven into conversation. "How often do you cross the bridge?" "Do you carry loads across, or use the cargo lift?" "Have you ever had trouble with the turnstiles?"

The answers are revealing. Most of the fieldworkers cross the bridge infrequently — once a month, maybe, for medical appointments or to buy things not available on this side. Many of them need accompaniment. One woman, Dolma, says she hasn't crossed alone in four years.

"My score is too low," she explains. She's sitting in the shade during lunch break, eating dried meat from a cloth bundle. "Six point zero. Borderline."

"What does that mean?" Kiran asks.

"It means I don't stand right. My back is curved from the work, and the platform reads it as unstable."

"Are you unstable? Do you fall?"

Dolma laughs, a short, sharp sound. "No. I've been crossing that bridge since I was twelve. I've never fallen. But the platform knows better."

Kiran writes this down. Later, back at the apartment, Kiran adds it to the growing list of observations:
- Fieldworkers cluster at borderline/failing scores
- No correlation between PI and self-reported stability
- "The platform knows better" — repeated phrase

The pattern is becoming visible. Kiran requests access to aggregate PI data from the Registry, citing the research fellowship. The request is denied. Kiran requests accident statistics. The request is approved, but the data is limited — just the overall numbers, no demographic breakdown.

Pre-Index: 24 deaths per year, average.
Post-Index: 9 deaths per year, average.

Sixty-two percent reduction. The Index works.

But Kiran keeps watching the turnstiles, keeps recording who crosses alone and who waits for accompaniment, and the pattern is there. Fieldworkers wait. Haulers wait. Older women wait. Young men in administrative jobs cross alone, confident, their wrist-swipe quick and automatic.

Kiran makes another note: *Who set the standard?*

---

At three months, Kiran submits a formal research proposal to the Equilibrium Registry: "Cross-jurisdictional comparison of fall risk assessment methodologies." The proposal requests access to the calibration history of the Posture Index, including the original reference template and all subsequent recalibrations.

The proposal is denied within a week. No explanation given.

Kiran tries a different approach. The university library has an archive of public health records, including the original documentation from the Index's implementation. Kiran spends a week in the archive, reading through reports that smell like old paper and dust.

The reference template was created in 2051 by the first Equilibrium Officiant, a man named Dorje Tsering. He calibrated the sensors using soldiers from the Margrave's own regiment — men aged 18-25, who had trained in formation drill, whose bodies had been shaped by the same exercises, the same nutrition, the same daily rhythms.

The report describes this as "establishing a baseline of optimal standing posture." It does not mention that the soldiers were all men. It does not mention that they were all from the plateau, all from families wealthy enough to send their sons to military service instead of fieldwork. It does not mention that their bodies had never been bent over rows of quinoa, never carried fifty-pound sacks up steep paths, never held children on their hips for hours at a time.

The report just says: "Baseline established. Reference template stored."

Kiran reads this three times, then closes the folder and sits in the silence of the archive.

The Index measures deviation from a standard. The standard was built from soldiers' postures. Everyone whose body was shaped differently — by different work, different habits, different lives — is measured as deviant.

It's not measuring fall risk. It's measuring difference.

Kiran makes a note: *The template is the extraction mechanism.*

---

At four months, Kiran requests an interview with one of the Officiants. Professional courtesy, researcher to practitioner. The request is approved.

The Officiant is Davin. He's the same age as Kiran, maybe a year older, with a face that suggests kindness and a voice that suggests caution. They meet in a small office at the Registry, and Davin offers tea.

"What do you want to know?" he asks.

Kiran sips the tea. It's good — butter tea with salt, the kind Kiran's grandmother used to make. "I'm curious about the assessment process. How the platform works, what it measures."

"It measures standing stability. Pressure distribution across the feet, postural sway, center of gravity. The algorithm compares the reading to a reference template and generates a score."

"What's the reference template based on?"

Davin hesitates. "Optimal standing posture."

"Defined how?"

"Defined by the original calibration. In 2051."

"Using soldiers."

Davin's face doesn't change, but something in his posture shifts. "You've been reading the archives."

"Yes."

A long pause. Davin drinks his tea. "The Index works," he says finally. "Accidents are down. People are safer."

"I'm not questioning whether it works. I'm questioning what it measures."

"It measures stability."

"Does it?" Kiran leans forward slightly. "I've been observing the turnstiles for three months. Most of the people who need accompaniment are fieldworkers, haulers, mothers with young children. Do they have higher accident rates?"

"I don't have that data."

"I do." Kiran pulls out a notebook, flips to a page covered in numbers. "I cross-referenced the Registry's public access logs with the accident reports from the past ten years. People with PI scores between 5.0 and 6.4 — the ones who need accompaniment — have the same accident rate as people with scores above 6.5. Actually, slightly lower."

Davin is silent.

"So it's not measuring fall risk," Kiran continues. "It's measuring something else. Deviation from a template that was built from soldiers' bodies forty years ago."

"The template has been recalibrated," Davin says. His voice is still careful, still neutral. "Twice. In 2063 and 2078."

"Using data from people who had already passed the assessment."

Davin doesn't answer.

Kiran sits back. "I'm not trying to attack the system. I'm trying to understand it. And what I see is a measurement tool that extracts mobility from people whose bodies don't match a standard that was never designed for them."

"It's the same platform for everyone," Davin says. "The same algorithm. That's fairness."

"Is it? If the standard itself is biased?"

Davin stands. The interview is over. "Thank you for your time," he says, and his voice is polite, distant, the voice of someone who has closed a door.

Kiran leaves the Registry and walks back to the apartment. The notebook feels heavy in the pocket. The questions are still there, but now they have answers, and the answers are worse than the questions.

---

## III.

The year turns. Summer gives way to autumn, and the plateau is gold and brown, the wind sharp with the promise of winter. Mirae's assessment is in two weeks.

She has done the exercises every morning for a year. Her body is the same. She knows this the way she knows her own name, the way she knows Yuna's weight on her left hip, the way she knows the rhythm of the bridge's sway. Her body is stable. The platform will read her as deviant.

At night, she lies awake and counts the possibilities. If she fails again — six point one to five point nine, borderline to failing — she'll still have bridge access with accompaniment. She can still get Yuna to school, still cross for medical appointments, still survive.

But if she fails the year after that, and the year after that — three consecutive fails — the chip will be flagged. Social Services will contact her. Relocation to Cliffside will be "strongly recommended," which is another way of saying required, because bridge access will be fully revoked and living on the plateau without bridge access is functionally impossible.

She has seen the letters. Her mother received one six years ago, after her third fail. She ignored it for a month, then two months, and then the Registry sent someone to her house — a woman in a dark coat with a tablet full of forms. Her mother signed them. She would have gone to Cliffside if Mirae hadn't been there to accompany her every time she needed to cross.

Mirae doesn't have someone like that. Tenzin is kind, but he has his own family, his own work. She can't ask him to accompany her every day, twice a day, for the rest of her life.

She gets up. The house is dark. Yuna is asleep in the next room, her breathing soft and even. Mirae goes to the kitchen, lights a candle, sits at the table.

Her mother's voice from the doorway: "Can't sleep?"

"No."

Her mother sits down across from her. The candlelight makes her face look older, the lines deeper. "Your assessment is soon."

"Two weeks."

"You've been doing the exercises."

"Yes."

"They won't work."

Mirae looks up. Her mother's face is calm, resigned. "How do you know?"

"Because they didn't work for me. Because they don't work for anyone whose body is shaped like ours." Her mother's hands rest on the table, palms down. Fieldworker's hands, scarred and callused. "The platform doesn't measure falling. It measures standing like a soldier."

"You don't know that."

"I do." Her mother's voice is quiet. "I've known it for six years. I just didn't have the words."

Mirae is silent. The candle flickers.

"If you fail three times," her mother says, "we'll go to Cliffside together. It's not so bad. Pema's sister lives there. She says it's quieter. Fewer rules."

"It's exile."

"It's a different place. That's all."

Mirae wants to argue, but she's too tired. She blows out the candle and goes back to bed.

---

The assessment is on a cold morning in late autumn. Mirae stands on the platform and feels the metal through her feet, colder than it was last year, or maybe she's just more aware of it. Davin watches the screen. She watches him watch it.

The screen blinks. His face doesn't change, but something around his eyes tightens.

"Five point nine," he says.

Failing. Not borderline anymore. Failing.

"I've been doing the exercises," Mirae says. Her voice is steady. She's surprised by that.

"I know." Davin's voice is careful. "Your body isn't responding. That means—"

"That means it won't change." Mirae switches to Creole without thinking. *Nga'i lus 'gyur-gi mi-'dug.* My body won't change. Then back to Margravian, because this is the Registry, and the Registry speaks Margravian: "I know my body is stable. I know I won't fall."

"The platform—"

"I know what the platform says." Her voice is still steady. "But I also know what my body says. And they're not the same thing."

Davin is silent. For a moment, Mirae thinks he might say something — might acknowledge that she's right, that the platform is wrong, that the whole system is measuring the wrong thing. But he doesn't. He just hands her the remediation plan, the same exercises she's been doing for a year, and says, "Next year. Same time."

She takes the paper. It feels like nothing in her hand.

---

At home, her mother asks, "How did it go?"

"Five point nine."

Her mother nods. She doesn't look surprised. "One more year," she says. "Then we'll go."

"I don't want to go."

"I know."

Yuna is at the table, drawing with charcoal on scraps of paper. She looks up. "Mama, why can't we cross the bridge alone?"

Mirae crouches down, pulls her daughter into her lap. The weight settles on her left hip, exactly where it belongs. "Because the platform says I don't stand right."

"But you don't fall down."

"No, I don't."

"Then why does the platform say you do?"

Mirae doesn't answer. She holds her daughter and feels the weight of her, the solid realness of her body, the way she fits perfectly against Mirae's ribs. The platform doesn't measure this. The platform doesn't know this.

But the platform is what decides.

---

The winter is long. Mirae does the exercises every morning, even though she knows they won't work. She crosses the bridge with Tenzin when she needs to, and every time she asks him, she feels the weight of the asking, the accumulating debt that she can't repay.

Yuna starts school in the spring. The school is across the bridge. She needs to cross every day.

Mirae's mother can't accompany her — her score is too low. Mirae can accompany her now, but next year, if Mirae fails again, neither of them will be able to cross.

Tenzin offers to take Yuna in the mornings. He crosses anyway, he says. It's no trouble. But Mirae sees his face when he says it, the small tightness around his mouth that means it is trouble, it is an imposition, it is one more thing he has to do for someone else.

She thanks him. What else can she do?

---

At the field, the work is the same as always. Bent over the rows, hands in the soil, back aching. The other workers talk during lunch, their voices low and tired.

Dolma's assessment was last week. She failed. Five point eight. "I did the exercises," she says. "Every day. I stood straighter, I tried to correct the curve. It didn't work."

Pema doesn't say anything. She failed three years ago, and again two years ago, and again last year. This year, she's going to Cliffside. She's already started packing.

"It's because of the work," Dolma says. "If we didn't have to bend over all day, our backs wouldn't curve. We'd stand straighter. We'd pass."

"The work is the work," Pema says. "Someone has to do it."

"Then someone else should do it. Someone who can pass."

Mirae listens. She wants to say: It's not the work. It's the platform. The platform is measuring the wrong thing.

But she doesn't have the words. And even if she did, what would change? The platform is the platform. The system is the system. It works for most people. It saves lives. The fact that it also extracts mobility from people whose bodies don't match the template — that's just the cost. That's just the way things are.

She pulls weeds. Her back aches. The sun moves across the sky.

---

The second assessment is in late spring. Mirae stands on the platform and doesn't count her breaths this time. She just stands. Her body knows what it knows.

The screen blinks. Davin's face is carefully neutral.

"Five point eight," he says.

Second fail. One more and she's gone.

"I've been doing the exercises," Mirae says. She's not sure why she says it. It doesn't matter.

"I know." Davin's voice is quiet. "I'm sorry."

He hands her a piece of paper. Not the remediation plan this time. A notice from Social Services. If she fails next year, she will be relocated to Cliffside. The notice explains the process — how to apply for housing, how to arrange transport, what services are available. It's all very reasonable, very organized, very clear.

Mirae folds the paper and puts it in her pocket.

At home, her mother is waiting. She takes one look at Mirae's face and nods. "One more year," she says.

"One more year."

They sit in silence. Yuna is at school. The house is quiet.

"Lung-ma isn't so bad," her mother says finally. In Creole, because this is not an official thing, this is a real thing. "Pema's sister says there's more space. Fewer rules. The work is different, but it's still work."

"It's exile."

"It's a different place." Her mother's voice is firm. "We'll be together. That's what matters."

Mirae wants to believe her. She wants to believe that Cliffside is just a different place, that relocation is just a change, that the platform's judgment doesn't mean what it feels like it means.

But she knows better. She knows what the notice in her pocket means. She knows what the turnstile will say next time she tries to cross. She knows what her body knows, and she knows it doesn't matter.

The platform knows better.

---

Kiran spends the winter analyzing data. The apartment is cold, the radiator insufficient against the plateau wind, but the laptop stays warm and the numbers fill the screen.

The pattern is clear. Posture Index scores correlate with occupation, with body type, with the kind of work people do. Fieldworkers cluster at borderline and failing. Haulers cluster at borderline. Mothers with young children cluster at borderline. Administrative workers, soldiers, young men who work indoors — they cluster at passing and high-passing.

But accident rates don't correlate with PI scores. Kiran has spent months cross-referencing the Registry's access logs with accident reports, and the correlation is zero. People with low scores don't fall more often than people with high scores. If anything, they fall less often — maybe because they're more careful, more aware of their bodies, more practiced at navigating the bridges.

The Posture Index doesn't measure fall risk. It measures deviation from a template built from soldiers' bodies forty years ago. And because the template has been recalibrated using only data from people who pass, it has drifted over time — become more homogeneous, more narrow, more exclusive.

Kiran writes this up. The paper is twenty pages, dense with statistics and citations. The title is careful, neutral: "Posture Index as Socioeconomic Sorting Mechanism: A Critique of Reference Template Bias."

The paper argues:
1. The PI template was built from a non-representative sample (soldiers).
2. Subsequent recalibrations have reinforced this bias.
3. The Index extracts mobility from people whose bodies were shaped by labor the template doesn't recognize.
4. Bridge safety could be maintained through other mechanisms (better maintenance, better training, better bridge design) without extracting mobility.

The paper does not argue that the Index should be abolished. It argues that the template should be rebuilt using a representative sample — fieldworkers, haulers, mothers, everyone whose bodies actually cross the bridges.

Kiran submits the paper to the *Journal of High-Altitude Kinesiological Studies*. It's accepted within a month. The journal is obscure, low-circulation, read mostly by other researchers in the field. But it's published. The knowledge exists.

In early summer, Kiran requests a meeting with Director Sokol. The request is granted. They meet in Sokol's office in the administrative tower — a large room with windows overlooking the plateau, furniture that looks expensive, air that smells like polish and paper.

Sokol is a woman in her fifties, with gray hair pulled back tight and hands that don't move when she talks. She reads Kiran's paper while Kiran sits across from her, waiting.

When she finishes, she sets the paper down carefully. "This is well-researched," she says.

"Thank you."

"It's also wrong."

Kiran doesn't respond.

"The Posture Index works," Sokol continues. "Accidents are down sixty-two percent. That's not a small number. That's hundreds of lives saved over forty years."

"I'm not disputing that the Index reduces accidents. I'm disputing what it measures."

"It measures standing stability."

"It measures deviation from a template built from soldiers. That's not the same thing."

Sokol's face doesn't change. "The template has been recalibrated. Twice."

"Using data from people who had already passed. That's circular. It doesn't correct the bias. It reinforces it."

"So what do you propose? Rebuild the template using fieldworkers? Haulers? People whose bodies are shaped by labor that makes them unstable?"

"They're not unstable. That's the point. They have the same accident rates as people with high scores."

"According to your analysis."

"According to the data."

Sokol picks up the paper again, flips through it. "Let me ask you something," she says. "If we rebuild the template the way you're suggesting, and bridge accidents go up — whose fault is that?"

Kiran doesn't answer.

"Because the Margrave won't accept higher accident rates. The residents won't accept higher accident rates. The Index works. It saves lives. The fact that it also restricts mobility for some people — that's a cost-benefit question. And the benefit outweighs the cost."

"For whom?"

Sokol's face hardens. "For everyone who doesn't fall off a bridge."

She hands the paper back to Kiran. "Thank you for your work. It's thorough. But the Margrave is satisfied with the current system. There will be no changes."

The meeting is over.

---

Kiran leaves the administrative tower and walks back to the apartment. The paper feels heavy in the bag. The knowledge is there — documented, published, preserved — but it doesn't matter. The system persists.

At the apartment, Kiran packs. The fellowship is ending. The research is complete. There's nothing left to do here.

Before leaving, Kiran walks to the bridge one last time. Stands at the turnstile and watches people cross. Some alone, some accompanied. The pattern is the same as it was ten months ago. Fieldworkers wait. Haulers wait. Older women wait. Young men cross alone.

Kiran makes one final note in the notebook: *The extraction is invisible to the people who benefit from it.*

Then closes the notebook, walks away, and doesn't look back.

The third assessment is in late spring. Mirae stands on the platform and her body knows what the platform will say before the screen blinks.

Five point nine.

Third fail.

Davin doesn't speak. He just hands her the notice. Relocation to Cliffside. Report to Social Services within thirty days to arrange transport.

Mirae takes the paper. She doesn't read it. She knows what it says.

Outside, the plaza is full of people. They cross the bridge in a steady stream, their wrist-swipes quick and automatic. Mirae watches them. None of them are waiting for accompaniment. None of them are carrying the weight she's carrying.

She walks home. Her mother is in the kitchen, and she takes one look at Mirae's face and knows.

"When?" she asks.

"Thirty days."

Her mother nods. "I'll start packing."

Yuna comes running from the other room. She's four now, taller, her face more defined. "Mama, can we go to the bridge today? I want to see the water."

"Not today," Mirae says. She pulls her daughter close, feels the weight of her on her left hip. "But soon. We'll go soon."

"Why not today?"

"Because the platform says so."

Yuna accepts this. She always does. She doesn't understand yet what the platform means, what the score means, what relocation means. She will understand someday. Mirae hopes that day is far away.

---

They pack slowly. There's not much to take — clothes, a few cooking pots, Yuna's toys, the wooden blocks she loves. Mirae's mother packs her mending, her needles and thread. They leave the furniture. Someone else will live here after they're gone.

On the last day, Mirae stands in the doorway and looks at the house. It's small, cold in winter, but it's hers. Was hers. She has lived here her whole life. Her mother lived here before her. Her grandmother before that.

Now they're leaving because the platform says Mirae doesn't stand right.

Tenzin comes to help them carry their things to the cargo lift. He doesn't say much. What is there to say? He hugs Mirae at the lift entrance, and his face is sad, and she knows he feels guilty even though none of this is his fault.

"Thank you," she says. "For everything."

"I'm sorry," he says.

"It's not your fault."

"I know. But I'm still sorry."

The lift descends slowly. Two hundred meters down, into the gulf. Yuna presses her face to the window, watching the rock walls slide past. "It's dark," she says.

"Yes," Mirae says. "It's dark."

At the bottom, Cliffside spreads out before them. It's smaller than Mirae expected, more crowded. The buildings are lower, closer together. The air smells different — damper, heavier, like the gulf traps everything that falls into it.

Her mother takes her hand. "We'll be alright," she says.

Mirae nods. She doesn't believe it yet, but maybe someday she will.

They walk into Cliffside together, and behind them, the lift rises back up to the plateau, where the bridges sway in the wind and the turnstiles read the chips and the platform measures standing and the system works, and works, and works.

In a library in the capital, two hundred kilometers from the plateau, a thin journal sits on a shelf. The journal is called *High-Altitude Kinesiological Studies*, and almost no one reads it. But it's there. Volume 47, Issue 3. Pages 412-431.

The article is titled "Posture Index as Socioeconomic Sorting Mechanism: A Critique of Reference Template Bias."

It documents everything. The template's origin. The calibration history. The correlation between occupation and score. The lack of correlation between score and accident rate. The extraction mechanism built into a system that claims to measure safety.

It's all there. The knowledge exists.

Someday, someone will read it. Someday, someone will ask the questions Kiran asked. Someday, the system might change.

But not today. Today, the platform measures standing. The turnstiles read the chips. The bridges sway in the wind. And in Cliffside, at the bottom of the gulf, Mirae unpacks her daughter's toys and tries to believe that this is just a different place, that the platform was wrong, that her body knows what it knows and that knowledge is enough.

It isn't enough. But it's what she has.

She sets the wooden blocks on the floor. Yuna builds a tower. It falls. She builds it again.
