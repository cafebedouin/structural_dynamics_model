# The Platform Knows

## I.

Mirae stands barefoot on the cold platform, toes curled against the metal. The morning air through the Registry's open windows carries the smell of someone's breakfast — buckwheat porridge, butter tea. Her stomach tightens. She should have eaten.

The Officiant — Davin, she knows his name though she's never used it — watches the screen mounted on the wall. Not her. Never her. Just the numbers the platform generates from the pressure of her feet, the distribution of her weight, the small corrections her body makes without thought.

Her left hip aches where her daughter's weight should be. Yuna is with her mother this morning, but Mirae's body hasn't forgotten the child's mass, the way she settles on the left side, head against Mirae's ribs. The platform reads this. It reads everything — the slight forward tilt from decades of bent fieldwork her mother did before her, the way her right foot takes more weight to compensate, the micro-adjustments her ankles make that she doesn't control, has never controlled.

She breathes. Slowly, steadily. A calming technique the last Officiant taught her. It doesn't calm.

The screen blinks. Davin's face doesn't change, but something around his eyes tightens. "Borderline," he says.

The same. The same as last year, and the year before that. Borderline. Not quite failing, but not safe either. Not the kind of score that lets you cross alone.

"Borderline," Davin confirms, and his voice is careful, neutral, the voice of someone who has said these words many times. "You're still favoring your left side. The asymmetry is—" He pauses. "I'm prescribing the same exercises. Morning wall-standing, weight distribution drills before bed. The core strengthening sequence I showed you last year."

Mirae nods. She has done them. Every morning before dawn, standing with her back pressed to the cold stone wall of her house, her daughter watching with sleepy confusion. Every night after Yuna is asleep, shifting her weight from foot to foot, trying to feel what the platform feels, trying to correct what her body insists is already correct.

"I do them," she says in Margravian, the language of the Registry, of official things. Her accent is thick, the tones imprecise. "Every day."

"Then you need to do them more carefully." Davin's voice is not unkind. "Your body isn't responding. That means you're not doing them correctly, or not consistently enough."

She wants to say: My body knows how to stand. I have never fallen. I carry heavy sacks across the practice bridge behind my house every week, and I have never stumbled, never felt the lurch of actual falling, never lost my grounding.

But she doesn't have the words. Not in Margravian, and not in Creole either. The words for what her body knows don't exist in the language of scores.

"I'll try harder," she says instead.

Davin nods. He prints the remediation plan on thin paper that feels expensive between her fingers. "Next year," he says. "Same time."

She steps off the platform. Her feet find the floor — solid, unmoving, real — and the relief is immediate and sharp. She puts on her shoes, old canvas work boots with the toes wearing through, and walks out into the morning.

The bridge is a short walk from the Registry. She could see it from here if the buildings weren't in the way — the suspension cables, the narrow walkway swaying slightly in the wind that comes up from the Sorting Gulf. Deep, the gulf. She has looked down into it exactly once, when she was young and her mother first brought her to the Registry for assessment. She hasn't looked since.

The turnstile at the bridge entrance reads her chip when she holds her wrist to the scanner. The screen blinks red. **ACCOMPANIED ACCESS ONLY.**

She waits. Her neighbor, Tenzin, is supposed to meet her here. He works at the grain cooperative across the bridge, crosses every morning, has a high score. High enough that the favor doesn't cost him anything except time.

He arrives late, breathless, apologetic. "Sorry, sorry. My daughter was sick, I had to—"

"It's fine," Mirae says, switching to Creole without thinking. *Mi gnod.* It's fine. "Thank you."

He swipes his wrist. The turnstile unlocks with a soft click. They walk through together, and the bridge takes their weight.

It sways. It always sways. The wind comes up from the gulf and catches the cables and the whole structure moves, a slow pendulum rhythm that Mirae's body knows how to read, how to match. She has crossed this bridge more times than she can remember. She has never fallen.

The sway is predictable. When the wind pushes from the east, the bridge tilts slightly left, and her body shifts right to compensate — not a thought, just a response her muscles have learned. When the cables sing their high note, tension increasing, she knows the next gust will be stronger and she plants her feet wider. Tenzin walks beside her, steady but stiff, his body fighting the movement instead of riding it. She could cross faster alone. Her body knows this bridge. The platform doesn't know this bridge.

But Tenzin is here, and the turnstile knows it, and that's what makes it safe.

Halfway across, the wind picks up. The bridge swings wider, and Tenzin's hand shoots out to grip the cable. Mirae doesn't reach for anything. Her knees bend slightly, her weight drops into her hips, and she rides the swing like she's done countless times before. Her left hip takes more of the load — the same asymmetry the platform reads as wrong — and it's exactly what keeps her stable. The compensation is the stability.

Tenzin looks at her, his knuckles white on the cable. "How do you do that?"

She doesn't know how to answer. Her body just knows.

---

Her mother is in the kitchen when Mirae gets home, stirring a pot of rice porridge. Yuna sits on the floor playing with wooden blocks, building towers and knocking them down.

"How did it go?" her mother asks. In Creole, always Creole at home. The question carries the weight of the question beneath it: *Did you pass?*

"Same as last year," Mirae says. She hangs her coat on the hook by the door, slips off her boots. "Borderline."

Her mother's hands still on the spoon. She doesn't look up. "The exercises?"

"He prescribed them again."

"You've been doing them."

"I know."

Yuna looks up from her blocks. "Mama, why do you stand against the wall?"

Mirae crouches down, pulls her daughter into her lap. The weight settles on her left hip, exactly where it belongs. "To make my standing better," she says. "So the platform will read me right."

"But you don't fall down."

"No, I don't."

"Then why does the platform say you do?"

Mirae's mother turns from the stove. Her face is lined, her back curved from years of fieldwork before her hips gave out and she had to stop. Her score has been failing for years. She can't cross the bridge at all anymore without accompaniment, and most days she doesn't bother. She stays on this side, in the house, in the kitchen, in the small radius of her own failing body.

"The platform knows better than we do," Mirae's mother says. She says it in Margravian, the language of official truth. Then switches back to Creole: "Even when it doesn't feel like it knows."

Yuna squirms out of Mirae's lap, goes back to her blocks. Mirae stands, her hip aching, her body settling into the stance it has learned over years of carrying this child: left hip out, right foot planted, spine curved just slightly to compensate.

The stance the platform reads as wrong.

---

At dawn, before Yuna wakes, Mirae stands against the wall. Her shoulder blades press into the cold stone. Her heels are out from the wall, her knees slightly bent, her core engaged the way the diagram on the remediation plan shows. She holds it, breathing slowly, waiting for her body to learn.

Her body knows this is not how she stands. This is not her grounding. But maybe if she does it long enough, the platform will stop reading her as deviant. Maybe her body will learn.

She breathes. Her thighs start to burn. Her lower back aches. This is not the ache of fieldwork, of useful labor. This is the ache of forcing her body into a shape it doesn't want to hold.

Eventually, she stops. Yuna is awake, calling for her. Mirae pushes off the wall and her body floods with relief, settling back into its own knowledge of where it belongs in space.

She makes breakfast. Rice porridge with dried apricots, butter tea with salt. Yuna eats slowly, asking questions about everything — why is the sky blue, why do birds fly, why does the bridge sway. Mirae answers what she can. The sky is blue because that's the color light makes when it comes through air. Birds fly because they have wings. The bridge sways because the wind pushes it.

"Why can't we cross the bridge alone?"

Mirae's hands still on the teacup. "Because the platform says I don't stand right."

"But you don't fall down."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because the platform says so."

Yuna accepts this. She is young. The world is full of rules she doesn't understand yet, rules that adults follow because adults say so. The platform is just another rule.

Mirae finishes her tea. She needs to get Yuna to her mother's house, then walk to the field. The quinoa needs weeding, and if she doesn't get there by mid-morning, the rows will be taken by someone else.

She gathers Yuna, walks the narrow path between houses. Her mother is awake, sitting in the sun outside her door, mending a shirt. She takes Yuna without comment, and Mirae walks to the field.

The work is the same as always. Bent over the rows, hands in the soil, pulling weeds that grow faster than the quinoa. Her back aches by midday. Her spine curves, her weight shifts to her left side because her right knee is weaker, has always been weaker since she fell off a wall when she was young.

Around her, other workers move through the rows. Mostly women, mostly older. She knows some of their names, knows most of their scores. Borderline, failing, chronic fail. The ones who cross the bridge do it accompanied, asking favors from neighbors, from family, from anyone with a passing score who will spare the time.

At lunch, they sit in the shade of the tool shed. Someone has brought dried yak meat to share. Mirae eats slowly, listening to the conversation drift around her.

"My assessment is next month," a woman says. Her name is Dolma, and she is older, and her score has been borderline for years. "I've been doing the exercises."

"They don't work," another woman says. Pema, older still, failing score. "I did them for years. My back won't straighten."

"You have to do them right," Dolma insists. "You have to be consistent."

"I was consistent."

"Then you weren't doing them right."

Pema is silent. Mirae watches her face, the way it closes, the way she turns back to her food. The conversation moves on.

Later, when they're back in the rows, Mirae works next to Dolma. She wants to ask: Do you really believe the exercises work? Do you really think it's about doing them right?

But she doesn't. Because Dolma needs to believe. Because if the exercises don't work, if standing straighter isn't the answer, then there's nothing she can do. And that's worse than believing she's just not trying hard enough.

So Mirae pulls weeds, and her back aches, and the sun moves across the sky, and she says nothing.

## II.

Kiran arrives at the Margraviate on a cold morning in late spring, when the plateau is still brown but the snow on the mountains is starting to melt. The bus from the valley takes hours, winding up roads that switch back and forth across the face of the cliffs, and by the time it reaches the plateau Kiran's ears are ringing from the altitude.

The research fellowship is for a year. Kinesiological study of high-altitude populations. Gait patterns, load-carrying biomechanics, adaptation to thin air. Kiran has done similar work in Nepal, in Tibet, in the Andes. This should be straightforward.

The university provides housing — a small apartment near the main campus, heated by a radiator that clanks and hisses. Kiran unpacks methodically. Laptop, recording equipment, pressure sensors, books. By evening, the apartment feels less like a temporary space and more like a place where work can happen.

The next morning, Kiran walks to the Central Registry for onboarding. The building is stone, old, with narrow windows that let in slanted light. Inside, it smells like paper and floor wax and something else, something metallic that Kiran can't identify.

The Officiant at the desk is young, with a face that suggests he smiles often but isn't smiling now. "Name?" he asks in Margravian.

"Kiran Shrestha."

"Purpose of visit?"

"Research fellowship. Kinesiological Institute."

The Officiant types something into his terminal. "You'll need an assessment. Standard procedure for all residents and visitors."

Kiran nods. This was mentioned in the fellowship paperwork, though not explained in detail. "What kind of assessment?"

"Equilibrium. Step onto the platform, please."

The platform is in the center of the room, a square of metal, slightly raised from the floor. Kiran steps onto it barefoot — the Officiant gestured at the shoes, so shoes come off first. The metal is cold. Kiran stands, weight distributed evenly, or as evenly as possible given the slight scoliosis that pulls the spine into a curve, the left leg that's shorter than the right.

The Officiant watches a screen. His expression doesn't change, but something in his posture relaxes slightly. "Well above threshold," he says. "You're cleared for unrestricted bridge access." He swipes a card through a machine, hands it to Kiran. "This is your chip band. Wear it at all times. The turnstiles will read it when you cross."

Kiran looks at the band — a simple strip of woven fabric with a small chip embedded in the center — and then at the Officiant. "What does the assessment measure?"

"Standing stability."

"Against what reference?"

The Officiant pauses. "Against correct posture."

"Whose?"

He doesn't answer. After a moment, he says, "If you have questions about the methodology, you can submit a request to the Equilibrium Registry's administrative office. They handle all research inquiries."

Kiran nods slowly. "Thank you."

Outside, Kiran stands in the plaza and looks at the bridge. It's visible from here — a narrow suspension bridge stretching across the gulf, cables gleaming in the morning light. People cross it in a steady stream, some alone, some in pairs. At the near end, there's a turnstile, and everyone who passes through it holds their wrist to a scanner.

Kiran walks closer. Watches. Most people cross alone, their wrist-swipe quick and automatic. But some — not many, but some — wait at the turnstile until another person arrives. They swipe together. The turnstile unlocks. They cross side by side.

Kiran makes a note in the small notebook that lives in every pocket: *Accompanied access. Why?*

---

The Kinesiological Institute is a low building on the edge of campus, with labs full of equipment that looks expensive and slightly outdated. Kiran's supervisor, Dr. Yangchen, is a woman with close-cropped gray hair and hands that move constantly while she talks.

"Your focus is gait analysis," she says, spreading a map across her desk. "We're particularly interested in load-carrying patterns. The fieldworkers here carry significant weight over uneven terrain, and we want to understand the biomechanical adaptations."

Kiran nods. "What about the bridge crossings? I noticed some people need accompaniment."

Dr. Yangchen's hands still for a moment. "That's related to the Posture Index. Not your research area."

"But it's a kinesiological question, isn't it? If the Index measures standing stability—"

"It measures standing posture. Not the same thing." Dr. Yangchen's voice is careful, neutral. "The Index is a public health measure. It's administered by the Equilibrium Registry, not by us. If you want to study it, you'll need separate approval."

"From whom?"

"The Registry. And the Margrave's office of Public Health."

Kiran makes another note. "Is that difficult to get?"

"It's not usually granted. The Index is considered... sensitive. Politically."

"Why?"

Dr. Yangchen looks at Kiran for a long moment. "Because it works," she says finally. "Bridge accidents are down significantly since it was implemented. The Margrave is very proud of it. So are most residents. Questioning it is..." She pauses. "Not encouraged."

Kiran closes the notebook. "I understand."

But later, alone in the apartment, Kiran opens the laptop and searches for information about the Posture Index. The official documentation is sparse — a public health bulletin from decades ago, some accident statistics, a brief description of the assessment procedure. Nothing about the reference template. Nothing about how "correct posture" was defined.

Kiran makes a list of questions:
- What is the reference template based on?
- Who fails the assessment, and why?
- Is there a correlation between PI scores and actual fall risk?

The questions sit on the screen, unanswered. Kiran stares at them for a long time, then closes the laptop and goes to bed.

---

The first month of fieldwork is straightforward. Kiran spends long days observing workers in the quinoa fields, recording gait patterns, measuring load distribution. The workers are polite, curious, willing to participate. They ask questions about the research, about where Kiran is from, about what it's like to study bodies for a living.

Kiran asks questions too. Casual ones, woven into conversation. "How often do you cross the bridge?" "Do you carry loads across, or use the cargo lift?" "Have you ever had trouble with the turnstiles?"

The answers are revealing. Most of the fieldworkers cross the bridge infrequently — for medical appointments or to buy things not available on this side. Many of them need accompaniment. A woman, Dolma, says she hasn't crossed alone in years.

"My score is too low," she explains. She's sitting in the shade during lunch break, eating dried meat from a cloth bundle. "Borderline."

"What does that mean?" Kiran asks.

"It means I don't stand right. My back is curved from the work, and the platform reads it as unstable."

"Are you unstable? Do you fall?"

Dolma laughs, a short, sharp sound. "No. I've been crossing that bridge since I was young. I've never fallen. But the platform knows better."

Kiran writes this down. Later, back at the apartment, Kiran adds it to the growing list of observations:
- Fieldworkers cluster at borderline/failing scores
- No correlation between PI and self-reported stability
- "The platform knows better" — repeated phrase

The pattern is becoming visible. Kiran requests access to aggregate PI data from the Registry, citing the research fellowship. The request is denied. Kiran requests accident statistics. The request is approved, but the data is limited — just the overall numbers, no demographic breakdown.

Pre-Index: deaths per year, significant.
Post-Index: deaths per year, much lower.

Major reduction. The Index works.

But Kiran keeps watching the turnstiles, keeps recording who crosses alone and who waits for accompaniment, and the pattern is there. Fieldworkers wait. Haulers wait. Older women wait. Young men in administrative jobs cross alone, confident, their wrist-swipe quick and automatic.

Kiran makes another note: *Who set the standard?*

---

The university library has an archive of public health records, including the original documentation from the Index's implementation. Kiran spends a week in the archive, reading through reports that smell like old paper and dust.

The reference template was created decades ago. The first Equilibrium Officiant — a man named Dorje Tsering — calibrated the sensors in the old military barracks, before the Registry building was constructed.

Kiran finds the calibration log. Handwritten, the ink faded but still legible. Dorje's notes are meticulous:

*First day: Baseline establishment. Subjects: Margrave's regiment, second company. Standing drill formation. Sensors calibrated to mean postural distribution. Template stored.*

*Second day: Verification trials. Same subjects. Consistency confirmed. Template locked.*

*Third day: First civilian assessments. High variance noted. Recalibration unnecessary — variance indicates deviation from optimal posture, not sensor error.*

Kiran reads this several times. The template was built from soldiers standing at attention. Young men who had trained in formation drill, whose bodies had been shaped by the same exercises, the same nutrition, the same daily rhythms. The report describes this as "establishing a baseline of optimal standing posture."

It does not mention that the soldiers were all men. It does not mention that they were all from the plateau, all from families wealthy enough to send their sons to military service instead of fieldwork. It does not mention that their bodies had never been bent over rows of quinoa, never carried heavy sacks up steep paths, never held children on their hips for hours at a time.

The report just says: "Baseline established. Reference template stored."

Kiran sits in the silence of the archive, holding the log. The template is the mechanism. It measures deviation from a standard that was never designed to include the people who do the work that keeps the plateau fed.

Later, Kiran finds the recalibration records. Every few years, the Registry recalibrates the sensors to account for drift. But the recalibration uses data from people who have already passed the assessment. The template doesn't broaden. It narrows.

Kiran makes a note: *The template reproduces itself.*

---

In the apartment, Kiran spreads the data across the desk. Access logs from the Registry (public record, anonymized). Accident reports from the past decade (approved request). Demographic surveys from the university (archived, accessible).

The correlation work takes weeks. Matching timestamps, filtering noise, building the dataset row by row. Kiran writes a script to parse the access logs, flagging every instance of accompanied access. Another script to cross-reference with the demographic data — occupation, age, household structure.

The pattern emerges slowly, then all at once.

Accompanied access clusters by occupation. Fieldworkers: high frequency. Haulers: high frequency. Domestic workers: high frequency. Administrative workers: low frequency. Soldiers: zero instances.

Accompanied access clusters by body type. Spinal curvature (self-reported in health surveys): correlation positive. Hip asymmetry: correlation positive. History of load-carrying work: correlation positive.

Accompanied access does not cluster by accident history.

Kiran runs the accident correlation repeatedly, checking for errors. The result is the same each time: people who need accompaniment have the same fall rate as people who don't. Actually, slightly lower — though the difference is within the margin of error.

Kiran sits back from the laptop. The room is dark. The radiator clanks.

The Posture Index doesn't measure fall risk. It measures deviation from a template built from soldiers' bodies. And because the template has been recalibrated using only data from people who pass, it has drifted over time — become more homogeneous, more narrow, more exclusive of the bodies that do the plateau's labor.

The knowledge sits there, undeniable. Kiran prints the correlation tables, files them in a folder. Opens a new document and starts writing.

---

After several months, Kiran submits a formal research proposal to the Equilibrium Registry: "Cross-jurisdictional comparison of fall risk assessment methodologies." The proposal requests access to the calibration history of the Posture Index, including the original reference template and all subsequent recalibrations.

The proposal is denied within a week. No explanation given.

Kiran tries a different approach. Requests an interview with an Officiant. Professional courtesy, researcher to practitioner. The request is approved.

The Officiant is Davin. He's around Kiran's age, with a face that suggests kindness and a voice that suggests caution. They meet in a small office at the Registry, and Davin offers tea.

"What do you want to know?" he asks.

Kiran sips the tea. It's good — butter tea with salt, the kind Kiran's grandmother used to make. "I'm curious about the assessment process. How the platform works, what it measures."

"It measures standing stability. Pressure distribution across the feet, postural sway, center of gravity. The algorithm compares the reading to a reference template and generates a score."

"What's the reference template based on?"

Davin hesitates. "Optimal standing posture."

"Defined how?"

"Defined by the original calibration. Decades ago."

"Using soldiers."

Davin's face doesn't change, but something in his posture shifts. "You've been reading the archives."

"Yes."

A long pause. Davin drinks his tea. "The Index works," he says finally. "Accidents are down. People are safer."

"I'm not questioning whether it works. I'm questioning what it measures."

"It measures stability."

"Does it?" Kiran leans forward slightly. "I've been observing the turnstiles for months. Most of the people who need accompaniment are fieldworkers, haulers, mothers with young children. Do they have higher accident rates?"

"I don't have that data."

"I do." Kiran pulls out a notebook, flips to a page covered in tables and annotations. "I cross-referenced the Registry's public access logs with the accident reports from the past decade. People with low PI scores — the ones who need accompaniment — have the same accident rate as people with high scores. Actually, slightly lower."

Davin is silent.

"So it's not measuring fall risk," Kiran continues. "It's measuring something else. Deviation from a template that was built from soldiers' bodies decades ago."

"The template has been recalibrated," Davin says. His voice is still careful, still neutral. "Multiple times."

"Using data from people who had already passed the assessment."

Davin doesn't answer.

Kiran sits back. "I'm not trying to attack the system. I'm trying to understand it. And what I see is a measurement tool that restricts mobility for people whose bodies don't match a standard that was never designed for them."

"It's the same platform for everyone," Davin says. "The same algorithm. That's fairness."

"Is it? If the standard itself is biased?"

Davin stands. The interview is over. "Thank you for your time," he says, and his voice is polite, distant, the voice of someone who has closed a door.

Kiran leaves the Registry and walks back to the apartment. The notebook feels heavy in the pocket. The questions have answers now, and the answers are worse than the questions.

---

Kiran spends the winter analyzing data. The apartment is cold, the radiator insufficient against the plateau wind, but the laptop stays warm and the numbers fill the screen.

The pattern is clear. Posture Index scores correlate with occupation, with body type, with the kind of work people do. Fieldworkers cluster at borderline and failing. Haulers cluster at borderline. Mothers with young children cluster at borderline. Administrative workers, soldiers, young men who work indoors — they cluster at passing and high-passing.

But accident rates don't correlate with PI scores. Kiran has spent months cross-referencing the Registry's access logs with accident reports, and the correlation is zero. People with low scores don't fall more often than people with high scores. If anything, they fall less often — maybe because they're more careful, more aware of their bodies, more practiced at navigating the bridges.

The Posture Index doesn't measure fall risk. It measures deviation from a template built from soldiers' bodies decades ago. And because the template has been recalibrated using only data from people who pass, it has drifted over time — become more homogeneous, more narrow, more exclusive.

Kiran writes this up. The paper is dense with statistics and citations. The title is careful, neutral: "Posture Index as Socioeconomic Sorting Mechanism: A Critique of Reference Template Bias."

The paper argues:
- The PI template was built from a non-representative sample (soldiers).
- Subsequent recalibrations have reinforced this bias.
- The Index restricts mobility for people whose bodies were shaped by labor the template doesn't recognize.
- Bridge safety could be maintained through other mechanisms (better maintenance, better training, better bridge design) without restricting mobility.

The paper does not argue that the Index should be abolished. It argues that the template should be rebuilt using a representative sample — fieldworkers, haulers, mothers, everyone whose bodies actually cross the bridges.

Kiran submits the paper to the *Journal of High-Altitude Kinesiological Studies*. It's accepted within weeks. The journal is obscure, low-circulation, read mostly by other researchers in the field. But it's published. The knowledge exists.

---

In early summer, Kiran requests a meeting with Director Sokol. The request is granted. They meet in Sokol's office in the administrative tower — a large room with windows overlooking the plateau, furniture that looks expensive, air that smells like polish and paper.

Sokol is a woman with gray hair pulled back tight and hands that don't move when she talks. She reads Kiran's paper while Kiran sits across from her, waiting.

When she finishes, she sets the paper down carefully. "This is well-researched," she says.

"Thank you."

"And your correlation data is correct."

Kiran pauses. "Then you agree the Index doesn't measure fall risk."

"No." Sokol's voice is calm, matter-of-fact. "I agree your data shows no correlation between PI scores and accident rates. But that's not what the Index measures."

"The public health bulletin says—"

"The bulletin says the Index improves bridge safety. Which it does. Accidents are down dramatically since implementation. That's documented."

"But if low-scoring people don't fall more often—"

"The Index doesn't measure individual fall risk," Sokol says. "It measures compliance with postural standards. Compliance produces predictability. Predictability reduces accidents."

Kiran stares at her. "You're saying it works because it restricts access."

"I'm saying it works because it creates a population of bridge users whose bodies move in consistent, measurable ways. The bridges were designed for a certain range of postural variation. The Index ensures users stay within that range."

"By excluding people whose bodies don't fit the template."

"By identifying people who need additional support." Sokol's face doesn't change. "Accompanied access exists for a reason. It's not exclusion. It's accommodation."

"Accommodation that requires asking favors. That makes crossing dependent on someone else's schedule, someone else's willingness."

"That's a resource allocation question. Not a measurement question."

Kiran leans forward. "Let me ask you something. If we rebuild the template the way I'm suggesting — using a representative sample — and bridge accidents stay the same or go down, would you support it?"

Sokol is silent for a moment. "If accidents went down, yes. But I don't think they would."

"Why not?"

"Because the current template works. It's been refined over decades. Changing it would introduce unpredictability." She picks up the paper again. "Your proposal assumes the template is arbitrary. It's not. It's the result of iterative optimization. Every recalibration makes it more precise."

"More precise at measuring what?"

"Deviation from optimal standing posture."

"Optimal for whom?"

Sokol's face hardens slightly. "For bridge safety. Which is what the Margrave cares about. Which is what the residents care about. The Index saves lives. That's not a small thing."

"I'm not saying it doesn't save lives. I'm saying it also restricts mobility for people whose bodies were shaped by the work that keeps this place running."

"That's a cost-benefit question," Sokol says. "And the benefit outweighs the cost."

"For whom?"

"For everyone who doesn't fall off a bridge."

She hands the paper back to Kiran. "Thank you for your work. It's thorough. But the Margrave is satisfied with the current system. There will be no changes."

The meeting is over.

---

Kiran leaves the administrative tower and walks back to the apartment. The paper feels like nothing in the bag. The knowledge is there — documented, published, preserved — but Sokol's words sit heavier. *The Index measures compliance with postural standards. Compliance produces predictability.*

The mechanism isn't broken. It's working exactly as designed.

At the apartment, Kiran packs. The fellowship is ending. The research is complete. There's nothing left to do here.

Before leaving, Kiran walks to the bridge. Stands at the turnstile and watches people cross. Some alone, some accompanied. The pattern is the same as it was months ago. Fieldworkers wait. Haulers wait. Older women wait. Young men cross alone.

Kiran opens the notebook that's lived in every pocket for a year, pages filled with observations, correlations, questions that became answers. The final entry reads: *The mechanism is invisible to the people who benefit from it.*

Kiran closes the notebook and puts it in the bag with everything else. The data exists. The analysis is sound. The journal will sit on library shelves, unread by anyone who could change the system. Kiran knew this when writing the paper, knew it when submitting it, knows it now walking away from the bridge without looking back. The frustration isn't despair. It's the specific, material weight of producing knowledge that will change nothing.

The bus down from the plateau takes hours. Kiran doesn't look out the window.

## III.

The year turns. Summer gives way to autumn, and the plateau is gold and brown, the wind sharp with the promise of winter. Mirae's assessment approaches.

She has done the exercises every morning for a year. Her body is the same. She knows this the way she knows her own name, the way she knows Yuna's weight on her left hip, the way she knows the rhythm of the bridge's sway. Her body is stable. The platform will read her as deviant.

At night, she lies awake and thinks through the possibilities. If she fails again — borderline to failing — she'll still have bridge access with accompaniment. She can still get Yuna to school, still cross for medical appointments, still survive.

But if she fails the year after that, and the year after that — consecutive fails — the chip will be flagged. Social Services will contact her. Relocation to Cliffside will be "strongly recommended," which is another way of saying required, because bridge access will be fully revoked and living on the plateau without bridge access is functionally impossible.

She has seen the letters. Her mother received such a letter years ago, after her third fail. She ignored it for a while, and then the Registry sent someone to her house — a woman in a dark coat with a tablet full of forms. Her mother signed them. She would have gone to Cliffside if Mirae hadn't been there to accompany her every time she needed to cross.

Mirae doesn't have someone like that. Tenzin is kind, but he has his own family, his own work. She can't ask him to accompany her every day, twice a day, for the rest of her life.

She gets up. The house is dark. Yuna is asleep in the next room, her breathing soft and even. Mirae goes to the kitchen, lights a candle, sits at the table.

Her mother's voice from the doorway: "Can't sleep?"

"No."

Her mother sits down across from her. The candlelight makes her face look older, the lines deeper. "Your assessment is soon."

"Soon."

"You've been doing the exercises."

"Yes."

"They won't work."

Mirae looks up. Her mother's face is calm, resigned. "How do you know?"

"Because they didn't work for me. Because they don't work for anyone whose body is shaped like ours." Her mother's hands rest on the table, palms down. Fieldworker's hands, scarred and callused. "The platform doesn't measure falling. It measures standing like a soldier."

"You don't know that."

"I do." Her mother's voice is quiet. "I've known it for years. I just didn't have the words."

Mirae is silent. The candle flickers.

"If you fail repeatedly," her mother says, "we'll go to Cliffside together. It's not so bad. Pema's sister lives there. She says it's quieter. Fewer rules."

"It's exile."

"It's a different place. That's all."

Mirae wants to argue, but she's too tired. She blows out the candle and goes back to bed.

---

The assessment is on a cold morning in late autumn. Mirae stands on the platform and feels the metal through her feet, colder than it was last year, or maybe she's just more aware of it. Davin watches the screen. She watches him watch it.

The screen blinks. His face doesn't change, but something around his eyes tightens.

"Failing," he says.

Failing. Not borderline anymore. Failing.

"I've been doing the exercises," Mirae says. Her voice is steady. She's surprised by that.

"I know." Davin's voice is careful. "Your body isn't responding. That means—"

"That means it won't change." Mirae switches to Creole without thinking. *Nga'i lus 'gyur-gi mi-'dug.* My body won't change. Then back to Margravian, because this is the Registry, and the Registry speaks Margravian: "I know my body is stable. I know I won't fall."

"The platform—"

"I know what the platform says." Her voice is still steady. "But I also know what my body says. And they're not the same thing."

Davin is silent. For a moment, Mirae thinks he might say something — might acknowledge that she's right, that the platform is wrong, that the whole system is measuring the wrong thing. But he doesn't. He just hands her the remediation plan, the same exercises she's been doing for a year, and says, "Next year. Same time."

She takes the paper. It feels like nothing in her hand.

---

At home, her mother asks, "How did it go?"

"Failing."

Her mother nods. She doesn't look surprised. "Another year," she says. "Then we'll go."

"I don't want to go."

"I know."

Yuna is at the table, drawing with charcoal on scraps of paper. She looks up. "Mama, why can't we cross the bridge alone?"

Mirae crouches down, pulls her daughter into her lap. The weight settles on her left hip, exactly where it belongs. "Because the platform says I don't stand right."

"But you don't fall down."

"No, I don't."

"Then why does the platform say you do?"

Mirae doesn't answer. She holds her daughter and feels the weight of her, the solid realness of her body, the way she fits perfectly against Mirae's ribs. The platform doesn't measure this. The platform doesn't know this.

But the platform is what decides.

---

The winter is long. Mirae does the exercises every morning, even though she knows they won't work. She crosses the bridge with Tenzin when she needs to, and every time she asks him, she feels the weight of the asking, the accumulating debt that she can't repay.

Yuna starts school in the spring. The school is across the bridge. She needs to cross every day.

Mirae's mother can't accompany her — her score is too low. Mirae can accompany her now, but next year, if Mirae fails again, neither of them will be able to cross.

Tenzin offers to take Yuna in the mornings. He crosses anyway, he says. It's no trouble. But Mirae sees his face when he says it, the small tightness around his mouth that means it is trouble, it is an imposition, it is something more he has to do for someone else.

She thanks him. What else can she do?

---

The year between second fail and third fail is a year of asking. Mirae keeps a mental inventory of the favors — Tenzin for Yuna's morning crossings, her neighbor Sangmo for medical appointments, Dolma's cousin for the market runs. Each request costs something. Not money — the favors are freely given, or seem to be — but the invisible accumulation of social debt that Mirae can feel but not quantify.

She tries to minimize the crossings. Buys in bulk when she can. Postpones the dentist. Sends Yuna to her mother's house on days when school is closed so there's no crossing needed. But life requires crossings. The plateau is divided by the gulf, and everything necessary is on the other side.

In summer, Sangmo's daughter gets sick. Sangmo needs to cross several times daily for a week to take her to the clinic. She can't accompany Mirae during that time. Mirae asks Tenzin to add another crossing to his daily routine — the evening crossing, when Yuna comes home from school.

He agrees. Of course he agrees. But his wife mentions, casually, that their family is stretched thin. That Tenzin is tired. That maybe Mirae should think about Cliffside, where crossings aren't necessary, where life is simpler.

The suggestion is gentle. It's meant kindly. It still feels like a door closing.

Mirae stops asking Tenzin for the evening crossing. She arranges for Yuna to stay with her mother on school days, crossing only in the morning when Tenzin is already going. It means Mirae doesn't see her daughter most evenings. It means her mother, whose back is worse every year, is doing more childcare than her body can sustain.

But it means fewer favors asked. Fewer debts accumulated. Fewer reminders that Mirae's body doesn't fit the template and everyone around her is adjusting their lives to accommodate that fact.

At the field, Pema is gone. She moved to Cliffside in early spring. The other workers don't talk about her much. It's not that they've forgotten — it's that her absence is a reminder of what's coming for most of them. Better not to dwell.

Dolma's assessment is in late summer. She comes back to the field the next day and doesn't say anything, but Mirae sees her face and knows. Failing. Maybe second fail, maybe third. Dolma works through the morning without speaking, her hands moving automatically through the rows.

At lunch, she sits apart from the others. Mirae brings her tea.

"Thank you," Dolma says. Her voice is flat.

"How many?" Mirae asks.

"Third."

Mirae is silent. Third fail means relocation. Dolma knows this. Everyone knows this.

"I did the exercises," Dolma says. "Every day. I stood straighter. I tried." Her hands are shaking slightly. "It wasn't enough."

"It's not about trying," Mirae says quietly. "The platform measures the wrong thing."

Dolma looks at her. "What do you mean?"

Mirae doesn't know how to explain it. She doesn't have the words, doesn't have the knowledge that Kiran documented in a journal that sits unread in a library somewhere. She just knows what her body knows: that the platform is wrong, that the template is wrong, that the whole system is measuring deviation from a standard that was never meant to include them.

"I mean our bodies are fine," Mirae says. "We don't fall. We've never fallen. But the platform says we will, so we're the ones who have to leave."

Dolma is silent. After a while, she says, "Where will you go?"

"Cliffside. When it's time."

"Me too." Dolma's voice is quiet. "Pema's sister says it's not so bad."

They both know she's lying. But what else is there to say?

---

The second assessment is in late spring. Mirae stands on the platform and doesn't try to control her breathing this time. She just stands. Her body knows what it knows.

The screen blinks. Davin's face is carefully neutral.

"Second fail," he says.

Second fail. Another and she's gone.

"I've been doing the exercises," Mirae says. She's not sure why she says it. It doesn't matter.

"I know." Davin's voice is quiet. Then, after a pause: "I'm sorry."

He hands her a piece of paper. Not the remediation plan this time. A notice from Social Services. If she fails next year, she will be relocated to Cliffside. The notice explains the process — how to apply for housing, how to arrange transport, what services are available. It's all very reasonable, very organized, very clear.

Davin holds the paper for a moment before releasing it. His hand is steady, but something in his face has changed. He looks at the notice, then at Mirae, then back at the notice.

"I have to file this," he says. His voice is still neutral, but quieter. "It's required after a second fail."

Mirae takes the paper. She folds it and puts it in her pocket.

Davin sits down at his desk. He doesn't look at her. "The exercises," he says. "They're not—" He stops. Starts again. "Your body is doing what it's supposed to do. For your work. For your life. The platform just..." He trails off.

Mirae waits.

"I'm sorry," he says again. And this time it sounds like he means something more than the score.

She leaves without answering.

---

The third assessment is in late spring. Mirae stands on the platform and her body knows what the platform will say before the screen blinks.

Failing.

Third fail.

Davin doesn't speak. He just hands her the notice. Relocation to Cliffside. Report to Social Services within a month to arrange transport.

Mirae takes the paper. She doesn't read it. She knows what it says.

Outside, the plaza is full of people. They cross the bridge in a steady stream, their wrist-swipes quick and automatic. Mirae watches them. None of them are waiting for accompaniment. None of them are carrying the weight she's carrying.

She walks home. Her mother is in the kitchen, and she takes a look at Mirae's face and knows.

"When?" she asks.

"A month."

Her mother nods. "I'll start packing."

Yuna comes running from the other room. She's older now, taller, her face more defined. "Mama, can we go to the bridge today? I want to see the water."

"Not today," Mirae says. She pulls her daughter close, feels the weight of her on her left hip. "But soon. We'll go soon."

"Why not today?"

"Because the platform says so."

Yuna accepts this. She always does. She doesn't understand yet what the platform means, what the score means, what relocation means. She will understand someday. Mirae hopes that day is far away.

---

They pack slowly. There's not much to take — clothes, a few cooking pots, Yuna's toys, the wooden blocks she loves. Mirae's mother packs her mending, her needles and thread. They leave the furniture. Someone else will live here after they're gone.

On the last day, Mirae stands in the doorway and looks at the house. It's small, cold in winter, but it's hers. Was hers. She has lived here her whole life. Her mother lived here before her. Her grandmother before that.

Now they're leaving because the platform says Mirae doesn't stand right.

Tenzin comes to help them carry their things to the cargo lift. He doesn't say much. What is there to say? He hugs Mirae at the lift entrance, and his face is sad, and she knows he feels guilty even though none of this is his fault.

"Thank you," she says. "For everything."

"I'm sorry," he says.

"It's not your fault."

"I know. But I'm still sorry."

The lift descends slowly. Deep down, into the gulf. Yuna presses her face to the window, watching the rock walls slide past. "It's dark," she says.

"Yes," Mirae says. "It's dark."

At the bottom, the air changes. Mirae feels it before she sees anything — the weight of it in her lungs, thick and damp, nothing like the thin clean wind of the plateau. She takes a breath and it doesn't satisfy. Takes another. Her body knows immediately: this air will make her sick.

The lift doors open.

Cliffside spreads out before them. The buildings are low, crowded together, their walls black with damp. The streets are narrow, unpaved, slick with water that drips constantly from somewhere above. The air is heavy, thick, hard to breathe. It smells like mold and standing water and something else, something sour that Mirae can't identify.

The light is dim. The sun reaches the bottom of the gulf for only a few hours each day, and even then it's filtered, weak, more gray than gold. Shadows pool in the corners, in the alleys between buildings. Everything is damp. Everything is dark.

People move through the streets with their heads down, their shoulders hunched. Their faces are tired, their bodies bent. A woman coughs wetly in a doorway, the sound rattling, going on and on. No one looks at her. No one looks at anything.

The housing office is a small building with a crooked door and a woman behind a desk who barely glances at their papers before assigning them a room. "Third building, second floor," she says. "Rent is due monthly. Work assignments are posted every morning at the cooperative."

The room is a single space with a small window that looks out at a wall. There's a stove, a table, a sleeping platform. The walls are damp. Mold grows in the corners, black and spreading. The floor is uneven. Water has pooled near the door.

Her mother sets down her bundle and looks around. "We'll make it work," she says. But her voice is thin.

Mirae doesn't answer. She unpacks Yuna's toys, sets the wooden blocks on the floor. Yuna builds a tower. It falls. She builds it again.

Outside, someone is coughing — the same wet, rattling sound. It goes on and on. The dripping water is constant, a rhythm that never stops. Mirae thinks of the plateau, the wide sky, the wind that comes clean off the mountains. She thinks of the bridge, the way it sways, the way her body knows its rhythm.

She thinks of the platform, measuring her body against a template built from soldiers who never bent over quinoa rows, never carried children on their hips, never lived in bodies shaped by the work that keeps the plateau fed.

The platform was wrong. Her body knows this. But the platform is what decides, and now she is here, at the bottom of the gulf, where the failed are sent.

This is not a different place. This is exile. This is where they put the people whose bodies don't fit the template. This is where they put the people who cost the system more than they're worth.

Her mother touches her shoulder. "We'll be alright," she says.

Mirae wants to believe her. But the room is damp, and the air is heavy, and the light is dim, and she knows this is abandonment dressed up as relocation. This is what happens when your body doesn't match the standard. This is the cost.

Yuna's tower falls again. She laughs, starts rebuilding. She doesn't understand yet. Someday she will. Someday she'll stand on the platform and the screen will blink and someone will tell her she doesn't stand right, and she'll remember this room, this moment, her mother's face in the dim light.

Mirae sits down on the floor next to her daughter. She picks up a block. Together, they build.

---

In a library in the capital, far from the plateau, a thin journal sits on a shelf. The journal is called *High-Altitude Kinesiological Studies*, and almost no one reads it. But it's there. Recent issue. The article is titled "Posture Index as Socioeconomic Sorting Mechanism: A Critique of Reference Template Bias."

It documents everything. The template's origin. The calibration history. The correlation between occupation and score. The lack of correlation between score and accident rate. The mechanism built into a system that claims to measure safety.

It's all there. The knowledge exists.

But in Cliffside, at the bottom of the gulf, Mirae builds towers with her daughter in a room that smells like mold. The platform measured her body and found it wanting. The knowledge that the platform was wrong exists somewhere, in a journal no one reads, in an archive no consultants.

It exists. But it changes nothing.

She sets another block on the tower. It stands, for now. That's all she has.
