# The Datum Stone

Every right to water in the Osset valley is written as a height. So many hands above the Stone: that is the whole grammar of it. The miller at Crossfords holds at two hands above; the Berrel orchards at four; the high pastures, which were folded into the rolls last of all, at eleven and twelve. In a wet year nobody thinks in heights at all — the river is enough for everyone, and the rolls sleep in the assessor's chest. In a dry year the assessor walks the channels with his gauge-staff, and the sluices close from the top of the rolls downward, twelve first, eleven next, until what is left in the river matches what the lowest heights are owed. The high numbers water last and go dry first. That is not cruelty; it is arithmetic. Everything in the valley drinks in the order of its distance from the Stone.

The Stone itself sits in the bank below the old bridge, a block of grey wacke with a cut line across its face and the crown's mark over the line, and I want you to understand what that cut line is, because the valley did not, for four generations, and I did not either, and I carried the staff.

It is nothing. It is a line. It is not the river's own mind, not the level of any flood, not the height of God's thumb. One hundred and some years ago a surveyor came up the valley with a brass level and a chain crew, lodged eleven weeks at the upper mill — the upper mill paid his lodging; the rolls do not record this, the mill's own daybook does — and when he left, there was a line cut in a stone in the bank, and every water right in the Osset has been counted from it since. From it, mind. Not toward anything. The heights hang from that line like hooks from a beam, and if you ask what the line itself hangs from, the honest answer, the only answer, is: eleven weeks at the upper mill, a brass level, and a man's hand. The rolls call the line *the crown datum*. The valley calls it the Stone and steps over it to fish. Nobody in my lifetime asked what it stood on, because a thing that everything else is measured from does not look like a thing that could itself be anywhere. It looks like the bottom.

I was born at nine hands, which is to say: born to go dry early. My family walks the water — walked it, I should say. Walking the water is not in the rolls. It was the valley's older habit, from before the Stone, and it survived beside the rolls the way a footpath survives beside a turnpike: on the dry-year nights, the walkers went along the channels dusk to dawn, opening and stopping the little cuts by turns, moving what water there was around the valley's gardens by a rhythm nobody wrote and everybody's feet knew. Who walked, and when, and how long each garden drank — none of it was owed, none of it could be demanded, and it bound the high numbers and the low exactly alike, which is a thing I ask you to notice, because nothing else in the valley did. You could not buy a turn. Buying a turn would not have got you one; a bought turn is a different thing than a turn, the way a bought friend is. The walking held only because it was no one's. It was the one level thing in a valley of heights.

The dry years came closer together. The petitions began in my father's time: the low heights charging that the Stone had been cut to the upper mill's convenience — the intake sits two hands above, snug as a proof — and praying the crown for a correction. I signed. My father signed. We were not fools; we were thirsty, and the daybook and its eleven weeks made our case, and after thirty years of praying, the crown, to everyone's astonishment, granted it. A new survey. A new datum, to be set fairly, in the sight of all parties, by commissioners with no lodging taken in anyone's mill. I was forty that summer, strong in the legs, known to be careful, and the commissioners hired me to carry the gauge-staff. I held the brass-shod staff on point after point up and down the valley for a whole season while they turned their level's eye on it, and I will swear to my death that no man among them cheated, and I watched, from under the staff, the whole undoing that an honest correction is.

Because the new line, too, had to be *put* somewhere. That is the part the petitions never imagined, in thirty years of imagining the old line gone. The commissioners gathered their season of readings, and then they sat in the assize room at Berrel — I stood at the wall, staff in my hand, no longer needed — and they chose. Fair-minded men, weighing the millers' pleaders against the orchards' pleaders against the crown's own intake charter, which the crown had no mind to see shortened. The best-pleaded interests moved the line by inches; the unpleaded — the walkers have no pleader; the walking was never a right, so it was never at the table — moved it not at all. And when the choosing was done, the masons cut the line into a new block of grey wacke forty paces upstream of the old one, and the crown's mark went over it, and the rolls were renumbered from it, every height in the valley re-hung from the new hook, and the valley, which had prayed thirty years for this day, watched the assessor seal the assize record and understood — some of us understood — that we had not touched the thing we came for. We had asked whether the line was in the right place. There is no right place. There is only, ever, whose hand cut it, and the whole art of the crown's mark is to heal over the hand by morning.

My family came out of the renumbering at eight hands. The upper mill came out at two, as before; its pleaders were very good. And the walking of the water did not survive the correction — that is the loss I actually mean this account to carry, the others being only money. The commissioners, honest men, had heard of the night-turns, and thinking to preserve them, wrote them into the new rolls: a schedule of hours, assigned by height, enforceable at the assize. You can read it there. It is fair, as written things go. And it is not the walking. A turn you are owed at your height is a right, and a right is a hook hung from the Stone like every other; the thing that bound twelve hands to two hands because it was no one's became, overnight, one more thing the numbers order. The old walkers' feet still know the rhythm. The rolls know the hours. These are not the same knowledge, and only one of them can be enforced, and the enforceable one drove out the other inside three dry years, as anything written drives out anything kept.

I walk anyway, some nights, dusk to dawn, opening nothing, an old woman on the channel paths. It is not defiance; nothing downstream changes; I am off the schedule and the sluices do not know me. I walk because my feet still hold the turn the rolls could not, and I am not willing to be the one who sets that down and lets it heal over. The new Stone sits in its bank, forty paces from the old, grey, marked, level with nothing but itself. The valley is already forgetting there were ever two. Give it a generation and the new line will not look like a thing that could be anywhere either. It will look like the bottom. There is no bottom. There is a hand, and a mark cut over the hand, and water, which was never at the table, moving in the dark past both stones exactly as it likes.
