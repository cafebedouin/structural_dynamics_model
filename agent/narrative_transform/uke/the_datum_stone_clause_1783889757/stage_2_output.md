# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION

## SECTION 0: INVARIANT CONTRACT

**The floor invariant (primary):** Beneath the whole apparatus of rights and rotations sits a founding choice that nobody chose and everybody obeys: a zero-point, treated as given, that was in fact set for someone's convenience. The system's instruments are *honest* — they measure truly — but they measure truly against a line that was never neutral to begin with. **Falsifier:** if the finished story implies a better-calibrated instrument, or a fairer surveyor, could fix the injustice, the floor invariant was lost.

**The subordinate grain (present, not primary):** There exists a shared, unwritten order of turns that belongs to no one and binds everyone equally — and the instant anyone writes it down, measures it, or claims it as a right, it stops being that thing and becomes property. **Falsifier:** if the story treats the unwritten order as simply an inferior, less-precise version of the written right — recoverable by better documentation — the grain was lost.

**Substrate — how the world holds both:** The floor is not a number that could be re-measured; it is a mark on the land itself, put there once, in a specific place, by a specific hand, for a specific and long-forgotten convenience — and every honest instrument in the valley now measures *from* that mark without any way to ask whether the mark was ever level. The grain is the older thing the mark was cut into: a practice of turns that ran without a mark at all, and cannot survive being given one.

**Substrate inhabitation sentence:** *You stand at the stone with the notch in it, and you know the notch is honest — the water truly rises to where the notch says it rises — and you also know that someone, generations gone, decided where to cut the notch, and that decision is the one thing in this valley that no instrument, however honest, can ever measure.*

**Break contract address:** The naturalization below leaves open a story that could end with "the notch recut" — a satisfying correction — but the substrate itself (a mark cut once by a hand now dead, into stone that predates every institution built atop it) forecloses any ending in which recutting the notch fixes the zero-point's *origin*. The notch can be moved. The fact that someone, once, for some reason, chose where to put it cannot be undone by moving it again. That foreclosure is structural, not authorial — downstream stages inherit a world where the deepest fix is unreachable by design, not by omission.

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

The Antler Concessions, high alpine glacial valleys of a mountain range never named in any language spoken by outsiders — call it, as the valley people do, **the Reckoning Country**. Three centuries ago, when the ice was thicker, a surveying order came through under royal charter to map grazing rights across the high pastures for tax purposes. The surveyors found no fences, no deeds — only an old, unwritten order among the herding clans: whoever's flock reached a spring first in a given season held it until the frost, then ceded it in rotation, spring by spring, generation by generation, by a logic no clan could fully explain to the surveyors and no surveyor thought worth learning. So the Crown's men did what surveying orders do. They picked a benchmark — a single stone shelf on the valley's western wall, chosen because it was visible from the road, not because it meant anything — and cut a notch into it at the elevation their transit level called "sea-level equivalent, zero." Every grazing right in the valley was thereafter defined by elevation above the Antler Notch: high pastures, drawing the coldest, latest snowmelt, the most contested water, were granted as permanent, heritable *high rights* to the clans already camped nearest the notch when the surveyors arrived. Low pastures, warmer, greener, but flood-prone and disease-ridden, were left as *unclaimed commons* — meaning: without institutional standing, without water-court representation, without appeal. The notch was never level. It was cut where the surveyor's tent happened to be pitched. Nobody living remembers this. Everybody living treats the notch as simply where "zero" is.

Three generations of herding families now live inside consequences of a rock notch. The high-rights clans consolidated into a chartered pastoral corporation — the **Antler Compact** — which controls water-court seats, drives up transit-license fees for low pasture access, and maintains a certified surveyor's office that re-validates the notch's elevation readings every spring with genuine, uncorrupted instruments. The low families still practice something like the old rotation among themselves — an unwritten schedule of who waters stock where and when, honored without documentation, understood by feel, by memory of whose grandmother watered where — but it has no standing in water-court, and every year the Compact's licensing office nibbles further into it, converting more of the old rotation into a fee schedule the low families can't afford to keep pace with.

### Constraint Reference Table

**C1 → The Antler Notch (the naturalized datum).** The benchmark itself: the belief, held by everyone, that "zero elevation" in this valley is simply a fact of the land rather than a mark cut by a bored surveyor's assistant sometime in 1710. Herder **Coyt Braddal** (X1) has spent his whole life farming a mid-slope pasture whose rights depend entirely on distance above the Notch, and has never once wondered who cut it or why there. His cousin **Iss Braddal** (X2), who left to clerk for a water-court advocate in the lowland capital and learned to read survey plats, has started to notice how convenient the Notch's placement was for the families camped closest to it in 1710 — and how that convenience has calcified into three centuries of "natural" right.

**C2 → The High-Rights Ledger (height-based rights).** The Compact's water-court system, which allocates spring access strictly by recorded elevation above the Notch. Low-pasture herder **Meret Sanne** (X3) experiences this as a wall she cannot climb: her family's rights sit forty feet below the cutoff the court recognizes, and no court date, no petition, no argument moves the line — it is simply read off the certified survey. **Warden Ossit Fenn** (X4), who runs the Compact's licensing office, experiences the same ledger as an elegant, self-executing coordination device — it settles disputes the old rotation used to settle through slow, costly negotiation, and he genuinely believes he is running a fairer system than the one it replaced. Iss (X2), reading the plats professionally, sees what Meret feels and what Ossit denies: the ledger's elegance *is* the extraction — its fairness is the fairness of an honest scale set on a tilted floor.

**C3 → The Rotation Charter (destructive codification).** The Compact's recent initiative — spearheaded by Ossit's superior, Chief Warden **Dael Korrin** (X6) — to formally document the low families' remaining unwritten turn-schedule into a filed, licensable rotation, replacing memory with paperwork "for everyone's protection." Low-pasture elder **Sarn Vey** (X5) watches the thing that kept her family fed for four generations — an order that required no fee, no filing, no court, because it belonged to no single herder and answered to no single authority — become, on paper, just another asset the Compact can price, revoke, and reassign. Dael genuinely believes the charter protects the low families by giving their rotation legal weight; he does not, or cannot, ask whether the rotation needed his protection, or whether writing it down is the precise act that kills it.

**C4 (deferred) → The Turn-Feel.** The old rotation itself, in its unwritten form — referenced only as memory, as the thing Sarn Vey still keeps in her body and will not describe to Compact clerks, because describing it is how you lose it.

**C5 (deferred) → Riding to the Capital.** The low families' recourse, in theory, to petition the distant Crown court above the Compact's water-court — a path that exists on paper and has never once, in living memory, been successfully walked.

**C6 (deferred) → No Voice Without a Deed.** The precondition, understood by everyone and stated by no one, that only a rights-holder may speak in water-court at all — so the families with no rights have no forum in which to say the Notch was ever anything but level.

### Characters as Positions

- **Coyt Braddal (X1)** — mid-slope herder, high-rights family, three generations removed from the surveyor's tent. Owns a stone house near the Notch itself. Worries about frost dates and lambing, not elevation politics — the Notch is simply where the world's zero is, the way the sun is where it rises.
- **Iss Braddal (X2)** — Coyt's cousin, water-court clerk's assistant in the lowland capital. Reads plats for a living. Owns nothing in the valley but knowledge of how the valley's paper works.
- **Meret Sanne (X3)** — low-pasture herder, forty vertical feet below the cutoff. Walks two extra hours daily to a spring the Ledger says isn't hers. Owns her flock and a leased low hut; nothing else is hers to leave to her children.
- **Warden Ossit Fenn (X4)** — Compact licensing officer. Administers the Ledger daily, genuinely believes it is fairer than the chaos it replaced, has never walked to a spring at 4 a.m. in his life.
- **Sarn Vey (X5)** — low-pasture elder, keeper of the unwritten turn-schedule in memory alone. The last person in her line who can recite the whole rotation without a document.
- **Chief Warden Dael Korrin (X6)** — architect of the Rotation Charter, believes documentation is protection, has never asked the families whether they wanted protecting.

### Linguistic Strategy: Direct Translation + Naming

Base register: formal Compact/court English for anything touching the Ledger, licensing, or the Charter — clipped, procedural, faintly legal ("the elevation stands as certified," "the rotation is now filed under license"). Against this, the low families carry a private vocabulary with no direct court equivalent:

- ***the turn-feel*** — the bodily, non-transferable knowledge of whose water it is this week; untranslatable into a filed schedule without becoming something else.
- ***notch-blind*** — said of someone (usually high-rights) who cannot see that the Notch was a choice at all. "Coyt's not cruel. He's notch-blind."
- ***cutting the water*** — what happens when an unwritten turn gets written down; carries the same dread register as "selling a name."
- ***the tent-line*** — low families' term for the arbitrary fact that rights depend on where the surveyor happened to camp, used bitterly, e.g., "We're not low because our people were lazy. We're low because of the tent-line."

Naming: high-rights families carry three-generation-consolidated compound surnames (Braddal, Fenn, Korrin) reflecting old land-grant lineages recorded in the Ledger itself — the name *is* a documented claim. Low-pasture families carry single, older clan names (Sanne, Vey) that predate the Ledger and were never re-registered, because registering cost money and standing they didn't have. The asymmetry in naming *is* the asymmetry in the system — legible in the roll call of any water-court session before a word of testimony is spoken.

**Iss (X2):** *You left the valley to learn how paper works, and what you learned is that the paper is honest and the honesty is the trap.*

**Meret (X3):** *You walk two hours past a spring the law says is not yours, carrying water that is heavier every year than the law that keeps it from you.*

**Ossit (X4):** *You administer a ledger you believe is fairer than chaos, and you have never once carried a bucket far enough to learn what the ledger costs someone else.*

**Dael (X6):** *You believe you are handing the old families a shield, and you cannot see that a shield with your name filed on it is a leash.*
