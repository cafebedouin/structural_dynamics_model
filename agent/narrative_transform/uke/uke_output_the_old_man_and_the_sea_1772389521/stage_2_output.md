# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT

### Setting: Barra de Navidad, Jalisco Coast — 1952

The village sits where the lagoon meets the Pacific, a crescent of weathered boats and salt-crusted nets. Forty families make their living from the water. The Americans come in winter—sportfishermen with deep pockets and deeper boats, paying top dollar for marlin and sailfish mounted on their walls back in California. The locals work the inshore waters: snapper, grouper, the steady fish that feed families but won't make anyone rich.

The fishermen's cooperative sets prices, allocates selling rights at the tourist dock. If you're in good standing, you can sell direct to the hotels. If not, you sell to the middlemen at half-price. Good standing means consistent catches. Three months of empty nets and the cooperative votes. Not a formal expulsion—just a quiet redistribution of dock privileges. "For the good of everyone," they say. "The tourists want to see full nets. Bad luck is contagious."

The village talks. In the plaza after Mass, at the cantina, in the market where women sell the day's catch. Reputation moves like weather—you feel it shift before you can name what changed. A fisherman who comes back empty too many times becomes "unlucky." Not cursed, exactly. Just... marked. The younger men stop asking his advice. Boat owners stop offering partnerships. Parents mention, casually, that their sons should crew elsewhere. No one's cruel about it. They'll still nod in the street, share a drink. But business is business, and luck is luck, and a man who can't catch fish shouldn't be teaching boys to fish.

The old fisherman lives in a shack at the edge of the village where the beach becomes rocks. Eighty-four days now. The boy used to crew for him—learned everything from him, really. Could read the water like text, knew where the fish would be before they got there. But the boy's father runs a successful boat, brings in steady catches for the cooperative. After forty days of the old man's bad streak, the father made his decision. Not anger—concern. "My son needs to learn the trade properly. Needs to work with someone who can still put fish in the boat."

The boy is fourteen. Old enough to crew, young enough to still be under his father's authority completely. In this village, a son's labor belongs to his father until marriage or death. That's not law—it's older than law. It's how families survive. The father decides which boat, which catches, which future. The boy can argue, but defiance costs. Not just punishment—it costs the father's reputation, the family's standing, the mother's place among the other women. A disrespectful son shames the whole household.

So the boy fishes with his father now. But he brings the old man food when he can, sneaks away to help with repairs, sits with him in the evening talking about the sea. The father doesn't forbid it—that would acknowledge the old man matters. He just... expects the boy at dawn, expects full days on the boat, expects obedience. The boy navigates the space between love and duty, between what he owes his father and what he owes the man who taught him everything his father didn't.

### The Characters

**The old fisherman** has hands like coral and a back that remembers every storm of forty years. He fishes alone now from a skiff barely seaworthy, goes farther out than safe because the inshore waters are crowded and the good spots belong to men with better reputations. He knows he's good—maybe the best who ever worked these waters. But knowing doesn't matter when the cooperative has already decided, when the village has already labeled him, when even the boy's time with him is stolen hours and guilty glances.

He speaks the formal Spanish of an earlier generation, the kind learned in church and school. But with the boy, sometimes, he slips into something older—words his grandmother used, phrases that don't quite translate, a way of talking about the sea that treats it like family rather than resource. The village has mostly lost this language. It sounds archaic now, poetic but impractical. The cooperative does business in modern terms: quotas, prices, market efficiency.

**The boy** speaks the contemporary Spanish of the coast—quick, practical, mixed with English words from the American tourists. "Tackle," "charter," "tip." With his father and the other fishermen, he uses the language of business and respect: formal address, careful deference, nothing that could be called back-talk. With the old man, he relaxes into something warmer. Not the archaic phrases—he doesn't quite know those—but a gentleness, a way of speaking that acknowledges love without naming it.

He's caught between worlds. His father's world: successful boats, cooperative membership, a future as a respected fisherman with his own crew someday. The old man's world: reading the water like poetry, fishing as relationship rather than extraction, dignity that doesn't depend on the cooperative's approval. The boy knows which world feeds him. He's starting to suspect which world he'll remember on his deathbed.

**The father** runs a tight boat and a tighter household. Not cruel—respected. He genuinely believes the cooperative system works because from where he stands, it does. Good fishermen get good spots, bad luck gets managed out, everyone benefits from the stability. He pulled his son from the old man's boat not from malice but from logic: why would you let your son learn from failure? The old man had his time. Now he's a cautionary tale, and the boy needs to learn from success.

When he talks about the old man, he uses the language of risk management: "unreliable," "past his prime," "unfortunate but necessary." Not vindictive—practical. He doesn't see himself as enforcing anything. He's just making rational decisions about his son's labor allocation, about family resources, about the future. If that happens to align with the cooperative's interests, well, that's because the system works.

### The Physical World

The old man's skiff: twelve feet of scarred wood, patched canvas sail, oarlocks worn smooth by decades. The newer boats have outboards now, fiberglass hulls, radio contact with shore. His boat is a relic. But it's paid for, and it's his, and it goes where he tells it to go.

The water close to shore: crowded with the cooperative boats, the good spots already claimed by dawn. The water far out: deeper blue, stronger current, bigger fish, bigger danger. The old man goes far because he has to, because the inshore water belongs to men with better standing, because proving himself means catching something the others can't.

The village itself: narrow streets, bright paint fading in salt air, nets drying on every available surface. The cooperative building is the newest structure—concrete block, tin roof, the Mexican flag and the cooperative banner flying side by side. Inside: a chalkboard with the week's catches, names and numbers in careful columns. The old man's name still there, but the numbers next to it tell the story: 0, 0, 0, 0.

The tourist dock: Americans in clean clothes pointing at fish they didn't catch, paying prices that make the locals wince and grin simultaneously. The cooperative members sell direct here, get top dollar, shake hands with sunburned men who call them "amigo" and tip in American dollars. The old man sells to the middleman in the market, gets half-price, watches the good fish go to the tourist dock in someone else's basket.

### The Linguistic Landscape

The village operates in layers of Spanish. The cooperative conducts business in formal modern Spanish—the language of contracts and efficiency and progress. The fishermen among themselves use coastal slang, technical terms for wind and current and fish behavior, mixed with the English words the tourists brought: "marlin," "charter," "sport-fishing."

The old man carries an older register. Not archaic enough to sound foreign, but formal enough to mark him as from a different time. He uses subjunctive mood where others use indicative, says "the sea gives" where others say "we catch," refers to the marlin as "el pez" (the fish) with a weight of respect that makes it sound like a title. When he talks to the boy about fishing, he slips into his grandmother's phrases: "el mar tiene memoria" (the sea has memory), "los peces saben" (the fish know), constructions that treat the ocean as animate, aware, reciprocal.

The boy understands this language but doesn't fully speak it. He can follow the old man's meaning, but when he responds, it's in the contemporary register—he doesn't have the old words, or he's embarrassed to use them, or he's already learning that this way of talking marks you as obsolete. The gap between their registers is small but growing. Each conversation is the old man teaching a language the village is forgetting.

### The Inhabitation Sentences

**The old fisherman:** You are a man who knows his skill is real, watching the village decide it isn't, unable to prove yourself without catching a fish, unable to catch a fish because the village has already decided you can't.

**The boy:** You are the space between the man who taught you to see the ocean and the father who owns your labor, and every hour you spend with one is an hour stolen from the other, and you love them both, and the village's judgment makes this love impossible to honor.

**The father:** You are a man who pulled his son from a failing teacher and placed him on a successful boat, and this was the obvious right decision, and you don't understand why the boy looks at you sometimes like you've done something cruel.

**Ω_E01:** Historical accuracy of Mexican fishing cooperatives in 1952  
→ Resolution: Ejidal fishing cooperatives were established in Mexico in the 1930s-40s, particularly on the Pacific coast. Barra de Navidad had active fishing community in this period. Cooperative structure (membership, dock privileges, informal reputation systems) is historically plausible.  
→ Impact: Naturalizes C2 (unluck stigma) through real institutional structure rather than pure social pressure.

**Ω_E02:** Linguistic registers in 1950s coastal Jalisco  
→ Resolution: Formal Spanish from earlier education systems would coexist with coastal slang and emerging Anglicisms from tourism. Older generation would retain more formal registers and indigenous-influenced constructions. Generational language shift is documented pattern.  
→ Impact: Language becomes the texture of power differential and temporal displacement, not just decoration.

**Ω_C03:** What makes a "good catch" in this economy?  
→ Resolution: Two markets: (1) Local/subsistence: steady smaller fish, measured in family meals and local sales. (2) Tourist/sport: large game fish (marlin, sailfish), measured in American dollars and mounted trophies. Cooperative privileges access to (2), which is 3-5x more lucrative.  
→ Impact: Clarifies stakes of C2 (stigma). Being "unlucky" doesn't mean starvation—means exclusion from the profitable market, which over time degrades to subsistence crisis.

**Ω_C04:** How does "unluck" stigma function mechanically?  
→ Resolution: Not supernatural belief, but risk management heuristic wrapped in cultural language. Tourists want to see full nets (social proof), cooperative wants reliable members (predictable supply), families want sons learning from success (rational skill acquisition). "Unluck" is the village's word for statistical variance they can't afford to absorb.  
→ Impact: Makes C2 genuinely Rope from powerful indices (coordination function is real) and genuinely Snare from powerless indices (extraction is real). Both true simultaneously.

**Ω_P05:** Tone: Hemingway homage or independent voice?  
→ Resolution: Independent voice. Hemingway's style (sparse, stoic, iceberg theory) is one approach to this material. This setting needs its own texture: more linguistically layered, more explicitly about power and institutions, more willing to name what Hemingway left submerged. Respectful divergence rather than imitation.  
→ Impact: Frees Stage 4 to write in the voice this setting demands rather than ventriloquizing Hemingway.

**Ω_C06:** Why doesn't the old man organize collective action (TR2)?  
→ Resolution: Multiple barriers naturalized by setting: (1) Pride/individualism (cultural), (2) Age (lacks energy for organizing), (3) Isolation (already excluded from cooperative), (4) Immediate time horizon (needs catch NOW, can't wait for long-term organizing), (5) Historical context (1952 Mexico, cooperative structure already established and powerful—challenging it means challenging ejidal system itself).  
→ Impact: Makes Type I error (False Mountain) tragic but rational from his index, not just character flaw.

**Ω_E07:** Physical plausibility of solo marlin fishing from 12-foot skiff  
→ Resolution: Extremely difficult but not impossible. Historically documented. Requires exceptional skill, luck, and physical endurance. High risk of boat damage, injury, loss of fish to sharks (common). This is why it's desperate—only someone with no other options would attempt it.  
→ Impact: Naturalizes C1 (physical contest) as genuine Mountain. The difficulty is structural, not just personal limitation.

**Ω_C08:** What does the boy's "duty" actually consist of?  
→ Resolution: (1) Labor: full-time crewing on father's boat, (2) Obedience: no back-talk, deference in public, (3) Reputation management: son's behavior reflects on father's standing, (4) Future obligation: expectation of care in father's old age. This is C3's coordination function—genuine reciprocity across generations.  
→ Impact: Makes C3 genuinely Tangled Rope. The duty isn't just extraction—it's how families survive without social safety nets. Both coordination AND extraction are irreducible.

**Ω_P09:** How much of the cooperative's internal mechanics to show?  
→ Resolution: Minimal direct exposition. Show effects (who sells where, who gets what price, who's invited to meetings) rather than rules. Let reader infer structure from consequences. Cooperative should feel like weather—omnipresent, powerful, but not requiring explicit explanation.  
→ Impact: Keeps framework invisible while making power structure viscerally clear.

**Ω_C10:** Does the village actually believe in supernatural "unluck"?  
→ Resolution: Layered. Older generation (old man's age): residual folk belief mixed with Catholic fatalism. Middle generation (father's age): pragmatic risk management dressed in traditional language. Younger generation (boy's age): mostly pragmatic, but the language persists. "Unluck" is the word everyone uses, but it means different things to different speakers.  
→ Impact: Adds texture to indexical variance. Same word, different ontological commitments, all coexisting.
