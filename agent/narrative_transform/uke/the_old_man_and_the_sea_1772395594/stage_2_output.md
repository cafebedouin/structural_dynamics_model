# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

**2089 CE, Orbital Manufacturing Zone 7 (OMZ-7), Low Earth Orbit**

The station floats 380 kilometers above Earth's surface, a cylindrical warren of fabrication bays and living quarters built by the Sino-European Industrial Consortium in 2067. Three generations have lived here since launch. The original crew came as specialists—precision welders, materials engineers, quality inspectors. Their children were born in microgravity, their bodies adapting to conditions that make planetary return impossible. Their grandchildren have never known weight.

OMZ-7 produces components for deep-space vessels: hull segments, life support modules, propulsion assemblies. The work requires absolute precision in an environment where a single structural flaw can kill hundreds. Quality standards are enforced through peer review, not management oversight. The fabricators police themselves.

The station operates on a reputation economy. Fabricators are rated by their peers after each shift: precision, efficiency, safety consciousness. High ratings attract apprentices, secure premium assignments, earn better living quarters. Low ratings compound. Once labeled *jinxed*—a fabricator whose modules consistently fail inspection, whose welds crack under stress testing—the designation becomes self-fulfilling. Other fabricators refuse collaboration. Apprentices are reassigned. The jinxed work alone on low-priority tasks until they burn out or transfer to waste reclamation.

The station's social structure is rigid but not formalized. Families control apprenticeship assignments. Parents decide which master fabricator their children will train under, prioritizing safety and career advancement over personal preference. A child might spend years learning from someone they've never met while the fabricator they grew up alongside works three bays away. This is understood as parental duty—protecting children from poor choices, ensuring family economic security.

The original crew is aging. They remember Earth gravity, planetary horizons, weather. They fabricate with techniques learned dirtside, compensating for microgravity through practiced discipline. The second generation relies on station-developed methods and computational aids. The third generation has never worked any other way.

### Linguistic Strategy: Code-Switching (Station Cant / Institutional English)

**Station Cant:** Evolved from Mandarin, Russian, and technical English, spoken among fabricators during work and in living quarters. Marks intimacy, solidarity, shop-floor identity. Rich in metaphors from orbital mechanics and material stress.

**Institutional English:** Used for official communications, quality reports, family negotiations. Signals formal relationships, hierarchical distance, performance of proper conduct.

**Code-switching signals:**
- **Cant → English:** Shift from peer to authority, personal to institutional
- **English → Cant:** Dropping formality, acknowledging solidarity
- **Sustained English with family:** Maintaining emotional distance, enforcing hierarchy
- **Sustained Cant with strangers:** Claiming kinship, resisting institutional categorization

**Examples:**
- *Cant:* "Wei de gongzuo hen wen" (His work is stable/warm—metaphor from thermal equilibrium)
- *English:* "The fabricator's quality metrics meet minimum standards"
- *Mixing:* "I filed the paperwork, but *xinli hen tong*" (heart hurts—what can't be said in English)

### Constraint Naturalizations

**The Fabricator's Discipline**

What it is: The precision protocols that govern module construction. Every weld must be within 0.02mm tolerance. Every seal must hold 3 atmospheres pressure differential. Every structural joint must survive 10,000 thermal cycles. These standards emerged from early disasters—explosive decompressions, cascade failures, deaths that could have been prevented by better craftsmanship.

Why these properties: The protocols are self-imposed. No supervisor checks every weld. The station's survival depends on fabricators maintaining discipline when no one is watching. For the old guard, this discipline is identity—proof they can be trusted with lives, that they belong in orbit. For younger fabricators with computational aids and automated testing, the protocols are efficiency measures. Both maintain them, but the meaning differs.

What locals call it: *Gongyi* (工艺)—craftsmanship, but carrying connotations of spiritual discipline and professional honor. The old guard say *gongyi* with weight. Younger fabricators say it casually, like "workflow."

Example in use: 
> **Yao** (老药, "Old Yao," 67, original crew): Checks a weld joint by hand, running his fingers along the seam in the dim light of Bay 12. His apprentice left three months ago, reassigned to a fabricator with better ratings. He works alone now, but the discipline remains. *"Gongyi bu shi guize,"* he mutters in Cant. *"Shi ni de ming."* (Craftsmanship isn't rules. It's your life.) He files the quality report in English: "Module 7-449-B meets structural specifications."

**The Reputation System**

What it is: The peer-rating network that determines a fabricator's economic and social position. After each shift, fabricators rate their collaborators on precision, safety, and reliability. Ratings accumulate into reputation scores displayed on every workstation. High scores attract apprentices and premium work. Low scores trigger a cascade: fewer collaboration opportunities, which means more solo work, which means higher error rates (no peer review), which means lower scores. Once labeled *jinxed* (凶手, xiōngshǒu—literally "ominous hand"), the label sticks. Other fabricators avoid you. Parents pull their children from your apprenticeship. You're not formally expelled—just isolated until you quit.

Why these properties: The system evolved to allocate risk. In an environment where mistakes kill, fabricators need to know who's reliable. Reputation aggregates distributed knowledge—who consistently produces quality work, who cuts corners under pressure. But the system is also self-reinforcing. Bad luck (a material defect, equipment malfunction) gets coded as incompetence. The powerless can't escape the label. Those with mobility—family connections, alternative skills—can transfer to other stations or negotiate reassignment.

What locals call it: *Mingyu* (名誉, reputation), but the degraded state is *xiōngshǒu* (jinxed). The term carries superstitious weight even among those who know it's social construction. "His work is *xiōngshǒu*" means both "statistically unreliable" and "cursed by bad fortune."

Example in use:
> **Chen** (陈明, 19, third generation): Sits in the family quarters, listening to his parents argue in English—the language of decisions, not feelings. His mother: "The statistics are clear. Yao's modules fail inspection at twice the rate of other senior fabricators. Moving Chen protects his career." His father nods. Chen wants to say *"Yao shi wo de shifu"* (Yao is my master—Cant, intimacy), but he responds in English: "I understand the reasoning." Later, in Bay 12, he tells Yao in Cant: *"Baba mama yao wo zou"* (Parents want me to leave). Yao nods. *"Ta men dui."* (They're right.) Chen hears the resignation—not agreement, but acceptance that the system works this way.

**Filial Duty**

What it is: The obligation structure governing parent-child relationships on the station. Parents control major life decisions—apprenticeship assignments, living quarter allocations, transfer requests—until children achieve economic independence (typically mid-20s). This isn't legal authority but social expectation backed by material dependence. Children live in family quarters, eat from family rations, access station resources through family allocation. Disobedience means conflict, not exile, but the cost is high: emotional rupture, social disapproval, possible loss of family support during emergencies.

Why these properties: The structure provides economic security and risk management. Families pool resources, share quarters, support members during illness or injury. In exchange, children defer to parental judgment on career decisions. Parents are understood to have better information (longer time horizons, broader networks) and stronger incentive to protect children's futures. But the system also restricts agency. A child might see their parents' decision as wrong—based on outdated assumptions, excessive risk aversion, misunderstanding of the child's capacities—but cannot easily refuse without breaking family cohesion.

What locals call it: *Xiao* (孝)—filial piety, but on the station it means practical obligation more than Confucian virtue. When parents invoke *xiao*, they're claiming authority. When children accept it, they're acknowledging dependence.

Example in use:
> **Chen's parents** (speaking English, the language of authority): "You will transfer to Fabricator Liu's apprenticeship. His ratings are stable. His modules pass inspection consistently. This is not negotiable." Chen feels the weight—not cruelty, but care expressed through control. His parents genuinely believe Liu is safer. They're protecting him from Yao's declining reputation. But Chen knows Yao's work is sound. The ratings reflect bad luck and systemic bias, not incompetence. He wants to argue in Cant—*"Ni bu dong"* (You don't understand)—but that would be disrespect. So he says in English: "I'll submit the transfer request tomorrow." The sentence is compliance, but his heart is with Yao.

### Character Positions and Inhabitation Sentences

**Yao (老药, "Old Yao")** — Original crew, 67, master fabricator
- **Power:** Powerless (π = 1.5) — ratings collapsed, no apprentice, isolated
- **Time:** Biographical — sees his lifetime's work, legacy at stake
- **Exit:** Trapped — too old for planetary return, body adapted to microgravity, no alternative skills
- **Scope:** Local — face-to-face relationships, Bay 12 community

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are a craftsman in a system that measures worth by numbers on a screen, and the only language that is yours is the one you speak to the boy you taught, who is being taken from you because the system says you are unlucky.*

**Chen (陈明)** — Third generation, 19, apprentice fabricator
- **Power:** Powerless (π = 1.5) — economically dependent, no authority over own assignments
- **Time:** Biographical — sees career ahead, wants to choose his path
- **Exit:** Trapped — born in orbit, body cannot survive planetary gravity, family controls resources
- **Scope:** Local — knows Yao personally, sees his work quality directly

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are a child in a system that forces you to choose between the person who taught you everything and the family that keeps you alive, and the only rebellion available is silence.*

**Chen's Parents** — Second generation, 40s, moderate fabricators
- **Power:** Moderate (π = 1.0) — stable ratings, family resources, can negotiate
- **Time:** Biographical — planning son's career, protecting family security
- **Exit:** Constrained — could transfer stations but at high cost, have alternatives but limited
- **Scope:** Local — know Yao's reputation through ratings and gossip, not direct observation

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are parents in a system that works if you follow its logic, and protecting your son means moving him away from a man whose numbers say he's dangerous, even though your heart says otherwise.*

**Liu (刘师傅, "Master Liu")** — Second generation, 45, senior fabricator with high ratings
- **Power:** Moderate to Powerful (π = 0.6-1.0) — high ratings, multiple apprentices, influence
- **Time:** Biographical — building reputation, securing position
- **Exit:** Mobile — could transfer to other stations, has marketable skills, options available
- **Scope:** Local to Regional — known across multiple bays, reputation extends beyond immediate community

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are a fabricator in a system that rewards you for being good at your job, and when parents ask you to take their son from a failing master, you say yes because that's how the system is supposed to work.*

### Power Differential Through Material Conditions

**Yao's position (powerless, trapped):**
- **Physical space:** Bay 12 is older, dimmer. Lighting panels flicker. The air recyclers hum louder here. His workstation is cramped—equipment from 2067, no upgrades. He works alone, so no one complains about the noise or the cold spots where insulation has degraded.
- **Daily rhythm:** Survival mode. He takes low-priority assignments no one else wants. Checks every weld three times because he has no peer review. Files reports in English to invisible administrators who never respond. Eats alone in the corner of the mess hall.
- **Material possessions:** His quarters are smallest allocation—2.5 cubic meters. One hammock, one storage locker. Tools he brought from Earth, older than most fabricators on the station. A photo of his daughter (transferred to Mars, hasn't spoken to him in four years).
- **Social interactions:** People avoid eye contact. When he enters a bay, conversation stops. Chen used to greet him in Cant—*"Shifu zao"* (Master, good morning)—but now there's only silence.

**Chen's position (powerless but loved):**
- **Physical space:** Family quarters—8 cubic meters shared with parents. Crowded but warm. His hammock is strung between storage lockers. He can hear his parents breathing at night.
- **Daily rhythm:** Structured by family. Wakes when parents wake. Eats together. Works in Bay 12 with Yao, then returns for family meal. Homework (advanced materials science) in the common area. Parents review his progress.
- **Material possessions:** Shares everything. Clothes from family allocation. Tools borrowed from Yao. One personal item: a jade pendant his grandmother gave him before she died, carved on Earth.
- **Social interactions:** Defers to parents in English. Laughs with Yao in Cant. Caught between languages, between loyalties. When parents speak, he listens. When Yao teaches, he learns. The transfer order forces him to choose.

**Parents' position (moderate, constrained):**
- **Physical space:** Family quarters are adequate—not spacious but functional. They've lived here fifteen years. Photos on the walls (Chen as a baby, family gatherings). The space feels like home.
- **Daily rhythm:** Strategic planning. Morning shift in Bay 6 (both are fabricators). Lunch review of Chen's progress. Evening family time. They discuss Chen's future in English—formal, careful. They discuss daily life in Cant—warm, intimate.
- **Material possessions:** Accumulated over two decades. Better tools, newer equipment. Savings in station credit. Enough buffer to handle emergencies. They could transfer stations if necessary, though it would cost.
- **Social interactions:** Speak to other parents as equals. Negotiate apprenticeships. Make decisions as a unit. When they tell Chen to transfer, it's not cruelty—it's the calculus of people who've navigated the system successfully and want their son to do the same.

**Liu's position (powerful, mobile):**
- **Physical space:** Bay 4, newest section. Bright lighting, quiet air recyclers, climate-controlled. His workstation has the latest fabrication equipment. Three apprentices work alongside him—peer review is constant, easy.
- **Daily rhythm:** Strategic execution. Takes premium assignments. Trains apprentices efficiently. Files reports that administrators actually read. Eats in the senior fabricators' section of the mess hall. People seek his advice.
- **Material possessions:** Larger quarters (12 cubic meters). Newer tools. Station credit surplus. He could transfer to OMZ-3 or OMZ-9 if he wanted. Has options.
- **Social interactions:** People greet him first. Parents approach him to request apprenticeships. He has leverage—can accept or decline. When he agrees to take Chen, it's a favor to the parents, an investment in his own reputation (training successful apprentices boosts ratings), and barely a thought about Yao.

## SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

### RESOLVED

**Ω_E01: What is the station's population and demographic structure?**
- **Resolution:** ~2,400 people. Original crew (60+): ~300. Second generation (25-50): ~1,100. Third generation (0-25): ~1,000. Demographic pyramid inverted—third generation largest, but many have bodies incompatible with planetary gravity.
- **Impact:** Establishes generational tension. Original crew remembers Earth, maintains old disciplines. Third generation knows only orbit, adapts to new methods. Chen is third generation—has never known weight, cannot leave even if he wanted to.

**Ω_E02: How does the reputation system technically operate?**
- **Resolution:** Peer ratings submitted via workstation interface after each shift. Algorithm aggregates scores, weights recent performance more heavily, displays running average. Scores visible to all fabricators. No appeals process—ratings are "objective" aggregation of peer judgment.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes the constraint. System appears neutral (just math), but it encodes social biases. Bad luck gets coded as incompetence. No mechanism to distinguish material defect from fabricator error.

**Ω_E03: Why can't Yao transfer to another station?**
- **Resolution:** Age 67, body fully adapted to microgravity (bone density loss, muscle atrophy, cardiovascular changes). Planetary return would be fatal. Transfer to other orbital stations possible in theory, but reputation scores are networked—his *xiōngshǒu* label would follow him. Other stations would see his ratings and refuse transfer.
- **Impact:** Establishes E = trapped. Yao has no exit. The constraint is inescapable from his position.

**Ω_E04: What specific technical work do fabricators do?**
- **Resolution:** Precision welding of hull segments (titanium-aluminum alloys), assembly of life support modules (atmospheric processors, water recyclers), installation of propulsion components (thruster assemblies, fuel line connections). Work requires microgravity-specific techniques—managing momentum, preventing contamination, ensuring structural integrity in vacuum.
- **Impact:** Grounds the discipline constraint. The work genuinely requires precision. A bad weld kills people. The stakes are real, not manufactured.

**Ω_C01: How much Mandarin vs. English in the Cant?**
- **Resolution:** Cant is ~40% Mandarin-derived, ~30% Russian-derived, ~20% technical English, ~10% neologisms. Grammar simplified—no tones (Mandarin), no cases (Russian), heavy code-mixing. English remains prestige language for formal contexts.
- **Impact:** Creates linguistic texture. Characters can express things in Cant they can't in English. Code-switching signals relationship shifts. Yao and Chen speak Cant (intimacy). Chen and parents speak English (hierarchy).

**Ω_C02: What does "jinxed" (xiōngshǒu) mean structurally?**
- **Resolution:** Originally superstitious term (ominous hand, cursed touch). Now rationalized as statistical label—fabricator whose modules fail inspection above threshold rate. But the term retains superstitious weight. People avoid *xiōngshǒu* fabricators even when they know it's social construction.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes the Snare. The label is both "objective" (statistical) and social (self-fulfilling). Yao is trapped by a category that appears neutral but encodes bias.

**Ω_C03: What is the legal/formal structure of filial duty?**
- **Resolution:** No formal legal code. Station charter is silent on family structure. Filial duty is social expectation backed by material dependence. Parents control resource allocation (quarters, rations, equipment access) until children achieve economic independence (typically mid-20s when they become full fabricators).
- **Impact:** Establishes constraint as Tangled Rope, not Snare. It's not coercive law—it's reciprocal obligation with genuine benefits (family support) and genuine costs (restricted agency). Chen can resist, but at high price.

**Ω_P01: Why do parents speak English to Chen instead of Cant?**
- **Resolution:** English signals formality, authority, institutional role. When parents make decisions about Chen's future, they speak English to mark the interaction as hierarchical, not peer-to-peer. Cant would signal intimacy, negotiation. English signals: this is not negotiable.
- **Impact:** Linguistic choice naturalizes power differential. Chen hears English and knows he's being told, not asked. The language itself enforces the constraint.

**Ω_P02: What's the station's economic model?**
- **Resolution:** Fabricators paid in station credit (internal currency). Credit buys better quarters, food variety, equipment upgrades, transfer requests. Families pool credit. Reputation affects credit allocation—high ratings get premium assignments (higher pay), low ratings get low-priority work (subsistence pay).
- **Impact:** Materializes the reputation constraint. It's not just social disapproval—it's economic marginalization. Yao earns barely enough to maintain his quarters. He can't save, can't transfer, can't escape.

**Ω_P03: How does Chen's body differ from his parents'?**
- **Resolution:** Chen was born in microgravity. His bones are less dense (no gravitational stress), his cardiovascular system adapted to low-pressure environment, his vestibular system calibrated to three-dimensional movement. He could not survive Earth gravity without years of reconditioning (if at all). His parents were born on Earth, came to orbit as adults. They could return (painfully, with rehabilitation) if they wanted. Chen cannot.
- **Impact:** Establishes Chen's E = trapped at physical level. He's not just economically trapped—he's biologically trapped. The station is his only possible home.
