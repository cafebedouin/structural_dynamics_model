REVIEW ASSESSMENT

STRENGTHS:
The hull-read/grain motif earns its keep — it's introduced concretely in Section I (Ossin's palm against Fesk's boat), then transferred cleanly to Mereth's hand flat on the tribunal table and finally to her hand "flat against a hull it hasn't decided yet whether to trust" in the confession scene. The Charter's clause-logic ("it asks whether the order was given, and whether it was obeyed") is genuinely chilling institutional writing — a procedural mechanism that excludes the truth by design, not by malice. Katt's held-breath pause in testimony and her quiet confrontation in Section V is the story's best piece of characterization: restrained, specific, and it lands without anyone naming what it means.

BIGGEST WEAKNESS:
The ending resolves nothing and risks reading as inertia rather than earned ambiguity — Mereth and Ossin dive, surface, and "begin hauling the lines in for home" while Prace's quota review, Katt's fraying line, and Torvel's dry composure are simply left standing, unremarked, as a list. The story asserts unresolved threads (Section V's closing paragraph: "None of that changed, and Mereth stopped expecting it to") rather than dramatizing why that stasis is the right note to end on, which makes the close feel like a shrug dressed as theme.

INVARIANT FALSIFIER:
HOLDS — the story is careful never to let the tribunal's error read as a correctable mistake. Mereth's own account to Ossin ("Both. Neither... there's no report format on the Coast that holds both of those without one of us being the liar") explicitly forecloses a recoverable true value. The Charter clause is shown as structurally incapable of asking the right question, not merely badly staffed or badly judged — the elder's "no column on the form" line does the work of the floor invariant correctly.

BREAK:
From the text: I expected a leader's institutional loyalty to win out over personal debt — that Mereth or Torvel would ultimately side with the Guild's letter against Ossin, or that the tribunal scene would force a choice between rule and code. Instead the story sidesteps that binary entirely: nobody chooses institution over person, and nobody achieves psychological resolution either — Mereth ends with "I don't have a clean thing to hand you. I have that," which is neither victory nor institutional capitulation. This partially meets the target_prior (internal victory over external consequences) only by inverting it — the story explicitly refuses Mereth an internal victory, leaving the external mess intact and psychologically unresolved too. It's a break, but a harder-edged one than "internal victory matters more" — closer to "neither victory is available, and the story refuses to pretend otherwise." Meets the spirit of the target_prior's death but doesn't cleanly demonstrate the prior being replaced.

READINESS:
Close — the prose is controlled and the central invariant is intact, but the ending needs one more pass to earn its stasis rather than list it.
