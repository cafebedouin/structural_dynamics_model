# The Bridge at Gang Pinggir

The cart's handles fit Tan Ah Kow's palms the way his father's tools had—worn smooth in the right places, the wood darker where hands pressed. He'd oiled them last night, working the linseed in with his thumbs while his eldest son swept the workshop floor. The buffalo stood patient in her traces, her breath making small clouds in the morning air. The camphor-wood cabinet in the cart was his best work this month: dovetailed corners, brass hinges he'd bargained for at the Javanese metalsmith's stall, the wood rubbed until it caught light like water.

Tuesday. Market day. The harbor would be full by seven.

Tan checked the ropes holding the cabinet, tested each knot. His son appeared in the doorway, barefoot, still in his sleeping clothes.

"*Beh khì chhut-chhī?*" Going to market?

"Before the Dutch buyers arrive," Tan said. He switched to Malay, the language of instruction. "You'll open the workshop at eight. If Lim Ah Seng comes for his altar table, tell him Thursday."

His son nodded, solemn with the responsibility. Twelve years old, already learning to read the customers, to know which promises could bend and which couldn't.

Tan clicked his tongue. The buffalo leaned into her harness. The cart wheels creaked on the packed earth of the workshop yard, then found the smoother stone of Gang Pinggir.

The lane was already awake. Smoke rose from kitchen fires. Tan smelled ginger, shrimp paste, the burnt-sugar smell of someone making kaya. Mrs. Lim sat on her stool outside her provisions shop, grinding something in her stone mortar. Her movements stopped when she saw him.

She said something in Hokkien—too quiet to hear, but her face said enough. Tan kept walking. The buffalo's hooves made a steady rhythm on the cobblestones.

"Tan Ah Kow." Mrs. Lim's voice, louder now, in Malay so others could hear. "A man of property should ride, not walk beside his animal like a coolie."

The Tamil dockworker sitting against the wall looked up. He nodded, agreeing.

Tan stopped. The buffalo stopped. His hands were still on the cart handles, but the wood felt different now—less familiar, more aware of being held.

Mrs. Lim was watching. The dockworker was watching. Two children playing congkak on a doorstep had stopped their game to watch.

He climbed onto the cart, careful not to jostle the cabinet. The buffalo felt the weight shift and turned her head, one dark eye finding him. He'd never ridden the cart to market before. The buffalo didn't need him to—she knew the route, knew to stop at corners, knew to move aside for the Chinese medicine seller's wagon.

But Mrs. Lim was nodding now, satisfied. The dockworker had returned to his betel nut. The children went back to their game.

Tan sat on the edge of the cart, feet dangling, hands gripping the side boards. The buffalo started walking again. The cart swayed. From up here, he could see into windows, could see Mrs. Lim's face as they passed—approving now, proper order restored.

The lane curved toward Jalan Karet. Here the houses pressed closer, the morning shadows deeper. Tan's thighs were already aching from holding himself steady. The cabinet shifted slightly with each bump. He should get down, walk beside the cart, keep one hand on the cabinet to steady it.

But Mrs. Lim might still be watching. And they were nearly to Jalan Merak, nearly to the wider street that led to the harbor.

"*Ayyo*, what are you doing?"

Ong Ah Huat stood in front of his cousin's house, a basket of rattan strips over his shoulder. He was heading to his market stall, same as Tan, but Ong paid monthly fees for permanent space. He didn't have to arrive early.

"Going to market," Tan said.

"Riding like a *tuan besar* while your animal works?" Ong shook his head. "The Dutch see you like that, they think you're lazy. You think they buy furniture from a lazy man?"

The Tamil dockworker had followed them, was standing at the corner now. "He's right," the dockworker said in Malay. "Show you can work. Show respect for the animal."

Tan climbed down. His hands found the cart handles again—familiar, right. The buffalo kept walking. He pushed, helping her, showing he worked. The cabinet was safe with him walking. He could feel if it shifted.

Ong nodded and continued toward the harbor. The dockworker turned back toward Gang Pinggir.

Tan's hands were sweating now, though the morning was cool. The cart handles felt slick. He'd have to wash them when he got home, oil them again tonight.

Jalan Merak opened ahead—wider, busier. A Javanese woman balanced a basket of vegetables on her head. Two Chinese boys chased a hoop. A Malay man led a goat. Normal traffic, normal morning, but Tan felt the eyes. Everyone who saw him would have an opinion about whether he was doing this right.

The vegetable seller stopped. She was older, her face creased from years in the sun. She looked at the buffalo, then at Tan, then at the cart.

"Your son should lead the animal," she said in Malay. "Teaches him responsibility. You push the cart, he leads. This is how my husband taught our sons."

Tan's son was back at the workshop. The market would close at two. There wasn't time to go back.

"My son is watching the workshop," he said.

"*Hmm.*" The woman's face said what her mouth didn't: *Then you're doing it wrong.*

She walked on. Tan kept pushing. His palms were burning now where they gripped the handles. He'd developed calluses from plane work, from chisel work, but not from pushing. The cart was heavier than it should be. Or his arms were tired. Or something.

The bridge appeared ahead—Jembatan Mberok, the old wooden span over the canal. Twenty meters of uneven planks, the supports underneath green with algae. The construction noise from the new godown was louder here: *thunk-thunk-thunk*, rhythmic, metal on wood. Pile-driving. The Dutch were expanding the harbor, driving foundation posts into the soft ground.

The buffalo's ears flicked back. She slowed.

Tan pushed harder. They had to cross. The market was on the other side.

The buffalo stopped.

"*Lâi, lâi.*" Come on. Tan clicked his tongue, the sound his father had used. The buffalo didn't move.

A crowd was gathering. Morning traffic backed up behind them: two hand-carts, a bicycle, a woman with a child. On the bridge itself, people were crossing from the other direction. The pile-driving continued: *thunk-thunk-thunk*.

Under the bridge, dogs were fighting over something. Their snarls rose through the planks.

The buffalo's nostrils flared. Her head lowered.

"What's the problem?" A Chinese merchant Tan didn't know, well-dressed, probably heading to his shop in the harbor district.

"She won't cross," Tan said.

"Hit her. Show her who's master."

Tan had never hit the buffalo. She was patient, steady, worth six months of profit. You didn't hit something worth that much.

But the crowd was growing. The merchant was watching. The people on the bridge were watching. Someone behind him said something in Javanese—he didn't catch the words, but the tone was clear. *Move.*

Tan picked up a small stick from the side of the road. He tapped the buffalo's flank—not hard, just enough to show he was trying.

She shifted her weight but didn't move forward.

*Thunk-thunk-thunk.* The pile-driver echoed off the water.

"You're being too gentle," the merchant said. "Animal doesn't respect you."

A Javanese man pushing a hand-cart called out: "Take the harness off. Lead her across by hand. Sometimes they balk at the cart noise."

Tan's hands were shaking as he unbuckled the harness. The buffalo stood free, just her halter rope in his hand. He pulled. She pulled back.

"Tie something over her eyes," someone suggested. "She can't see, she won't be afraid."

"No, no," someone else said. "Let her see. She needs to see it's safe."

The pile-driver stopped. In the silence, Tan could hear the canal water lapping against the posts, the dogs still fighting underneath, his own breath coming too fast.

Then the pile-driver started again: *THUNK-THUNK-THUNK*, louder now, faster, a different rhythm.

The buffalo's head came up. Her eyes showed white at the edges.

"Just carry her across," someone said.

Tan turned. It was a young man, Chinese, someone's apprentice probably. "What?"

"Carry her. Get two men, tie her to a pole, carry her across like they do with pigs at slaughter. She's stubborn, you show her you're stronger."

The merchant laughed. "There's your answer."

But the Javanese man with the hand-cart was shaking his head. "That's not—animals panic if you—"

"You have a better idea?" The merchant's voice had an edge now. "We're all waiting. Market opens in an hour."

Tan looked at his hands. They were still shaking. He looked at the buffalo. She was trembling too, her whole body tense.

He looked at the cart. The cabinet inside, his best work, his father's legacy.

He looked at the crowd. Twenty people now, maybe more. All watching. All waiting. All with opinions about what a man should do with his own animal.

The Javanese vegetable seller from earlier pushed through. "Get your son. Bring him here. Young voice, the animal will—"

"He doesn't have time," the merchant said. "Market closes at two. You want him to lose a day's wages because his buffalo is stubborn?"

Mrs. Lim's voice came from somewhere in the crowd: "A man provides for his family. That's what matters."

Tan's son. Three more years of school if business stayed good. Five more years until he could take over the workshop. All of it depending on days like this, on sales like this, on getting the cabinet to market before the Dutch buyers left.

"I'll help you." The young apprentice was already looking for a suitable pole. "You get someone else. We'll carry her."

The Javanese man was still shaking his head, but quietly now, stepping back.

Tan found himself nodding. Found his hands untying the rope from the buffalo's halter, retying it to the pole the apprentice had found. Found another man—one of Ong's cousins—agreeing to help.

The buffalo's eye found his again. He'd seen that look before, years ago, when his father was dying. Recognition. Knowing something was wrong but not understanding what.

"We have to get her off her feet first," the apprentice said.

They pushed. The buffalo resisted, then stumbled, then was down on her side. Her legs thrashed. Tan's hands fumbled with the rope, trying to secure her legs to the pole. The rope was rough hemp, the kind used for ship cargo. It bit into his palms.

The buffalo made a sound he'd never heard from her—high, desperate.

"Lift!" the apprentice said.

They lifted. The pole bent but held. The buffalo's weight was terrible—not dead weight, but fighting weight, every muscle straining. Tan's hands were slipping. He gripped harder. The rope was cutting into his palms now, through the calluses, finding new skin.

They started across the bridge.

*Thunk-thunk-thunk.* The pile-driver.

The buffalo thrashed. The pole jerked. Tan's hands lost their grip.

Time became strange then—very slow and very fast at once. He saw the pole tilt. Saw the apprentice trying to adjust. Saw the buffalo's body twist. Saw the other man let go—a rational choice, save yourself—saw the apprentice let go—another rational choice.

Saw the buffalo fall.

The splash was smaller than he expected. The canal wasn't deep. But it was deep enough.

The buffalo's legs were still tied. She couldn't stand. She couldn't get her head up. The water was brown with silt, and he couldn't see her beneath it, could only see the water moving, thrashing, then moving less, then still.

Tan was on the bridge, looking down. His hands were bleeding. The cart was behind him, the cabinet still safe inside it. The crowd was silent.

Sergeant De Vries arrived then, his white uniform bright against the morning. "Wat is hier aan de hand?" What's going on here?

The merchant spoke in Dutch—too fast for Tan to follow. The sergeant nodded, made notes in a small book. He looked at Tan.

"Doorlopen!" Move along.

Tan understood the tone if not all the words. He understood that there was no appeal, no explanation that would matter. He understood that the sergeant saw an obstruction, not a tragedy.

His hands found the cart handles. They were sticky with blood. The buffalo's harness lay on the ground where he'd dropped it. Someone picked it up, handed it to him. He didn't see who.

He pulled the cart himself across the bridge. It was much heavier without the buffalo, or maybe his arms had no strength left. The cabinet shifted. He heard something crack inside it—not the wood, maybe the hinges, maybe one of the dovetails.

The market was open when he arrived. Ong Ah Huat was already at his stall, arranging rattan chairs. He looked at Tan, at the cart, at Tan's hands.

"Where's your buffalo?"

"In the canal."

Ong's face changed. "Dead?"

Tan nodded.

"*Aiyah.*" Ong looked away. "That's... I'm sorry. That's bad luck."

Luck. Tan wanted to laugh. Or scream. Or sit down and never stand up again.

He pulled the cart to an empty space near the harbor wall. He opened the cabinet's doors. The left hinge was cracked. One of the dovetail joints had separated slightly—not broken, but loose, visible to anyone who looked.

A Dutch man in a linen suit approached, looked at the cabinet, ran his hand over the wood. He found the loose joint. His finger probed it.

"How much?"

Tan's voice came from somewhere far away. "Twenty guilders."

The Dutchman shook his head. "Fifteen. The joint is loose."

Fifteen guilders. Half what the cabinet was worth. A quarter of what the buffalo had cost.

"Fifteen," Tan heard himself say.

The Dutchman paid. Tan helped him carry the cabinet to a waiting cart. When he came back, his cart was empty. Just the bloodstained handles and the empty space where everything had been.

He sat down on the harbor wall. His hands were still bleeding. He should wrap them. He should go home. He should tell his wife, tell his son.

He should have—

What? Not taken the buffalo to market? But he had to sell. Not listened to Mrs. Lim? But she was his neighbor, his customer's mother, someone whose good opinion mattered. Not listened to Ong? But Ong knew the Dutch buyers, knew what they expected. Not listened to the vegetable seller? But she was older, experienced, trying to help.

Not carried the buffalo across the bridge?

But everyone had agreed. Everyone had said it was the only way. Everyone had been waiting, watching, expecting him to do something.

He'd tried to do what everyone said. He'd tried to please them all. And now he had nothing. No buffalo. No cabinet worth selling. Fifteen guilders that wouldn't cover a month's rice, wouldn't replace what he'd lost, wouldn't undo what he'd done.

His hands were shaking again. He pressed them against the stone wall, trying to make them stop. But the stone was rough, and it hurt, and they kept shaking anyway.

The market noise continued around him—vendors calling prices, buyers bargaining, the normal morning sounds of people doing business, making their way, getting by. As if nothing had happened. As if a buffalo hadn't drowned. As if a man's hands weren't bleeding. As if everything was fine.

Tan sat there until the blood dried on his palms. Then he stood, gripped the cart handles—the wood sticky now, the familiar grip gone—and pulled the empty cart home.
