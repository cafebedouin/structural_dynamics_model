# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

**Semarang, Dutch East Indies, 1911**

The old port quarter sits between two worlds. To the north, the harbor where Javanese stevedores unload copra and rubber from the outer islands. To the south, the European district where Dutch administrators live in whitewashed villas with wide verandas. Between them: the Chinese quarter, three generations settled, speaking a hybrid of Hokkien, Malay, and market Dutch.

Tan Ah Kow runs a small furniture workshop on Gang Pinggir—a narrow lane that dead-ends at the canal. He makes camphor-wood cabinets for Dutch households, teak benches for Chinese merchants, rattan chairs for Javanese officials. The workshop is his father's legacy: four walls, three apprentices, two decades of customer relationships built on quality and fair dealing.

The harbor market operates on Tuesdays and Saturdays. Livestock, furniture, textiles, spices—anything that can be carried or led changes hands there. It's the only place where a craftsman can turn finished work into cash without going through a middleman. The Dutch colonial administration maintains the market as a controlled space: licensed vendors, posted prices, harbor police to prevent "disturbances."

The road from Tan's workshop to the harbor market passes through the densest part of the Chinese quarter. It's a fifteen-minute walk, but the lane is never empty. Neighbors sit outside grinding spices, children play congkak on doorsteps, old men smoke and watch. Everyone knows everyone. Everyone has an opinion.

**The buffalo**—Tan owns one, a young female, four years old, bought from a Javanese farmer two years ago. She pulls the cart that carries furniture to market. She's not a pet, but she's valuable: a good buffalo costs six months of profit. Tan feeds her, his eldest son grooms her, his wife complains about the smell. The buffalo is patient with the cart, steady on the uneven cobblestones, unbothered by the harbor noise. Until she isn't.

### Linguistic Strategy: Code-Switching (Hokkien / Market Malay / Institutional Dutch)

**Hokkien** (閩南語/Bân-lâm-gú): The language of intimacy, family, private calculation. Used between Chinese residents of the quarter, in the workshop, in the home. Carries the weight of ancestral obligation and communal judgment. When Tan speaks Hokkien, he is speaking to people who know his father's reputation, who remember when he was an apprentice, who will remember what he does today.

**Market Malay** (Bahasa Pasar): The language of transaction and public performance. A creole of Malay, Javanese, Hokkien, and Dutch commercial terms. Used in the market, on the street, with customers of different backgrounds. Grammatically simplified, pragmatically flexible. When Tan speaks Market Malay, he is performing competence—showing he can navigate the colonial economy, that he belongs in public space.

**Institutional Dutch** (Nederlands): The language of power and official reality. Used by harbor police, colonial administrators, licensing officials. Tan understands it but speaks it poorly. His sons are learning it in the Chinese school. When Dutch is spoken to Tan, it creates a power differential he cannot easily challenge—the language itself enforces deference.

**Code-switching signals**:
- Hokkien → intimacy, vulnerability, genuine emotion
- Market Malay → performance, transaction, public face
- Dutch → institutional power, non-negotiable reality
- Switch from Hokkien to Malay = putting on public mask
- Switch from Malay to Hokkien = dropping mask, revealing private self
- Inability to switch to Dutch = structural powerlessness

### Character Positions

**Tan Ah Kow** (陈亚九, Tân A-káu)
Furniture maker, 43 years old, second-generation Semarang Chinese. Inherited the workshop from his father. Has three sons, two apprentices. Speaks Hokkien at home, Market Malay in public, understands but struggles with Dutch. His reputation is local—bounded by the three streets where his customers live. He has enough savings to survive a bad month, not enough to survive losing his primary asset (the buffalo). The market is his only access point to cash economy; the Chinese merchants who could buy wholesale want prices he can't afford to offer.

**Position**: Moderate power (owns means of production, but dependent on market access), biographical time horizon (thinking about his sons' inheritance), constrained exit (could theoretically relocate, but would lose all customer relationships), local scope (reputation and relationships don't travel).

**Mrs. Lim** (林太太, Lîm Thài-thài)
Neighbor, 50s, widow who runs a small provisions shop. Sits outside most afternoons, sees everything that happens on the lane. Speaks Hokkien with Tan's wife, Market Malay with customers. Her judgments carry weight because she's older, widowed (therefore morally authoritative), and controls access to credit for small household purchases. She doesn't have institutional power, but she has the power of communal enforcement—what she says becomes what the neighborhood believes.

**Position**: Powerful within local scope (social authority, information control), immediate time horizon (concerned with daily propriety), arbitrage exit (can choose to speak or stay silent), local scope (influence doesn't extend beyond the neighborhood).

**Ong Ah Huat** (王亚发, Ông A-hoat)
Market vendor, 30s, sells rattan and wicker goods. Competes with Tan for customers. Speaks Market Malay fluently, some Dutch. His stall is permanent—he pays monthly fees to the harbor authority. He sees Tan as a threat when Tan brings furniture to market: same customers, better quality goods, lower prices (no stall fees). He has the power to speak loudly in a public space where volume matters.

**Position**: Moderate power (licensed vendor status, but still dependent on daily sales), immediate time horizon (daily sales targets), constrained exit (could work elsewhere, but license is valuable), local scope (market authority doesn't extend beyond the harbor).

**Sergeant De Vries**
Harbor police, Dutch, 40s. Enforces market regulations, breaks up fights, moves along unlicensed vendors. Speaks Dutch and limited Market Malay. He doesn't care about furniture or buffalo ownership—he cares about maintaining order in a space where the colonial administration has decided order must be maintained. He has a baton and the authority to use it. He represents institutional power that doesn't need to explain itself.

**Position**: Institutional power (state authority, physical force), immediate time horizon (shift-based work), mobile exit (can transfer to other posts), local scope (authority is local instantiation of colonial system).

**The buffalo** (水牛, chúi-gû)
Four years old, female, 450 kilograms. Bought from a Javanese farmer for 60 guilders (six months of profit). Patient, steady, good with the cart. Has been across the Jembatan Mberok (the old wooden bridge over the canal) dozens of times. The bridge is loud—wooden planks over water, traffic from both directions, vendors calling out prices. On this particular morning, something is different: construction noise from the new godown on the north side, a pack of dogs fighting underneath, the bridge more crowded than usual.

**Position**: Powerless (animal, subject to decisions made by humans), immediate time horizon (responds to immediate stimuli), trapped exit (physically restrained), local scope (experiences only immediate environment).

### Inhabitation Sentences

**Tan Ah Kow**: You are a craftsman in a system where your reputation is everything and your reputation is local, and the walk to market takes you through a neighborhood where everyone knows your father's name and will judge whether you are worthy of it.

**Mrs. Lim**: You are a widow whose authority comes from being seen to uphold propriety, and the cost of speaking is nothing but the satisfaction is immediate, and you genuinely believe that correcting improper behavior is how a community maintains itself.

**Ong Ah Huat**: You are a market vendor who pays fees to be here and this man doesn't, and you see him leading an animal through the crowd in a way that makes customers uncomfortable, and pointing this out is both self-interest and public service.

**Sergeant De Vries**: You are a colonial policeman maintaining order in a space where different populations mix, and you don't need to understand the details—you just need to see that something is causing disruption and make it stop.

**The buffalo**: You are an animal that has learned to tolerate noise and crowds, but this morning the bridge is too loud and too crowded and the construction noise is too sharp, and when they tie you upside-down to carry you across, every instinct you have screams that you are going to die.

### Constraint Naturalizations

**The Market Requirement** (C1: Market_Imperative)

What locals call it: *Pergi pasar* (going to market), or in Hokkien, *chhut-chhī* (出市, going out to the market).

In the Chinese quarter, craftsmen like Tan work on commission and direct sale. The commission work—Dutch households ordering cabinets, Chinese merchants ordering altar tables—pays better but comes irregularly. The direct sale work—taking finished pieces to the harbor market—pays less per piece but provides steady cash flow. The market is the only place where a small craftsman can access customers without going through a middleman who takes a cut.

The colonial administration licenses the market and charges vendor fees. Permanent stall holders like Ong pay monthly. Itinerant sellers like Tan don't pay fees but also don't get stall space—they have to walk through the market with goods on display, hoping to catch a buyer's eye. This means Tan must invest time (half a day), labor (loading the cart, walking to the harbor, walking back), and risk (the buffalo might be injured, the furniture might be damaged in transport, the prices might be lower than expected).

The market operates Tuesday and Saturday, 6 AM to 2 PM. If Tan doesn't go, he doesn't sell. If he doesn't sell, he doesn't have cash for rice, lamp oil, apprentice wages. The workshop can survive missing one market day. It cannot survive missing many.

This is not oppressive—it's the structure of the economy. But it's also not optional. The market is the gate, and Tan must pass through it.

**The Neighborhood Gaze** (C2: Public_Performance_Demand)

What locals call it: *Dijaga orang* (being watched by people), or in Hokkien, *hō͘ lâng khòaⁿ* (予人看, being seen by people).

The walk from Tan's workshop to the harbor market passes through Gang Pinggir, Jalan Karet, and Jalan Merak—three streets where everyone knows everyone. The lane is narrow. Houses open directly onto the street. People sit outside—grinding spices, mending nets, watching children, watching each other.

This is not surveillance. This is community. Neighbors help each other, lend each other money, attend each other's weddings and funerals. But the cost of this mutual support is mutual judgment. What you do in public is subject to public commentary. If you do something strange, people will say so. If you do something improper, people will correct you. This is how the community maintains standards—not through institutional enforcement, but through constant, low-level social pressure.

The judgment is expressed in Hokkien when speaking to family, Market Malay when speaking to the person being judged. It's not angry—it's matter-of-fact. "That's not how it's done." "You're doing it wrong." "People will talk." The speaker genuinely believes they are helping—maintaining propriety, preventing shame, upholding the community's reputation in the eyes of the Dutch authorities and the Javanese majority.

But the judgments don't coordinate. Mrs. Lim thinks Tan should walk beside the buffalo (shows respect for the animal). Ong thinks Tan should ride the buffalo (shows he's a man of property, not a coolie). A Tamil dockworker thinks Tan should put a nose ring on the buffalo (proper control). A Javanese vegetable seller thinks Tan should let his son lead the buffalo (teaches the boy responsibility). Each judgment is sincere. Each judgment contradicts the others. Each judgment is delivered with the expectation of immediate compliance.

Tan cannot avoid the gaze. The lane is the only route to the market. He cannot ignore the judgments—these are his neighbors, his customers, the people who will help him when his roof leaks or lend him money when a customer pays late. He must be seen to take the judgments seriously, even when they contradict each other.

**The Bridge and the River** (C3: Material_Limits)

What locals call it: *Jembatan Mberok* (the Mberok Bridge, named after the canal it crosses), or *kâu-á* (溝仔, the ditch) in Hokkien.

The Jembatan Mberok is an old wooden bridge, twenty meters long, crossing the canal that separates the Chinese quarter from the harbor. It's narrow—two carts can barely pass each other. The planks are uneven, some rotted, some replaced with newer wood that doesn't quite match. When traffic crosses, the whole bridge shakes and creaks.

Underneath: the canal, two meters deep, flowing slowly toward the harbor. The water is brown, full of silt and refuse. The Dutch colonial government has been talking about dredging it for five years. They have not dredged it.

The bridge is loud. Wooden wheels on wooden planks. Vendors shouting. Construction on the new godown—hammering, sawing. Dogs barking. The noise echoes off the water.

For a buffalo, the bridge is a test. Buffaloes are patient animals, but they're also prey animals. Loud noise + unstable footing + water below = danger signals. A calm buffalo will cross. A nervous buffalo will balk. A panicked buffalo will fight.

When a buffalo panics, it thrashes. When it thrashes while tied upside-down to a pole carried by two men, the pole becomes a lever. The men lose their grip. The buffalo falls. The canal is too shallow to swim in, too deep to stand in, too silted to see through. A buffalo that falls into the canal will drown—not from the water, but from the mud.

This is not a social rule. This is not a policy. This is physics and biology. The bridge is what it is. The buffalo's panic response is what it is. The canal is what it is.

## SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

### RESOLVED

**Ω_E01: Historical accuracy of Semarang Chinese quarter in 1911**
- Question: Did this demographic/economic structure exist?
- Resolution: Yes. Semarang had established Chinese quarter (Pecinan) by 1900. Furniture making, market economy, and colonial licensing system all documented. Hokkien-speaking community, second/third generation settlement.
- Impact: Setting is historically grounded. No anachronisms in economic or social structure.

**Ω_E02: Language mixing patterns**
- Question: Would Hokkien/Malay/Dutch code-switching occur in this context?
- Resolution: Yes. Documented in colonial records and linguistic studies of Peranakan Chinese communities. Hokkien for in-group, Malay for market/interethnic communication, Dutch for institutional contexts.
- Impact: Code-switching is naturalized through historical reality, not invented for narrative convenience.

**Ω_E03: Buffalo ownership and use**
- Question: Would Chinese craftsman own buffalo for transport?
- Resolution: Yes, but less common than among Javanese farmers. Chinese craftsmen more often used hand-carts or hired transport. However, a successful craftsman with regular market sales could justify the investment.
- Impact: Buffalo ownership marks Tan as moderately successful (not poor, not wealthy). Makes the loss more significant.

**Ω_C01: Why carry buffalo across bridge instead of leading it?**
- Question: What scenario makes this action plausible?
- Resolution: If buffalo refuses to cross (balking at noise/crowd) AND Tan has already tried leading it AND he's under time pressure (market closing soon) AND neighbors have suggested "carry it" as solution to apparent "stubbornness," then the action becomes plausible—not rational, but plausible under accumulated social pressure + immediate need.
- Impact: Requires setup showing buffalo's initial refusal, time pressure, and escalating suggestions. The absurdity must emerge from situation, not from Tan's character.

**Ω_C02: Indexical variance in power positions**
- Question: Do the selected character positions create sufficient χ divergence?
- Resolution: Yes. Calculated χ values show clear splits: Tan (0.36 for C1, 0.96 for C2), Mrs. Lim (0.384 for C2), Sergeant De Vries (institutional position, negative χ for C2). Variance is structural, not arbitrary.
- Impact: Characters will experience same constraints differently without requiring "villain" roles. Conflict emerges from structure.

**Ω_C03: What makes the neighborhood gaze "Snare" vs "Rope"?**
- Question: Why is social judgment extractive rather than coordinative?
- Resolution: Becomes Snare when: (1) judgments contradict each other, (2) compliance is enforced but coordination value is zero (speakers don't coordinate with each other), (3) burden falls entirely on subject (Tan), (4) no exit (must use this route). From Mrs. Lim's position, it appears as Rope (maintaining community standards, zero cost to enforce). Both classifications objectively true.
- Impact: The constraint is not inherently Snare—it's Snare from Tan's index, Rope from enforcers' indices. This is the engine.

**Ω_P01: Should the buffalo survive?**
- Question: Does the tragedy require the buffalo's death, or could Tan lose it another way (injury, sale under duress)?
- Resolution: Death is structurally necessary. C3 (Material_Limits) must assert itself irreversibly to terminate the system. Injury could be reversed, sale could be undone. Death is the only truly terminal state that forces recognition without possibility of recovery.
- Impact: The bridge scene must be written to make the death feel inevitable (not arbitrary violence) once the absurd action (carrying buffalo) is attempted.

**Ω_P02: Tone—tragic or satirical?**
- Question: Aesop's original is satirical (moral lesson: "please everyone, please no one"). Should this version maintain satire or shift to tragedy?
- Resolution: Tragedy. The original fable treats the miller as foolish. The indexed version treats Tan as rational from his position—he's responding to real social pressure from a position of structural powerlessness. The tragedy is that he's not foolish; he's trapped. Satire would undermine the indexical logic (would imply he should have "just ignored" the judgments, which is not structurally available to him).
- Impact: Tone must be empathetic to Tan, not mocking. The absurdity is in the system, not in Tan's character.

**Ω_P03: Should Tan have explicit recognition moment (TR4)?**
- Question: Does Tan articulate the lesson, or does recognition remain implicit?
- Resolution: Implicit recognition only. Tan realizes he's lost everything, but he doesn't articulate "the system was a Snare" because he doesn't have that meta-language. The reader performs the analysis. Tan just knows: "I tried to do what everyone said, and now I have nothing." The framework must stay invisible.
- Impact: No explicit moral. No "in trying to please everyone..." speech. Just the felt experience of loss and the structural understanding that emerges from it.
