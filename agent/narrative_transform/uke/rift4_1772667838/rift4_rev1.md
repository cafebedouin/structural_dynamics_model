# Wódr̥-Skar

---

The scuttlers were gone from the eastern pillar.

She stopped at its base. The congregation had been there for eighty-two cycles, pressed against the eastern face. The lower half ran darker than the upper half, discoloration at the exact height where shells pressed flat would leave their mark. No shells now. No fragments. Whatever had been here had moved, not dispersed.

She ran her scanner across the base. Baseline organic, same as the pillar had read for all the cycles anyone had been checking.

The enforcement chassis ran inward on its morning circuit. She tracked this without thinking about it, the same way she tracked the *H₁eǵʰ-syn* pulse intervals and the quota rhythms. The body held these things the way it held balance.

She crouched at the pillar base and pressed her left palm flat against the stone where the scuttlers had been.

The hum was not like the warren wall.

The warren wall ran a slow warmth in the deep register, the city's undertone. At the pillar base the hum was the same register but not the same volume. Not louder. More concentrated. The difference between a voice in a large chamber and a voice close to your ear.

She pressed harder.

The lattice in her left flank responded.

She felt it the way you feel a held breath releasing—not a sound, a change in pressure. The intrusions ran from her collarbone through her ribcage and down into the hip joint, and where they ran closest to the stone's surface they resonated. Running together. Running at the same pitch.

She had not known her body could do this.

She held still and let the resonance continue. The lattice had been growing into her for eighteen cycles, since they moved her to the vent face. Eighteen cycles of her body learning to work around the intrusions, building new patterns of movement and weight distribution. The patterns had made her different from the cores who still moved the way bodies moved before the lattice reached them.

The patterns had also made her capable of this.

She understood it in her body before the thought finished forming. The adaptation—the eighteen cycles of learning to carry the lattice's weight, to move with its architecture instead of against it—had built something the city hadn't intended. She could receive the substrate's frequency through the very structure that was supposed to keep her oriented toward production and quota.

The constraint and the capacity were the same thing.

She pressed harder still, and under pressure the hum opened. Not louder. It had layers to it—layers she could feel the existence of, the way you can feel that a stone has grain even when you can't see which direction the grain runs.

One layer in the hum separated from the others. She recognized the rhythm. The *H₁eǵʰ-syn* pulse, the interval that structured the city's time, the beat that called bodies to work and rest and movement.

The pulse in the stone was older than the pulse in the city.

She felt this the way the body feels the onset of heat before the mind names hot—direct, certain, arriving as physical fact rather than conclusion. The city's timing was following something in the substrate, not generating it. The *H₁eǵʰ-syn* that she had learned to feel as the city's rhythm was an echo of something that had been running in the deep strata since before the city was here.

The city thought it controlled the rhythm. The rhythm came from below.

She held her palm against the stone and let the lattice in her body run at the pillar's frequency. The work assignment's time was passing. She was past the limit a single-location debris assessment could justify.

She took three scanner readings she did not need, her left palm still flat against the stone between them. Baseline organic, same as before. She logged each reading. Three data points for a debris-field assessment. Nothing in the log would require explanation.

She stood.

The hum stopped the moment her palm left the stone. Not faded. Stopped, the way sound stops when the source stops—immediate, complete. Her body went on holding the frequency for a few seconds after, the lattice resonating on its own against nothing, and then that too settled.

Her hip joint, which had been stiff all morning where the lattice had reached it during the previous night, was no longer stiff.

---

The nest-touch was finishing when she returned to the warren.

She went to her position against the far wall. The space opened for her, same width as always. The murmur moved lateral through the bodies still threading kinship, clutch-mate to clutch-mate, and the space she left had its own shape now. Nobody arranged this. It was just how the space ran.

She lowered herself to the floor and pressed her left palm to the ferrocrete.

The hum was quieter here. Filtered through more material, running at the warren wall's depth rather than the rift margin's depth, dimmed by the distance between the two. But it was the same hum—she recognized it now in a way she hadn't before. The pillar had let her touch it directly. The warren wall was the same hum heard through what the city put between the living and the deep.

She pressed harder. The lattice responded the same way it had at the pillar—the resonance, the running-together at the same pitch. Less vivid here. Still present.

The layers were there too. Harder to distinguish through the warren wall's thickness, but there: the slow accumulation of something the city had built itself on top of without knowing, or without acknowledging it knew.

She found the *H₁eǵʰ-syn* rhythm in the layering. The pulse that would arrive in another few hours to call them all to work, already present in the stone, running at its own pace. She had thought it was the city's time. It was the substrate's time. The city had just learned to listen.

Around her the warren had finished its nest-touch. The breathing of sleeping bodies came in from the far end. She was awake in the dark, in the heat, her palm flat against the stone, and the hum was in the stone with its particular depth and the lattice was in her body with its particular architecture and the space between them was very small.

She did not know what this meant.

She did not know where this practice led, or what it would cost her, or whether it would cost her anything beyond what was already paid. The nest-touch moved lateral, clutch-mate to clutch-mate, threading kinship through bodies that shared the same time and the same structure. What she was doing moved vertical, down through the ferrocrete and the worked stone and the rift margin into the substrate that had been running its own rhythms since before the city learned to listen.

The choice was already made. Had been made at the pillar, or before that, on the night she had watched the elder's hand on the wall and understood that some forms of attention were not performed for anyone else. The space that opened for her in the warren, the isolation that had settled around her like a fact—this was the cost that was already paid. What came next was the cost she hadn't paid yet.

She held her palm to the stone and felt the hum run through the lattice in her body, the intrusions that marked her as constrained resonating with the frequency the constraint system didn't acknowledge existed, and she understood that she was choosing this. Choosing to hear what the city built itself on top of. Choosing the vertical over the lateral. Choosing to learn a rhythm older than the *H₁eǵʰ-syn*, older than the quota, older than the idea that time was something administered rather than something rising from below.

The hum continued. Her body continued. She was at a junction of something she had no map of, and the junction did not require her to know what it was.

Her arm began to tire.

She stayed until the tiredness had moved from discomfort into the kind of bodily fact that required response—the muscle's limit, not something to manage around. Then she shifted her weight and lay down on her side, her left palm still against the ferrocrete, the hip joint protesting the contact of bone against hard floor.

The hum ran through her palm and her wrist and up into the forearm where the lattice hadn't reached yet, into flesh that was still only flesh, and in that flesh it arrived as something with a shape she had no word for. Not instruction. Not signal. Not the kinship-inventory of the nest-touch murmur. Something older than language, carried in stone, patient enough to wait for a body that could receive it.

She pressed her palm flat and let the substrate run its rhythm through the architecture the city had built into her body, and the cost of this—the path that led away from the nest-touch and toward something she couldn't name yet—was already present in the space that opened around her, in the isolation that was no longer isolation because she had found what lived beneath it.

The hum continued, layered and old, carrying whatever it carried toward whatever could receive it.

She lay in the dark with her palm against the stone, and she listened.
