# Wódr̥-Skar

---

## I.

The intrusions had reached her hip joint sometime during the previous night.

She knew before she opened her eyes — the body delivered this kind of information before waking, before anything as deliberate as checking. The lattice in her left side ran from collar to ribcage to hip now, a settled fact, and what had felt like an extension three seasons ago felt like her own structure this morning. Not growth. Architecture. The kind of thing that stops being news.

She pressed her palm to the ferrocrete before rising.

The elder had showed her this without meaning to. On the night of the broken nest-touch — three breaths held, the elder's forehead against hers and the murmur trying to thread through and mostly failing, the static so loud she had felt it in her teeth — the elder had returned to the far wall afterward and laid her hand flat against the stone. Had stayed there for a long time. Had not looked up to see if anyone was watching.

She had been watching.

She had been pressing her own hand to the ferrocrete every morning for thirty-some cycles now, not because she had been told to, not because she understood it, but because the body learns from what it attends to, and she had attended to the elder the way you attend to the only thing in the room that isn't performing what it's doing.

The hum was there when she pressed. Below the warmth, below the temperature of the stone — which ran cold against her palm and had always run cold, the ferrocrete conducting heat out rather than in. The hum was underneath the coldness. A slowness. A vibration at a depth she could only feel in the flat of her palm and not in her fingers, as if the stone were breathing at a frequency the fingertips were too small to receive.

She did not know what it was. She held it for the time between waking and the *H₁eǵʰ-syn*, which was less time than it had once been — the pulse arrived earlier each cycle, had been arriving earlier for longer than she had been paying attention — and then she stood.

Around her the warren was doing the nest-touch. The murmur moved lateral, clutch-mate to clutch-mate, threading kinship through the bodies still settling into themselves. She went to collect her assignment without participating. The space she left had its own shape now; it opened ahead of her as she moved through the warren, and closed behind her, and nobody arranged this. It was just how the space ran.

*Debris-field clearing. Eastern rift margin. Sector twelve and adjacent.*

She collected her tools. Her right hand closed on the handle and her left arm found its position against her side, already adjusting its brace — the elbow angled to carry the weight the lattice had migrated out of her shoulder, the forearm oriented to let her right hand work freely. She had built this pattern into the body over eighteen cycles of working the vent face at Nineteen. The pattern was not slower than the way she had once moved. It was the way she moved.

She joined the morning flow toward the vents.

---

## II.

The eastern rift margin was older than the working sectors. You could feel it in the ferrocrete: thicker deposits, older pitting, the steam-scars that accumulated over seasons the maintenance crews hadn't reached in some time. The vent mouths here ran shallow and mineral-poor, their mouths irregular, their chambers cramped. The production numbers from this margin were what they were. The assignment-callers sent debris-clearing work here, not quota labor.

She moved through the debris field methodically. The scanner in her right hand read each section and she collected what it flagged and discarded the rest: worn tool-handles snapped at the ferrule, lattice fragments ground to crystal dust by machinery they'd gotten into, the mineral slag that built up in the channel junctions when the draw ran slow. There was a rhythm to this that was different from vent-face rhythm — slower, more stop-and-start, the body crouching and rising rather than holding a steady position. Her hip joint was stiff where the lattice had reached it overnight. It would loosen as the warmth came up.

She heard about the scuttlers the way everyone heard about things that didn't concern their work: in pieces, in passing, accumulating over cycles into a picture she had never had reason to look at directly. A congregation at the eastern pillar at Vent Twelve, been there since before the last quarter, nobody knows why, the Ventkeeper's been noting it. They don't feed there. They just stay. It had been interesting for a while and then it had become the kind of thing that people mentioned and forgot they'd mentioned, one of the city's persistent small oddities that the instruments all confirmed were nothing.

The scuttlers were gone. She knew this too. The gone-ness had traveled through the warrens the same way the congregation had — in pieces, in passing — and had been slightly more interesting than the congregation, briefly, because absence registers differently than presence. Then that had faded too.

She was almost past the eastern pillar when she stopped.

She hadn't intended to stop. The pillar was at the edge of her assigned sector, not inside it, and her scanner had nothing to say about it. She looked at it and the looking kept going.

The stone had a quality she didn't have a word for. Not dirty — used. The way a tool looks after long service: not degraded, only held. The lower half of the pillar's eastern face ran darker than the upper half, a discoloration at the exact height where a mass of shells pressed flat against rock would leave its mark. No shells now. No fragments. The marine residue that she might have expected from a congregation of that size and duration was absent — whatever had been here had moved, not dispersed, had gone all at once rather than shedding. She ran her scanner across the base. Baseline organic, same as the pillar had read for all the cycles anyone had been checking.

She logged: *Vent 12 eastern pillar, scuttler aggregation absent, no trace, baseline organic.*

She marked it *no anomaly* and went on.

She came back.

---

## III.

She crouched at the pillar base.

The habit — the same thing that took her palm to the ferrocrete each morning before the *H₁eǵʰ-syn*, the same thing that had made her watch the elder's hand on the wall and not look away. She pressed her left palm flat against the stone where the scuttlers had been.

The hum was not like the warren wall.

The warren wall ran a slow warmth in the deep register, the city's undertone, what she had come to think of as simply present the way the steam ceiling was simply present and the quota pulse was simply present — the continuous condition you stopped registering because registering it all the time was not possible. At the pillar base the hum was the same register but not the same volume. Not louder. More concentrated. The difference between a voice in a large chamber and a voice close to your ear — the sound itself no different, the presence entirely different.

She pressed harder.

Under pressure the hum did not get louder. It opened. She had no better word. The hum at the warren wall was a single thing, undifferentiated, warmth and vibration and depth without distinction. The hum at this pillar base, pressed hard, had layers to it. Not layers she could separate or identify, but layers she could feel the existence of, the way you can feel that a stone has grain even when you can't see which direction the grain runs. Something in the rock here had been accumulating. The scuttlers had been pressed against this surface for eighty-two cycles because of this — she understood that in the body before the thought finished forming, felt the sense of it the way the body feels the onset of heat before the mind names hot. They had come because the hum was loudest here. They had stayed because the hum was loudest here. They had moved on and the hum had not moved. The hum did not move.

The lattice in her left flank responded to the contact. She felt it the way you feel a held breath releasing — not a sound, a change in pressure, the tissue doing something the tissue had not done against the warren wall. The intrusions ran through her ribcage and down into the hip joint now, and where they ran closest to the stone's surface of her palm they resonated. The right word, she thought, was resonated, though she had never used the word in her own head before. Running together. Running at the same pitch.

She had not known her body could do this.

She pressed harder still.

The hum was layered and old and patient and it carried something she couldn't name except by the sensation of it — not information, not signal, not the kinship-inventory of the nest-touch murmur. More like the quality of air before a large thermal event: not the event itself but the pressure that preceded it, the stone's knowing that something was building in the deep strata and the surface had not yet been told. Something that had been accumulating for longer than eighty-two cycles of scuttler congregation. Something that lived in the rock the way the rift's heat lived in the rock — not placed there, not generated on the surface, rising from below.

The work assignment's time was passing. She tracked it without looking up, the cycle's intervals as embedded in her body as her own breathing. She was past the limit a single-location debris assessment could justify. She was past it by a margin the log would not absorb without notice.

The enforcement chassis ran inward. Its circuit did not touch the eastern margin on the morning schedule.

She pressed her crystalline palm flat against the stone and felt the old hum move through the lattice in her body and she did not stand up.

Later — the cycle's time passing in her body like a presence, the way all the city's timings were presences — she took three scanner readings she did not need, at the pillar base, her left palm still flat against the stone between them. Baseline organic, same as before. The scanner had nothing to say about what she was feeling. She logged each reading. Three data points for a debris-field assessment of the eastern pillar at Vent Twelve. Nothing in the log would require explanation.

She stood.

The hum stopped the moment her palm left the stone. Not faded. Stopped, the way sound stops when the source stops — immediate, complete, the silence a different quality than quiet. Her body went on holding the frequency for a few seconds after, the lattice resonating on its own against nothing, and then that too settled.

She went back to work. Her hip was no longer stiff.

---

## IV.

The name had been in the litany for thirty cycles.

This was how the litany worked: a name entered it and lived in the flesh for a season, and after a season it was simply one of the names, part of the running count, the weight of it distributed into the total weight and no longer its particular weight. Thirty cycles was not a season. Thirty cycles was the name still having its own gravity, still arriving in the litany with a specific pull rather than blending into the whole.

She had been at Vent Fourteen when the Warden had come.

She had not known what she was watching while she watched it. The disposal cadence — the longer phrase, the lower register, the way the stilts locked into position in a sequence that differed from the inspection stance — she had learned to read these things in the body the way all the cores at the vent face had learned to read them, a muscle-memory of looking away, of making the tool's rhythm very precise and very consistent, of giving the compliance scan no behavioral deviation to find. She had looked away. She had heard the tool fall. She had kept her own rhythm: brace, position, stroke, withdraw.

Thirty cycles. The name's particular weight still in the flesh.

---

The nest-touch was finishing when she returned to the warren.

She went to her position against the far wall. The space opened for her, same width as always, settled and automatic. She lowered herself to the floor and her left side ached where the hip joint had been stiff all morning — the looseness from the pillar had lasted through the work cycle and was now tightening again as the body cooled.

Across the warren the elder had her hands flat against the ferrocrete. Both palms, full weight bearing into the stone, the way she had been doing for longer than the young core had been watching. The elder's eyes were closed. Her breathing was slow.

The young core pressed her own palm to the wall.

The hum was quieter here. Filtered through more material, running at the warren wall's depth rather than the rift margin's depth, dimmed for the distance between the two. But it was the same hum — she recognized it now in a way she hadn't before, the way you recognize a voice in a bad connection: the connection degraded, the voice identifiable. The pillar had let her touch it directly. The warren wall was the same hum heard through what the city put between the living and the deep.

She pressed harder. The lattice responded the same way it had at the pillar — the resonance, the running-together at the same pitch. Less vivid here. Still present.

She thought the name from the litany. Not because she had decided to. Because the hum was running through her palm and the name was in the flesh with its particular weight, and the two things were occupying the same part of her body, and the body did what bodies do with things that occupy the same space.

The hum shifted.

She couldn't have said what shifted. The tone was the same, the depth was the same, the slow patient rhythm was the same. Something in the layering beneath had moved — had changed position the way a sleeper changes position, the quality of the weight redistributing. She felt it pass through her palm and her wrist and up into the forearm where the lattice hadn't reached, into flesh that was still only flesh, and in that flesh it arrived differently than the general hum arrived: not warmth, not vibration, but something with a specific shape. The shape of something she had no word for.

She did not know what this meant.

She held her palm to the stone and she held the not-knowing the way she held the brace-and-work when the rhythm was running clean — without analysis, as pure practice. The hum continued. Her body continued. She was at a junction of something she had no map of, and the junction did not require her to know what it was.

Around her the warren had finished its nest-touch. The breathing of sleeping bodies came in from the far end. The elder had lowered herself to the floor, both hands still against the stone, her cheek resting on the ferrocrete the way a body rests when it has found the exact position it intends to remain in.

The young core's arm began to tire.

She stayed until the tiredness had moved from discomfort into the kind of bodily fact that required response — the muscle's limit, not something to manage around. Then she shifted her weight and lay down on her side, her left palm still against the ferrocrete, the hip joint protesting the contact of bone against hard floor.

The hum continued.

She pressed her palm flat and let the lattice in her body run at the pillar's frequency, the frequency the stone had given her and the stone was still giving her, and the name from the litany was in her flesh with its particular weight and the hum was in the stone with its particular depth and the space between them was very small.

She did not sleep so much as remain: awake in the dark, in the heat, in the particular quality of a night that was like other nights in all the ways the compliance scan could measure, and different in the ways it couldn't, and the difference was in her palm against the stone and in what the stone returned against her palm, patient and old and entirely unhurried, carrying whatever it carried toward whatever could receive it.
