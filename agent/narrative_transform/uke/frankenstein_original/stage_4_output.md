# The Catalyst

The hydrogen sulfide alarm had a particular pitch—not the steady wail of the fire system, but a pulsing shriek that meant the scrubbers were overwhelmed. Rashid heard it through two floors of concrete and knew immediately which experiment had failed.

He took the stairs three at a time, his briefcase forgotten on his desk. The laboratory door stood open, ventilation fans roaring, and through the chemical haze he could see Yusof, his technician, bent over the emergency shut-off panel. The smell hit him like a fist—rotten eggs, thick enough to taste.

"Keluar! Get out!" Rashid shouted, but Yusof was already moving, one hand over his mouth, the other steadying himself against the wall.

They stood in the corridor while the ventilation system cleared the room. Yusof's eyes watered. Rashid's lungs burned.

"What happened?" Yusof asked in Malay, his voice hoarse.

Rashid switched to English. "Thermal decomposition. The catalyst wasn't stable at that pressure." He kept his voice level, professional. Inside, his heart hammered against his ribs.

"The gauge reading was normal," Yusof said. "I checked it twice."

"I know." Rashid had bypassed the pressure limiter that morning, before Yusof arrived. He had calculated the risk. He had been wrong.

The incident report took him forty minutes to write. *Unexpected exothermic reaction. Equipment malfunction. No injuries. Recommend replacement of pressure monitoring system.* He filed it with the ministry's safety office and returned to his laboratory to document the failure in his research journal. The entry was technical, precise: reaction temperature, decomposition products, timeline of the cascade. Nowhere did he write that he had known the catalyst was unstable. Nowhere did he write that he had ignored the safety protocols because five years of funding were running out and he had nothing to show Dr. Karim's review board except promising preliminary results.

That night, he told Zainab there had been a small equipment failure, nothing serious. She nodded and returned to her sewing. Aminah looked up from her book, met his eyes for half a second, then looked away. He saw the question in her face—*are you all right?*—but she didn't ask it aloud, and he didn't answer.

---

Three floors below Rashid's laboratory, Aminah filed incident reports in alphabetical order by department code, then chronological within each department. The system was efficient. She could process two hundred documents in a morning if no one interrupted her.

Today's batch included a safety report from the research division. She read the header—*Dr. Rashid bin Abdullah, National Petroleum Research Laboratory, Equipment Malfunction*—and her hands went still.

The report was brief. Professional. It revealed nothing except that something had happened and been contained. She placed it in the appropriate folder and continued working.

At lunch, she sat at her usual table by the wall, back to the room. The canteen was half-full, ministry employees collecting their rice and curry, finding seats with colleagues. Through the window, she could see the harbor, fishing boats moving between the offshore platforms.

Her father entered, collected his tray, scanned the room. His gaze passed over her table. He checked his watch and returned to the stairwell.

Aminah counted to twelve—one breath for each second—and finished her meal.

---

Hassan's wife, Siti, was discussing their eldest daughter's marriage prospects when Aminah arrived for Friday dinner. The conversation flowed around the low table like water around a stone: which families had sons of appropriate age, which had good reputations, how the photography should be handled to highlight Salmah's fair complexion.

Aminah sat at the edge of the gathering, slightly behind Zainab. She had learned this position years ago—present but peripheral, included but not in the conversational flow. From here, she could observe without being observed. Hassan's three children played in the corner. Zainab embroidered. Rashid and Hassan discussed the offshore platforms, technical details about pressure systems and maintenance schedules.

"Salmah is nineteen now," Siti said, glancing at Aminah, then away. "A good age. Not too young."

"Very good," Zainab agreed. "The right time."

Aminah watched her father's hands. They were steady on his teacup, but his shoulders had tightened. She knew that posture—the one that meant he was listening but wished he weren't.

Hassan leaned back, satisfied. "The system works if you work the system. Good family, good character, good prospects. That's what matters."

"And good health," Siti added. "Strong children."

The conversation moved on to Hassan's youngest, a son who was excelling in his technical training. Aminah let the words wash over her. She had learned not to flinch at the edges of things.

After dinner, while Siti and Zainab cleared dishes, Hassan found Rashid on the small balcony overlooking the street.

"You look tired, abang," Hassan said, using the term for older brother. "The work is eating you."

Rashid lit a cigarette. "Research is slow-slow. Can't be rushed."

"Aminah is looking thin also."

Rashid drew smoke into his lungs, held it, released it. "She's fine. The filing work suits her."

Hassan was quiet for a moment. Then: "Siti has a cousin. Widower, older man, needs someone to manage his household. No children yet. He's not particular about... appearance."

The cigarette burned between Rashid's fingers. "I'll think about it."

"Don't think too long. These opportunities don't wait."

After Hassan's family left, Rashid returned to the laboratory. He told himself he needed to check the ventilation system. He told himself the weekend documentation couldn't wait. He told himself these things, and they were true, but they were not the whole truth.

The whole truth was that he could not sit in his house with Zainab's careful silence and Aminah's careful absence and the weight of what he had failed to do pressing on his chest like a stone.

---

Dr. Karim's office overlooked the harbor. From his desk, he could see the offshore platforms, the tankers moving between them, the infrastructure of Brunei's prosperity. On his wall hung photographs: the Sultan at the petroleum ministry's opening ceremony, Karim himself receiving an award from the Edinburgh Alumni Association, his four sons at their school graduation.

The quarterly review meeting began at nine. Karim opened the folder containing research progress reports, funding allocations, incident summaries. Rashid's project was fifth on the agenda.

"Enhanced extraction methods for heavy crude," Karim read aloud. "Five years, three hundred thousand dollars, two published papers, zero commercial applications." He looked up at the review board members. "Dr. Rashid is a brilliant researcher. Trained at Imperial College. Published in *Petroleum Science and Technology*. But brilliance without results is just expensive thinking."

"The work is technically sound," offered Dr. Ibrahim, the deputy director. "The methodology is rigorous."

"I don't question the methodology. I question the timeline." Karim tapped the folder. "We have limited resources. Other researchers are producing commercial results. Platform Seven needs new extraction technology now, not in another five years."

"There was a safety incident last week," Dr. Ibrahim noted. "Equipment malfunction. No injuries."

Karim found the incident report, scanned it. "Pressure system failure. Hydrogen sulfide release. Contained by emergency protocols." He made a note in the margin. "Add safety compliance review to next quarter's oversight."

The board moved on to the next project. Karim filed Rashid's folder and turned to the budget projections. The system worked: allocate resources, measure outputs, adjust accordingly. Some research succeeded, some failed. The ministry's job was to identify which was which and act appropriately.

Outside his window, a tanker moved toward the offshore platforms, carrying equipment for the expansion project. The harbor was busy, the platforms productive, the Sultanate prosperous. The work continued.

---

Aminah learned to read her father's patterns the way sailors read the tide. She knew his footsteps in the corridor above her filing room—the quick pace that meant he was moving between meetings, the slower rhythm that meant he was thinking, the irregular hesitation that meant he had seen her and was choosing another route.

On Monday morning, she heard the hesitation. She continued filing, head down, hands steady. The footsteps retreated toward the stairwell.

On Wednesday afternoon, she was crossing the ministry courtyard when she saw him at the far entrance. He turned back inside. She continued to the bus stop.

On Friday, Zainab mentioned that Hassan's wife had a cousin, a widower, not particular about appearance. Aminah nodded and said she would think about it. She saw her father's shoulders relax slightly.

That night, she lay in bed and performed the calculation she had been performing since she was fourteen: her salary, her expenses, her savings. How long until she could afford a room of her own. How long until she could choose when to be visible and when to disappear. The numbers never quite added up. The filing clerk position paid enough to contribute to the household but not enough to leave it. Her father had arranged it that way, though she didn't think he had done so consciously.

*Susunan yang betul,* she thought. The proper order. Everything in its place.

In his laboratory, Rashid was recalibrating the pressure monitoring system. The work was meticulous, requiring absolute precision. He welcomed it. Precision was something he understood. Precision followed rules. Precision could be controlled.

The catalyst synthesis required three more weeks. After that, another test series. After that, if the results were promising, a scaled prototype. After that, commercial viability assessment. After that—

He stopped. After that, what? Another five years? Another safety incident? Another quarterly review where Dr. Karim's patience wore thinner?

The laboratory was silent except for the hum of ventilation fans. Through the window, he could see the lights of the offshore platforms, steady against the dark water. Men worked there around the clock, extracting oil, processing crude, maintaining equipment. Hassan was out there now, managing a night shift, confident in the system that employed him.

Rashid returned to his calibration. The work was precise. The work was measurable. The work was something he could complete.

---

The next incident report crossed Aminah's desk three weeks later. Same laboratory, same researcher, different equipment. *Pressure seal failure during catalyst testing. Minor chemical exposure. No injuries. Recommend enhanced monitoring protocols.*

She filed it and continued working.

That evening, Hassan came to dinner without his family. He sat across from Rashid and spoke in Malay, his voice careful.

"The cousin is interested. He's seen Aminah at the mosque. He understands the situation. He's willing to proceed with negotiations."

Rashid set down his fork. "I haven't discussed this with her."

"Discuss what? A man is interested. That's good news."

"She should have a choice."

Hassan's expression shifted—not anger, but something close to it. "A choice? Abang, you're a scientist. You know how the world works. Some people have choices. Some people have circumstances. Aminah has circumstances."

Zainab stood and began clearing dishes, her movements precise and silent.

"She has a job," Rashid said. "She has a salary."

"A filing clerk's salary. In a position you arranged. How long does that last if you're not there to maintain it?" Hassan leaned forward. "I'm not being cruel. I'm being realistic. The cousin is a good man. He has a house, a business, respect in the community. He's offering her security."

"I'll think about it."

"There's nothing to think about. There's only what is." Hassan stood. "Talk to her. Or don't talk to her. But don't pretend you're protecting her by doing nothing."

After Hassan left, Rashid sat at the table while Zainab finished in the kitchen. Aminah had gone to her room—he had heard the door close when Hassan's voice rose. He should go to her. He should ask what she wanted. He should have asked twenty years ago, when she was born and he chose his research over her surgeries. He should have asked ten years ago, when she learned to make herself small. He should have asked last week, last month, last year.

He went to his laboratory instead.

---

Dr. Karim's office door was open when Rashid arrived for his appointment. The quarterly review folder sat on the desk, Rashid's project tab visible.

"Sit," Karim said, gesturing to the chair. "We need to discuss timeline expectations."

Rashid had prepared a presentation: new data, refined methodology, projected outcomes. He opened his briefcase.

Karim held up his hand. "I've read your reports. The technical work is excellent. But we're five years in with no commercial application, and I'm seeing safety incidents every few weeks."

"The incidents are minor. Standard research hazards."

"Standard research hazards that require incident reports." Karim opened the folder, showing a stack of yellow forms. "Dr. Rashid, I respect your work. I respect your training. But respect doesn't change the fact that this project is consuming resources without producing results."

Rashid's prepared arguments dissolved. "I need more time."

"You've had time. What you need is a different approach." Karim closed the folder. "I'm not terminating the project. But I am scaling back funding and requiring monthly progress reviews. If we don't see commercial viability indicators within six months, we'll need to consider reallocation."

The meeting lasted fifteen more minutes, but Rashid heard none of it. He nodded at appropriate moments, collected his briefcase, returned to his laboratory. The catalyst synthesis was nearly complete. Six months wasn't enough time. Six months was barely enough time to test the next iteration, let alone scale to commercial viability.

He could stop. He could abandon the research, accept a teaching position, focus on his family. The thought sat in his mind like a foreign object—theoretically possible, practically impossible. Who was he if not the scientist pursuing this solution? What had the last five years meant if he stopped now?

He began the catalyst synthesis procedure. The work was precise. The work was measurable. The work was something he could complete.

---

Aminah was filing quarterly reports when she heard footsteps in the corridor—not her father's pattern, but someone else's, moving with purpose. Dr. Karim appeared in the doorway of the filing room.

"Miss Aminah," he said in English. "A moment, please."

She followed him to his office, her mind running through possibilities. Had she made an error? Misfiled a document? Failed to follow some protocol she didn't know existed?

Karim gestured to a chair. "Your work is excellent. The filing system has never been more efficient."

She waited.

"I'm restructuring the research division. Some projects are being scaled back, others expanded. This will generate additional documentation and reporting requirements." He paused. "I'd like to create a senior filing position. Increased responsibility, increased salary. I think you'd be well-suited for it."

The offer was generous. Too generous. Aminah had learned to recognize when something was being offered for reasons that had nothing to do with her.

"Thank you, Dr. Karim. May I think about it?"

"Of course. But don't think too long. The restructuring begins next month."

She returned to her filing room and continued working. The quarterly reports documented research projects, funding allocations, progress assessments. Her father's project was there, marked with yellow incident report tabs. She read the summary: *scaled funding, monthly reviews, six-month commercial viability deadline*.

She filed the report in its proper place and continued working.

---

That evening, Rashid came home at seven—earlier than usual. Zainab served dinner. Aminah sat at her usual position, slightly to the side, present but peripheral.

"There's been a change at work," Rashid said in Malay. "Budget adjustments. Nothing serious."

Aminah watched her mother's hands go still on her plate.

"Dr. Karim is restructuring," Rashid continued, still not looking at Aminah. "Some projects are being scaled back. Mine included."

"Will you lose your position?" Zainab asked.

"No. Just... reduced resources. I'll need to be more efficient."

Aminah set down her fork carefully. "Dr. Karim offered me a promotion today."

Rashid's gaze finally met hers. "A promotion?"

"Senior filing clerk. More responsibility, more pay. He said the restructuring will generate additional documentation."

The silence stretched. Zainab resumed eating. Rashid stared at his plate.

"You should take it," he said finally, in English. "It's a good opportunity."

"Is it?" Aminah asked in Malay. "Or is it compensation?"

Rashid's jaw tightened. "I don't know what you mean."

"Hassan's wife has a cousin. Dr. Karim has a promotion. Everyone has solutions for circumstances they didn't create."

Zainab stood and began clearing dishes, though no one had finished eating.

Rashid's voice was quiet. "What do you want me to say?"

Aminah looked at her father—at the exhaustion in his face, the guilt in his posture, the way he held himself like a man carrying something too heavy to set down. She had learned to read these signs before she learned to read books. She knew what they meant. She knew they meant he was sorry. She knew they meant he didn't know how to fix what he had broken. She knew they meant he would go back to his laboratory and continue working because work was something he understood and she was something he didn't.

"Nothing," she said. "There's nothing to say."

She helped her mother with the dishes. Rashid sat at the table for another ten minutes, then collected his briefcase and left for the laboratory.

---

The catalyst synthesis succeeded. Rashid tested it at low pressure, then moderate pressure, documenting each result meticulously. The reaction was stable. The extraction efficiency was promising. The process generated fewer toxic byproducts than previous iterations.

He prepared his monthly progress report for Dr. Karim, including data tables, methodology notes, projected timelines. The report was thorough, professional, optimistic.

The monthly review meeting lasted thirty minutes. Dr. Karim read the report, asked technical questions, examined the data tables. At the end, he closed the folder.

"This is good work, Dr. Rashid. But good work at laboratory scale isn't the same as commercial viability. We need industrial-scale results, not promising small-batch data."

"Industrial scale requires different equipment. Larger reaction vessels, enhanced monitoring systems, safety protocols for higher volumes. That infrastructure takes time to build."

"How much time?"

Rashid had calculated this. "Eighteen months. Possibly two years."

Karim was quiet. Then: "You have six months to demonstrate scalability potential. Not full industrial implementation—just evidence that scaling is feasible. If you can show that, we'll discuss extended funding. If not..." He left the sentence unfinished.

Rashid returned to his laboratory. Six months to demonstrate scalability with equipment designed for small-batch testing. Six months to prove that two years of additional work would be worth the investment. Six months to justify the last five years.

He could do it. He had to do it. The alternative was admitting that everything—the time, the money, the safety incidents, the absence from his family—had been for nothing.

He began designing the scaling tests.

---

Aminah accepted the promotion. The new position came with a small office—not a room of her own, but a room without other people. She could close the door. She could control who entered. She could choose when to be visible.

The salary increase was modest but meaningful. She recalculated her savings timeline: three years until she could afford a room somewhere else. Three years of filing documents, processing reports, maintaining the system that employed her father and excluded her from everything except employment.

Hassan's wife stopped mentioning the cousin. The opportunity had passed, Siti said with practiced sympathy. The man had found someone else. There would be other opportunities. Aminah nodded and did not say that she had never wanted that opportunity, had never been asked if she wanted it, had only been informed that it existed and then that it didn't.

She filed the documents that detailed her father's scaled funding, his monthly reviews, his six-month deadline. She filed the incident reports that documented his failures. She filed the quarterly assessments that measured his value to the ministry. She filed everything in its proper place, alphabetically by department code, chronologically within each department, and she did not think about what it meant that her job was to organize the evidence of her father's desperation.

---

The scaling test failed.

Rashid had calculated the pressure ratios, adjusted the catalyst concentration, modified the reaction vessel. The mathematics were sound. The methodology was rigorous. The equipment was properly calibrated.

The catalyst decomposed anyway.

The hydrogen sulfide alarm shrieked. The ventilation system roared. Yusof evacuated the laboratory, and Rashid stood in the corridor, breathing air that tasted like rot, and understood that six months would not be enough time because no amount of time would be enough time because the fundamental problem was not solvable with current technology and he had spent five years refusing to accept that.

He wrote the incident report. *Thermal decomposition during scaling test. Catalyst instability at elevated pressure. No injuries. Recommend project reassessment.*

He filed it with the ministry's safety office and returned to his laboratory to stare at his equipment and understand that he had failed.

---

Dr. Karim's office was quiet when Rashid arrived for his unscheduled appointment. The quarterly review folder was not on the desk. Karim gestured to a chair and waited.

"The scaling test failed," Rashid said. "The catalyst is not stable at industrial pressure."

Karim nodded slowly. "Can it be modified?"

"Possibly. With different materials, different reaction conditions. But that would require starting over. New synthesis, new testing, new timeline."

"How long?"

"Five years. Maybe more."

Karim leaned back in his chair. "Dr. Rashid, I respect your honesty. Most researchers would have hidden the failure, requested additional funding, promised results they couldn't deliver. You're telling me the truth, and I appreciate that."

Rashid waited.

"But the truth is that we can't invest another five years in a project that might not succeed. The ministry has limited resources. Other researchers are producing results. We need to allocate funding where it will generate returns."

"I understand."

"I'm not terminating your employment. You're a valuable researcher. But this specific project is concluded. I'd like you to transition to the technical review committee, help evaluate other researchers' work, contribute your expertise to project assessment."

The offer was generous. Rashid understood that. He would retain his position, his salary, his standing. He would advise other researchers, help guide the ministry's research priorities, contribute to Brunei's petroleum science capacity.

He would not pursue the solution he had spent five years chasing. He would not prove that the work had been worth it. He would not transform the extraction methods that could extend the Sultanate's petroleum independence.

He would be a man who had tried and failed, and the trying and failing would have to be enough.

"Thank you, Dr. Karim. I accept."

---

Aminah processed the project termination documents three days later. The file was thin: final progress report, budget reconciliation, equipment inventory, personnel reassignment. Her father's five-year pursuit reduced to sixteen pages of administrative paperwork.

She filed them alphabetically by department code, chronologically within the department, and did not think about what it meant that she was organizing the evidence of her father's failure the same way she had organized the evidence of his desperation.

That evening, Rashid came home at six. He helped Zainab prepare dinner. He asked about Aminah's work. He ate slowly, present in a way he had not been present in years.

After dinner, he asked if Aminah would like to take a walk. She agreed, though she could not remember the last time they had walked together.

They followed the harbor road, past the fishing boats and cargo vessels, toward the offshore platforms visible in the distance. The air smelled of salt and petroleum. The evening call to prayer echoed from the mosque.

"The project is finished," Rashid said in Malay. "Dr. Karim has reassigned me to technical review."

Aminah nodded. She had filed the paperwork.

"I thought..." Rashid stopped, started again. "I thought if I succeeded, it would matter. The work would be worth it. The time would be justified."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what it was worth."

They walked in silence. Aminah watched the platforms in the distance, their lights steady against the darkening water. Men worked there around the clock, extracting oil, processing crude, maintaining equipment. Hassan was out there somewhere, managing a shift, confident in the system that employed him.

"Hassan's wife has another cousin," Rashid said quietly. "Younger man, works in the petroleum ministry's accounting division. He's seen you at the office. He asked Hassan to make inquiries."

Aminah stopped walking. "And what did you tell Hassan?"

"I told him I would ask you what you wanted."

The question sat between them like something fragile. Aminah looked at her father—at the exhaustion in his face, the guilt in his posture, the way he held himself like a man who had finally set down something too heavy to carry.

"I don't know what I want," she said finally. "But I know what I don't want. I don't want to be arranged. I don't want to be someone's solution to someone else's problem. I don't want to be the proper order."

Rashid nodded slowly. "Then I'll tell Hassan no."

"He won't understand."

"He doesn't need to understand. He needs to accept it."

They resumed walking. The harbor stretched before them, the platforms steady in the distance, the system continuing its work regardless of individual failures or individual choices.

"I'm sorry," Rashid said, still in Malay, his voice barely audible over the sound of water against the dock. "For all of it. For not being there. For the job I arranged. For the circumstances I created. I'm sorry."

Aminah watched the platforms, their lights reflecting on the dark water. She thought about her small office, her modest salary, her three-year timeline to a room of her own. She thought about her father's five-year pursuit of a solution that didn't exist. She thought about the proper order that placed them both exactly where they were.

"I know," she said.

They walked back to the house in silence. Zainab had left the porch light on. Inside, everything was in its place: the low table where they ate, the cushions where they sat, the rooms where they slept. The proper order, maintained.

Rashid went to his study—not to work, but to sit in the chair by the window and watch the harbor lights. Aminah went to her room and recalculated her savings timeline, the numbers stretching before her like a bridge she would cross slowly, step by careful step, until she reached the other side.

In his office at the ministry, Dr. Karim filed the project termination documents and turned to the next quarter's budget allocations. Other researchers needed funding, other projects showed promise, the work continued.

On Platform Seven, Hassan supervised the night shift, confident in the system that employed him, unaware that his brother had finally learned what could not be extracted from insufficient materials, unaware that his niece was counting the days until she could afford to disappear.

The harbor lights reflected on dark water. The offshore platforms maintained their steady rhythm. The proper order held, and in its holding, revealed the shape of what it contained: the scientist who had learned that some solutions do not exist, the daughter who had learned that some circumstances cannot be chosen, the system that continued its extraction regardless of what it cost the people it employed.

*Susunan yang betul.* Everything in its place. Like that only.
