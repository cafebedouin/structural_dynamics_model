# The Assessment

The terminal hums. It's been humming for eighteen years, the same frequency, the same patient insistence. I know what the number will be before it appears.

My hands still smell like tank water — salt and mineral, the particular chemistry of Platform 47's systems. I've just finished the morning inspection: twelve cultivation tanks, visual assessment first, then temperature probes, then sample collection. Tank Seven is producing beautifully. The protein mass is dense and healthy, the water clarity perfect. I can tell by looking that this batch should score high eighties, maybe ninety if the pathogen screen comes back clean.

The screen flickers. Loading. I count the seconds without meaning to: one, two, three. Always three-point-two seconds. I've timed it.

The number appears: 73.

*Fēnshù xiànjǐng.* Score trap. I mutter it in Mandarin, under my breath, where the terminal can't hear me.

Seventy-three means secondary market. Seventy-three means the revenue split goes to industrial processing instead of premium distribution. Seventy-three means my daughter's engineering school stays out of reach for another quarter.

I know the measurement has error. Everyone knows this. Plus or minus three points — it's physics, not policy. You can't eliminate observer-dependence from measurement systems. The *sān fēn zhēnlǐ*, the three-point truth. The old-timers taught me this when I was sixteen and just learning to read water: never fight over differences smaller than three points. It's noise, not signal.

But this isn't three points. This is nine points below what I know this batch is worth. This is systematic.

I input the score into the processing queue. My hands know the keystrokes. Eighteen years of muscle memory. The English interface asks: "Confirm quality grade?" I press yes. The batch routes to secondary processing. The system accepts my compliance.

My jaw aches. It's been aching since second shift.

---

At home, the walls are thin. I hear Chen family next door arguing about their son's test scores. The air recycling system hums a different frequency than the terminal, but it's still humming. Everything on Platform 47 hums.

My daughter, Huìlín, is doing homework at the table. Math problems. She's good at math, better than I ever was. She wants to study marine engineering in Tianjin. She wants to design better cultivation systems, more efficient water chemistry, smarter algorithms.

She wants to fix what I can't.

"How was work?" she asks, not looking up from her calculations.

"Fine," I say. In Mandarin, I say: "*Yīyàng*." Same as always.

My husband is heating dinner. Recycled protein from Platform 52, ironically. Their harvest scores higher than ours. We buy it back at market rate. The apartment is forty-seven square meters for four of us. Huìlín will have to share a room with her brother until one of them leaves.

If either of them can afford to leave.

I sit down across from Huìlín. Her homework is covered in numbers, variables, optimization functions. She's learning the same mathematics that built the Assessment Network. The same logic that scores our work.

I want to tell her: the numbers lie. I want to tell her: the algorithm has a bias, a systematic error that isn't error at all but design. I want to tell her: I produce the same quality protein as Platform 52, biochemically identical, and they score twelve points higher because their water source is different and the algorithm overweights certain minerals that correlate with their geography.

But I don't tell her. Not yet. She's fourteen. She still believes the system works.

I believed it too, once.

---

Chen Wei calls me to his office three days later. The message comes through the terminal in English: "Performance review meeting, 14:00, Administrative Level 3."

I know what this means. I've been to these meetings before.

His office is quieter than the harvest floor. Better lighting. Climate-controlled. The hum is softer here, or maybe I just notice it less because everything else is so quiet. Chen Wei has been Platform 47's Quality Administrator for eight years. He transferred from the Bohai Authority's regional office. He's not from here. He commutes — two weeks on-platform, two weeks in Tianjin with his family.

He can leave. I can't.

"Supervisor Liang," he says, and switches to Mandarin: "Please, sit."

I sit. The chair is comfortable. More comfortable than anything in my apartment.

"I've been reviewing your quarterly metrics," he says, staying in Mandarin. He's trying to signal intimacy, person-to-person rather than administrator-to-worker. "Your efficiency is excellent. Tank maintenance is exemplary. But I'm seeing a pattern in your batch routing."

"The scores determine routing," I say, and I say it in English. Performing compliance. "The protocol is clear."

He winces. Switches back to English. "Yes. The protocol. But there are optimization strategies we could discuss. Ways to work within the system parameters to maximize outcomes."

"The samples are accurate," I say. Still English. "The assessment is standardized."

"I'm not questioning your work." Back to Mandarin now, and he sounds tired. "I'm trying to help you understand—" He stops. Looks at the screen on his desk. Looks away.

I wait.

"The system works well enough," he says finally. Mandarin. Quiet. "For coordination purposes. The Assessment Network enables market access for all sixty-three platforms. Without standardization, we'd have chaos. You understand this."

"I understand the algorithm underscores Platform 47 by eight to twelve points," I say. Mandarin now. Meeting him where he's trying to meet me. "I understand this has been documented since 2067. I understand every formal complaint has been acknowledged and ignored."

Chen Wei looks older than forty-one in this moment. The climate control hums around us.

"Why don't they fix it?" I ask. Not performing anymore. Just asking.

He doesn't answer. Just looks at his screen, where my metrics are displayed in neat columns. Efficiency. Compliance. Optimization.

"The transition cost would be too high," he says finally. English. Official voice. "The system satisfices."

I leave his office. Return to the harvest floor. The terminal is waiting. Tank Eight needs assessment. I collect samples. Input data. The screen flickers. Three-point-two seconds.

The number appears: 71.

I submit it.

My jaw aches worse now. Because now I know: Chen Wei knows. He knows the scores are wrong. He defends them anyway. He carries the system because dismantling it would disrupt the coordination, and coordination is what makes his job meaningful, what makes his position stable, what lets him commute to Tianjin every two weeks.

The trap has maintainers. Human ones. Tired ones.

I go home and the walls are thin and the air recycler hums and Huìlín is doing homework and I am so exhausted I can barely speak.

---

Three weeks later, I overhear Chen Wei on a call. I'm not trying to eavesdrop. I'm in the administrative corridor, waiting to submit a maintenance request form, and his office door is open a crack.

"Director Huang," he's saying, "I understand the quarterly report shows optimal function, but the workers are frustrated—"

A pause. I can't hear the other side of the call.

"Yes, I know Platform 47's efficiency metrics are strong. But the scoring bias—"

Another pause.

"I've explained to them that the transition cost would be too high. That recalibrating the entire network would cause supply disruptions. But they keep asking—"

Longer pause.

"Yes. Yes, I understand. Satisfactory compliance is the goal. I'll continue to emphasize system stability."

He sounds exhausted.

I leave before he sees me.

That night, I can't sleep. I lie in bed next to my husband and stare at the ceiling and think about Director Huang in Tianjin, seventeen floors above sea level, reviewing reports that show "optimal function" and "satisfactory compliance."

She doesn't know. Or she knows the way Chen Wei knows — as an abstraction, a footnote, a minor calibration variance within acceptable parameters.

From where she stands, the system works. The numbers are clean. Sixty-three platforms coordinated. Four hundred million people fed. The Assessment Network is a triumph of standardization.

From where I stand, my daughter can't afford engineering school.

Same system. Different view.

The trap isn't maintained by cruelty. It's maintained by distance. By the seventeen floors between Director Huang's office and the mudflats. By the gap between the people who design systems and the people who live inside them.

I am so tired.

---

The next morning, I'm at the terminal again. Tank Seven, second harvest cycle. The protein mass is beautiful. I know before I take the samples that this batch is premium quality. I know it will score in the low seventies.

I collect the samples anyway. Input the data. Watch the screen flicker. Count the seconds.

The number appears: 72.

I submit it.

That evening, Huìlín is struggling with a calculus problem. Optimization functions. Finding maximum values given constraints.

"*Māma*," she says, "I don't understand why the answer is wrong. My math is correct, but the system says it's not optimal."

I look at her homework. Her calculations are perfect. But she's optimizing for the wrong variable. The problem asks for maximum efficiency, but the constraint structure makes maximum efficiency impossible. The optimal solution is actually a compromise — good enough given the limits.

"The system satisfices," I tell her. In Mandarin. "*Xìtǒng mǎnzú*."

She frowns. "But that's not optimal."

"No," I say. "It's not."

I help her rework the problem. Show her how to find the satisficing solution instead of the optimal one. She gets it quickly. She's smart. Smarter than the system that will score her work.

When she's finished, she closes her notebook and asks: "Why is your work so hard?"

I could say it's not. I could say it's fine. I could say *yīyàng*, same as always.

Instead, I say: "The algorithm has a bias. It underscores our platform."

She looks up. "Can they fix it?"

"They can. They don't."

"Why?"

I think about Chen Wei's tired face. Director Huang's quarterly reports. The seventeen floors. The distance.

"Because from where they stand, it works."

Huìlín thinks about this. Then she opens her notebook again — not her homework notebook, her personal one — and writes something down. I can't see what. She doesn't show me.

But I know. I know because she's my daughter, because she's smart, because she's learning to see what I can see.

The recognition is passing. The alternative logic is seeding itself. Not in protests or formal complaints or system reform. In a fourteen-year-old's notebook. In what she writes down for herself, not for school.

The trap persists. But so does the knowledge of the trap.

---

The next day, I'm at the terminal again. The hum is constant. Has been for eighteen years. Will be for eighteen more, probably. Maybe for Huìlín's eighteen years after that, if she comes back here, if she can't escape to Tianjin, if the engineering school stays out of reach.

Tank Nine needs assessment. I collect samples. My hands know the water temperature without checking the probe. I can predict the score within two points just by looking.

The screen flickers. Three-point-two seconds.

The number appears: 74.

I submit it.

My jaw aches. My shoulders are tight. The exhaustion is permanent now, has been for years, will be for years more.

But tonight, I'll go home to the thin walls and the humming air recycler and Huìlín's homework. And maybe I'll tell her more. Not everything — I don't understand everything. I don't see the whole structure. I'm inside it, not above it.

But I see enough. I see that the trap is maintained. I see who maintains it. I see why they maintain it.

And now Huìlín sees it too.

The system satisfices. But knowledge doesn't. Knowledge accumulates. Passes down. Seeds itself in notebooks and quiet conversations and the mathematics of constraint.

The terminal hums. I submit the next score. And the next. And the next.

But at night, in Mandarin, where the system can't hear us, we speak a different language.

We name the trap. We count the distance. We teach each other to see.

The system persists. But so do we.

And someday, maybe, someone will stand close enough to see what we see. Someone with the power to fix what we can only name.

Someday.

For now: the hum. The scores. The exhaustion.

And at night: my daughter's notebook. The seed, planted in what she writes when no one's watching.

The trap continues. But the knowledge continues too.

Both truths. Same world. Different futures.

I submit the next score.

The terminal hums.

We endure.
