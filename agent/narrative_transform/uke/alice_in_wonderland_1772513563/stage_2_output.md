# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION OUTPUT

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

**Silo 47, Vertical City-State, Year 2891 CE**

The silo descends forty-seven levels into bedrock. Built during the Atmospheric Collapse as an emergency refuge, it now houses 3,200 souls who have never seen the surface. The architecture is vertical: residential levels stacked like coins, connected by a central spiral staircase that takes two hours to descend from top to bottom. The upper levels (1-15) house the administrative and technical class. The middle levels (16-35) contain workshops, hydroponic farms, and recycling plants. The lower levels (36-47) are residential warrens where density increases with depth.

Power flows from the Engineering Council on Level 3, which controls atmosphere processing, water reclamation, and the geothermal generators that keep everyone alive. The silo's environmental systems are ancient, partially understood, and irreplaceable. When something breaks, engineers improvise repairs using scavenged materials and half-remembered technical manuals. The deeper you live, the more you depend on decisions made forty-four levels above you.

The silo has been sealed for eleven generations. No one knows what's outside. The official narrative: toxic atmosphere, uninhabitable surface. But the sensors that would verify this stopped working six generations ago, and no one has the knowledge to repair them.

### The Atmospheric Lottery

Every month, the Engineering Council posts the "breathable hours" allocation on each level's bulletin board. This is the number of hours that level will receive full atmospheric processing — clean air, regulated temperature, controlled humidity. The allocation varies wildly: Level 5 might get 720 hours (full coverage), while Level 42 gets 380 hours (just over half). The formula is officially "based on atmospheric load and system capacity," but no one outside the Council understands it.

When your level's hours run out, the air gets thin. Not deadly — the baseline is survivable — but exhausting. People move slower, think slower, sleep more. Children don't grow as fast. The elderly develop respiratory problems. Everyone on the lower levels knows the feeling: the gradual thickening of the air, the way conversations become effortful, the headaches that start mid-month and don't stop until the allocation resets.

The Council insists the variation is environmental necessity. The lower levels generate more heat from human density, requiring less active climate control. The upper levels house critical equipment that requires stable atmospheric conditions. The math, they say, is physics.

But the pattern is suspicious. Administrative levels always get full allocation. Workshop levels get enough to stay productive. Residential levels get what's left. And the formula changes — sometimes Level 38 gets 420 hours, sometimes 340, with no explanation. People have tried to find the pattern. There isn't one.

Locals call it **the Breath Ceiling**. You can't see it, but you feel it every time you climb the stairs and notice breathing gets easier as you ascend.

### The Maintenance Lottery

The second system is more explicit. Every quarter, the Engineering Council posts a list of "approved maintenance requests" — repairs that will be executed in the coming months. Residents submit requests constantly: broken air vents, failing lights, water leaks, structural cracks. The Council reviews them and selects a subset.

The selection appears arbitrary. Critical repairs are denied while cosmetic ones are approved. A residential level might request a water leak fix for months while watching the Council approve decorative lighting for an administrative lounge. When residents ask why, the answer is always the same: "resource constraints and system priorities."

But there's a pattern underneath. If you complain too loudly about the Breath Ceiling, your maintenance requests disappear into the void. If you organize a petition, your level's allocation drops the next month. The Council never says this explicitly — they don't need to. Everyone learns.

The system has a name among the lower levels: **the Hammer**. You never know when it will fall, but you know what triggers it.

### The Vertical Commons

Despite everything, the silo functions. People coordinate. The spiral staircase operates on an unwritten traffic system: descenders on the right, ascenders on the left, load carriers in the middle. Workshop teams share tools and expertise across levels. When someone's allocation runs short, neighbors on better-allocated levels sometimes "lend" hours by hosting them overnight.

This coordination network is real. It's not enforced by the Council — it emerged organically over generations. People call it **the Spiral Contract**: the web of reciprocal obligations that keeps the silo livable despite the Council's systems.

But the Contract has a boundary. It works horizontally (between people at similar depths) but breaks vertically. Upper-level residents don't participate. They don't need to — their allocations are stable, their maintenance requests approved. From their position, the Contract looks like inefficient charity. Why share resources when the system already provides?

The Contract is strongest between Levels 30-45, where scarcity forces cooperation. It's weakest above Level 20, where abundance makes it unnecessary. And it's completely absent on Levels 1-10, where residents interact with the lower levels only through Council directives.

### Character Positions

**Kael** (Level 42, Residential Warren)
Maintenance worker, third generation. Lives in a 3x4 meter compartment with his partner and daughter. His level gets 360-400 breathable hours per month, depending on the Council's mood. He's submitted the same water leak repair request for eight months — the leak wastes ten liters daily, but the Council keeps denying it as "non-critical."

Kael knows the Breath Ceiling isn't physics. He's worked maintenance long enough to see the atmospheric processors up close. The system has excess capacity — the Council just doesn't allocate it downward. But he also knows what happens when you say this out loud. His neighbor organized a petition two years ago. The next month, their level's allocation dropped to 310 hours. The month after, the neighbor's daughter was denied a medical transfer to the upper-level clinic. The petition dissolved.

Kael's world is immediate and local. He thinks in terms of this month's allocation, this quarter's maintenance list, this leak that's wasting water his daughter needs. The idea of "changing the system" feels like fantasy — the system is the silo's physics, as unchangeable as gravity.

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are trying to raise a child in a compartment where the air runs out halfway through the month, and the people who control the air have made it clear that asking why is dangerous.*

---

**Vex** (Level 19, Technical Archives)
Archivist, second generation. Manages the silo's technical documentation — repair manuals, system diagrams, historical records. Her level gets 680-720 breathable hours (effectively full allocation). Her maintenance requests are approved within weeks.

Vex has access to the old engineering reports, back when the silo was first sealed. She's read the original atmospheric capacity studies. The system was designed to provide 720 hours to all levels — the variation wasn't part of the plan. It started three generations ago, when the first Engineering Council formed and began "optimizing allocation for system efficiency."

She knows the Breath Ceiling is constructed. She's seen the numbers. But she also benefits from it. Her air is clean, her space is comfortable, her work is intellectually stimulating. The lower levels are abstract to her — numbers in reports, not people she sees daily. When she thinks about them at all, she thinks: *If they managed their resources better, submitted clearer requests, understood the system's constraints...*

Vex participates in the Spiral Contract casually — she'll share tools with other archivists, help a colleague with a research problem. But she doesn't extend it downward. Why would she? The lower levels have their own networks. And honestly, the few times she's visited below Level 30, the air felt oppressive, the spaces chaotic. She doesn't belong there.

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are a person who believes the system works because from where you stand, it does, and the people it doesn't work for are too far away to be real.*

---

**Senna** (Level 38, Hydroponic Technician)
Third generation, works in the agricultural levels. Her level gets 400-440 breathable hours — enough to function, not enough to thrive. She's identity-locked to the silo in a way Kael isn't: her entire sense of self is bound to keeping the food systems running. If she left (and where would she go?), she wouldn't know who she was.

Senna experiences the Breath Ceiling as normal. Yes, the air gets thin mid-month. Yes, it's harder to work when that happens. But that's just how the silo is — you pace yourself, you rest when you need to, you don't waste energy on things you can't change. She's heard the rumors (the Council has excess capacity, the allocation is political), but she doesn't believe them. If that were true, someone would have fixed it by now.

She participates heavily in the Spiral Contract. She trades hydroponic produce for mechanical repairs, lends her compartment to neighbors during allocation crunches, teaches younger technicians the craft. The Contract feels natural to her — this is how people survive together. She doesn't see it as resistance to the Council's systems; she sees it as working within them.

When Kael talks about organizing, about demanding transparency from the Council, Senna gets uncomfortable. That kind of talk is dangerous. And unnecessary — the Contract already solves most problems. Why risk what works?

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are a person who has made peace with the system by not looking at it too closely, because looking closely would mean admitting that the thing you've built your life around is built on something rotten.*

---

**Thane** (Level 3, Engineering Council)
Fifth generation, senior atmospheric engineer. He doesn't live in the silo's residential levels — Council members have private quarters on Level 2, with dedicated atmospheric systems that never fluctuate. He experiences the Breath Ceiling as a coordination mechanism: a way to manage limited resources and maintain system stability.

From his position, the allocation variance is necessary. The lower levels ARE denser, hotter, less efficient. Giving them full allocation would strain the system and risk cascade failures. The upper levels house critical infrastructure — they need environmental stability. This isn't politics; it's engineering.

The Maintenance Lottery is similar. The Council receives hundreds of requests monthly. They can't approve all of them — there aren't enough spare parts, enough labor hours, enough system capacity. So they prioritize: critical infrastructure first, then high-impact repairs, then everything else. If a residential level's water leak gets denied, it's because five other repairs were more important.

Thane knows the lower levels resent the Council. He thinks they don't understand the constraints. If they saw the full system — the aging equipment, the supply shortages, the constant risk of catastrophic failure — they'd realize the Council is doing the best it can.

He doesn't see himself as extracting from the lower levels. He sees himself as keeping everyone alive. The fact that his air is always clean, his space always comfortable, his decisions always final — that's just the cost of the responsibility he carries.

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are the person who makes the decisions that keep three thousand people breathing, and the fact that you breathe easier than they do is simply the price of that burden.*

---

**Ren & Davi** (Level 44, Residential Warren)
Maintenance crew, fourth generation. They live two compartments down from Kael. Their level gets 340-380 breathable hours — the lowest in the silo. They've watched their children struggle to concentrate in the thin air, watched their elderly neighbors develop chronic respiratory problems, watched the Council deny their maintenance requests while approving cosmetic upgrades forty levels above.

Ren and Davi know the Breath Ceiling is a trap. They've tried everything: petitions (ignored), delegation visits to the Council (turned away), work slowdowns (punished with allocation cuts). Nothing works. The system is designed to make resistance impossible.

But they haven't given up. They're organizing quietly — building networks across the lower levels, documenting the allocation patterns, teaching people to recognize the Hammer before it falls. They don't talk about "overthrowing the Council." They talk about "making the system visible." If enough people see the pattern, maybe something will shift.

**Inhabitation sentence:** *You are trying to show people that the thing crushing them isn't gravity, it's a boot, and the hardest part is that the boot has been there so long it feels like gravity.*

### Linguistic Strategy: Code-Switching (Vertical Creole)

The silo has two languages:

**Technical Standard** (TS): The language of Council directives, maintenance manuals, and official communication. Descended from the original engineering documentation, it's precise, formal, emotionally flat. It uses passive constructions and impersonal subjects: "atmospheric allocation has been adjusted," "maintenance request status: deferred." Upper levels speak TS fluently. Lower levels understand it but rarely use it except when interfacing with the Council.

**Spiral Common** (SC): The language of the residential levels. A creole that evolved over eleven generations, mixing fragments of the original settlers' languages with technical jargon that drifted down from the engineering levels. It's concrete, metaphorical, relationship-focused. Where TS says "atmospheric allocation," SC says "the breath count." Where TS says "system optimization," SC says "who gets to breathe."

Code-switching marks power and intimacy:
- **TS → SC**: A Council member trying to connect with lower levels (usually condescending)
- **SC → TS**: A lower-level resident interfacing with the Council (performing compliance)
- **Pure SC**: Intimacy, solidarity, trust (only among equals)
- **Pure TS**: Institutional authority, impersonal power

**Examples:**

*Kael reading a Council directive (TS):*
"Level 42 atmospheric allocation for Cycle 8: 380 hours. Variance attributable to thermal load optimization and system capacity constraints."

*Kael translating for his daughter (SC):*
"We've got the breath count for next month. Three-eighty. Means we're gonna feel it around day twenty."

*Senna talking to a colleague (SC):*
"You hear about the Hammer dropping on 39? They pushed too hard on that repair request. Council cut their count by forty hours."

*Senna filing a maintenance request (TS):*
"Request atmospheric vent inspection, Level 38, Section D. Reduced flow detected. Priority: medium."

*Thane in a Council meeting (TS):*
"Recommend allocation adjustment for Levels 40-47. Current thermal load projections indicate capacity for reduction without compromising baseline survivability."

*Thane to a lower-level delegation (TS → SC attempt):*
"I understand your concerns about the breath count. We're doing everything we can within system constraints."

The switch signals who you're talking to and whether you're performing. Pure SC is the language of truth-telling among people who trust each other. TS is the language of the system — you use it when you need something from power, or when you are power.

### Constraint Reference Table

**C₁ (Environmental Instability) → The Breath Ceiling**

The monthly variation in atmospheric allocation. From any position, it appears as environmental fact — the system's capacity fluctuates, and the Council manages what's available. The allocation formula is opaque, the pattern unpredictable.

- **Kael experiences it as:** Unchangeable terrain. The air thins mid-month, every month. You can't fight it, you can only adapt — sleep more, work slower, wait for the reset. The idea that the Council could stabilize it feels like wishing for gravity to reverse.

- **Vex experiences it as:** Environmental property with administrative overlay. The base system has constraints (aging equipment, limited capacity), and the Council optimizes within those constraints. The variation is real physics, managed as well as possible.

- **Thane experiences it as:** System management necessity. The processors have finite capacity, and demand varies. The allocation adjusts to prevent cascade failures. It's not political — it's thermodynamics.

**Plot shift (TR₄ - Time Horizon Extension):** Kael gains access to the original engineering reports (via Vex or a data leak). He discovers the system was designed for stable allocation — the variation was introduced deliberately three generations ago. The Breath Ceiling isn't environmental physics; it's policy. His classification shifts from Mountain (unchangeable) to Snare (constructed trap). But he still can't change it — the Council has enforcement capacity, and he's still trapped on Level 42.

---

**C₂ (Exclusionary Coordination) → The Spiral Contract**

The informal network of reciprocal obligations that coordinates life in the residential levels. It provides genuine value — resource sharing, mutual aid, collective problem-solving. But it only works horizontally, between people at similar depths. Upper levels don't participate because they don't need to.

- **Kael experiences it as:** Hybrid system. The Contract helps him survive (neighbors lend hours, share resources, cover for each other), but it doesn't address the root problem (the Council's allocation system). He can see that upper levels don't participate — they get the coordination benefits (stable society) without contributing. It works, but it's not fair.

- **Vex experiences it as:** Functional coordination. The Contract is how the lower levels self-organize. It's impressive, actually — emergent order without central planning. She doesn't participate because she doesn't need to (her allocation is stable), but she respects it as a system.

- **Senna experiences it as:** Pure coordination. The Contract is how people survive together. She doesn't see the asymmetry (upper levels benefiting without contributing) because she doesn't interact with upper levels. From her position, it's just mutual aid.

- **Thane experiences it as:** Informal efficiency. The Contract reduces the burden on the Council by handling small-scale coordination. It's a useful supplement to the formal systems. He doesn't see extraction because from his position, there is none — the Contract costs him nothing and stabilizes the lower levels.

**Plot shift (TR₁ - Collective Organization):** Ren and Davi attempt to formalize the Contract — create explicit rules, expand it vertically, demand upper-level participation. If successful, this would shift their experience from Tangled Rope (hybrid) to Rope (pure coordination). But the attempt is blocked by C₂ itself: the Contract's informal nature is what makes it work. Formalizing it would require Council recognition, which would require explaining why it's needed, which would require admitting the allocation system is unjust. The Council responds with the Hammer (allocation cuts), and the organization attempt collapses.

---

**C₃ (Arbitrary Enforcement) → The Hammer**

The Council's selective enforcement of maintenance denials and allocation cuts. Not every complaint is punished — just enough to maintain fear. The pattern is unpredictable, the consequences severe.

- **Kael experiences it as:** Lethal trap. Any action might trigger the Hammer. Organizing is dangerous. Speaking out is dangerous. Even submitting too many maintenance requests feels dangerous. He's trapped (can't leave the silo) and powerless (can't predict or prevent enforcement). The Hammer takes everything — allocation cuts mean his daughter breathes less, maintenance denials mean the leak keeps wasting water.

- **Ren & Davi experience it as:** Same trap, but they're trying to map it. They document which actions trigger enforcement, looking for a pattern they can route around. So far: no pattern. The Hammer is genuinely arbitrary.

- **Thane experiences it as:** Necessary discipline. The Council can't approve every request or tolerate constant complaints. Selective enforcement maintains order without requiring constant intervention. From his position, it's efficient — the threat of the Hammer coordinates behavior better than actual punishment would.

**Plot shift (TR₃ - Enforcement Escalation):** After Ren and Davi's organization attempt, Thane recommends "allocation adjustment to restore system stability." Levels 40-47 see cuts of 40-60 hours. From Thane's position, this is a measured response to disruption (χ becomes more negative — he benefits more). From Kael's position, this is intensified extraction (χ increases from 1.08 to 1.14 — the trap tightens). Same action, opposite experiences.

### Transformation Narratives

**TR₁: Collective Organization (Blocked)**

Ren and Davi's plan: formalize the Spiral Contract into a "Residential Commons" with explicit rules and vertical expansion. If lower levels coordinate, they could demand stable allocation, transparent maintenance prioritization, Council accountability.

The attempt requires overcoming C₂'s own structure: the Contract works because it's informal, horizontal, and invisible to the Council. Formalizing it makes it visible. Expanding it vertically requires upper-level buy-in, but upper levels don't need it.

The Council's response: "We appreciate residents' self-organization efforts. However, formalized resource-sharing networks must be registered and approved to ensure system compatibility. Unapproved networks may interfere with critical infrastructure coordination."

Translation: organizing is now a maintenance violation. Levels 40-47 see allocation cuts. Ren and Davi's compartment gets flagged for "systems audit" (the Hammer). The organization dissolves before it starts.

**Structural note:** The constraint that would be transformed (C₂) is the same constraint that blocks transformation. The Contract can't become a Rope (pure coordination) without Council recognition, but seeking Council recognition triggers C₃ (the Hammer).

---

**TR₂: Exit Attempt (Blocked)**

Kael considers leaving Level 42 — applying for a transfer to the workshop levels (better allocation) or even the technical levels (stable allocation). But transfers require Council approval, and the criteria are opaque. His maintenance background should qualify him, but his history of "excessive maintenance requests" flags him as a complainer.

Even if he got approval, leaving means abandoning his neighbors, breaking the Spiral Contract bonds that keep his daughter fed. And it wouldn't change the system — just move him from the extracted position to the extracting position.

The deeper block: where would he go outside the silo? The surface is (officially) uninhabitable. The sensors are broken. No one knows what's out there. Exit from the system means exit from breathable atmosphere.

**Structural note:** Exit attempt doesn't change χ (power position unchanged), but it reveals the trap's depth. The silo is the world. There is no outside.

---

**TR₄: Time Horizon Extension (Revelation)**

Vex finds the original engineering reports in the archives. She brings them to Kael (motivated by guilt, curiosity, or genuine solidarity — the reason matters less than the action). The reports show:

1. The atmospheric system was designed for 720 hours/level, stable allocation
2. The variation was introduced in Year 2847 (three generations ago) as "temporary optimization during equipment failure"
3. The equipment was repaired in Year 2851
4. The variation was never reversed
5. Current system has 30% excess capacity

The Breath Ceiling isn't physics. It's policy. It's maintained by choice.

Kael's classification shifts: C₁ moves from Mountain (environmental fact) to Snare (constructed trap). But his material position doesn't change — he's still on Level 42, still powerless, still trapped. The revelation changes what he knows, not what he can do.

The question becomes: what does he do with this knowledge? Share it (risk the Hammer)? Stay silent (complicity)? Attempt reform (blocked by C₂ and C₃)?

This is where the story lives.

## SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

### RESOLVED

**Ω_E01: Atmospheric system technical plausibility**
- **Question:** Can a closed-system atmospheric processor actually vary allocation by level without physical cause?
- **Resolution:** Yes, if the system uses zone-based distribution with manual valve control. The processors generate breathable air centrally; distribution is controlled by flow regulators on each level. The Council adjusts the regulators monthly. Variation is policy, not physics.
- **Impact:** Establishes that C₁ can appear as Mountain (environmental) while actually being Snare (constructed). The technical plausibility grounds the False Mountain reveal.

**Ω_E02: Silo population sustainability**
- **Question:** Can 3,200 people survive in a sealed silo for eleven generations?
- **Resolution:** Yes, with hydroponics (food), geothermal (power), and closed-loop water recycling. Real-world closed-system life support (ISS, Biosphere 2) confirms feasibility at smaller scales. Eleven generations is ~275 years — long enough for institutional drift, short enough for original documentation to survive.
- **Impact:** Grounds the setting in plausible engineering constraints. The silo's systems are old and partially understood, which naturalizes both the Council's power (they maintain critical systems) and the Breath Ceiling (they control distribution).

**Ω_C01: "Breathable hours" as allocation metric**
- **Question:** Why measure in hours rather than volume, pressure, or quality?
- **Resolution:** Hours are legible to residents (you feel the difference day-to-day) and obscure to non-engineers (you can't verify the math). This asymmetry serves the Council — residents can't audit allocation without technical knowledge. Hours also create temporal scarcity (countdown pressure) rather than spatial scarcity (rationing).
- **Impact:** The metric itself is a control mechanism. It's specific enough to feel scientific, vague enough to resist verification.

**Ω_C02: Why doesn't the silo have windows/sensors to verify surface conditions?**
- **Question:** If the surface is uninhabitable, why can't residents verify this?
- **Resolution:** The sensors failed six generations ago (Year 2823). Repairing them would require surface access, which requires knowing surface conditions, which requires working sensors (circular trap). The Council maintains the "toxic surface" narrative without evidence. This creates existential uncertainty that reinforces the silo as total world.
- **Impact:** Blocks TR₂ (exit attempt) at the conceptual level. There is no verified outside. The silo might be a refuge or a prison — no one knows.

**Ω_P01: Should the Spiral Contract have a formal origin story?**
- **Question:** Did someone invent it, or did it emerge organically?
- **Resolution:** Emergent. No founding moment, no charter, no explicit rules. It evolved over generations as people solved local problems (share food during allocation crunches, lend space during maintenance outages). This makes it genuinely bottom-up, which is why it works horizontally but can't scale vertically (upper levels never needed it, so never developed the habits).
- **Impact:** Naturalizes C₂'s asymmetry. The Contract isn't exclusionary by design — it's exclusionary by structural position. Upper levels aren't invited because they were never in the conditions that created it.

**Ω_P02: Linguistic register — how much SC vocabulary to invent?**
- **Question:** Should SC have extensive unique vocabulary, or mostly be grammatical/metaphorical shifts?
- **Resolution:** Mostly grammatical and metaphorical. SC uses TS technical terms but repurposes them: "breath count" (allocation), "the Hammer" (enforcement), "the Ceiling" (system limit). This shows SC as a creole — it doesn't replace TS, it reinterprets it. Extensive unique vocabulary would make SC feel like a conlang rather than a living vernacular.
- **Impact:** Code-switching becomes legible to readers (they can follow both registers) while still marking power and intimacy. The metaphors do the work — "breath count" vs. "atmospheric allocation" signals not just language but worldview.

**Ω_P03: Should Thane be a villain?**
- **Question:** Is Thane consciously extracting, or does he genuinely believe the system is necessary?
- **Resolution:** Genuine belief. Thane is not lying when he says the Council is doing the best it can. From his position (institutional, negative χ), the system looks like functional coordination. He sees the lower levels' complaints as ignorance of constraints, not recognition of extraction. This makes him more dangerous than a conscious villain — he's structurally incapable of seeing the harm.
- **Impact:** Preserves indexical variance as the conflict engine. Thane isn't evil; he's in a position where extraction looks like coordination. The tragedy is that he's acting rationally from his index.

**Ω_P04: Timeline — when does the story take place relative to the Breath Ceiling's introduction?**
- **Question:** Is this early (people still remember stable allocation) or late (it's been normalized)?
- **Resolution:** Late. The Breath Ceiling was introduced three generations ago (Year 2847). Current year is 2891 (44 years later). No one alive remembers stable allocation. This is the world Kael was born into. The revelation (TR₄) isn't "they changed it" but "it was never supposed to be this way."
- **Impact:** Deepens the False Mountain pattern. The constraint has been naturalized across two generations. Kael doesn't think "the Council took something from us" — he thinks "this is how air works." The revelation is more shocking because the Mountain appears so solid.

### UNRESOLVED

**Ω_P05: What triggers Vex to share the engineering reports with Kael?**
- **Question:** What motivation makes an upper-level resident break institutional loyalty?
- **Recommendation:** Three options:
  1. **Personal connection:** Vex has a relationship with someone on the lower levels (family, romance, friendship) that makes the abstraction concrete.
  2. **Intellectual honesty:** Vex values truth over comfort. She discovers the reports during routine archival work and can't unsee them.
  3. **Structural accident:** Vex shares the reports without realizing their significance. She thinks she's answering a technical question; Kael realizes the implications.
- **Awaiting decision:** This choice affects Vex's character arc. Option 1 makes her morally sympathetic. Option 2 makes her intellectually principled. Option 3 makes her structurally complicit (she had the information but didn't see it). All three are valid — depends on whether the story is about moral choice, intellectual courage, or structural blindness.

**Ω_C03: Does the silo have a governing philosophy/ideology?**
- **Question:** Do residents have a shared belief system that naturalizes the hierarchy, or is it purely material?
- **Recommendation:** Minimal ideology. The Council doesn't need a philosophy beyond "we keep you alive." The hierarchy is naturalized through daily experience (you breathe better as you ascend) rather than through doctrine. This makes the system feel more like physics than politics, which is exactly what False Mountains do.
- **Awaiting decision:** If ideology exists, it should be vestigial (remnants of the original emergency justification) rather than active propaganda. Active propaganda would make the extraction too visible.

**Ω_E03: What's actually on the surface?**
- **Question:** Is the surface truly uninhabitable, or is that a lie?
- **Recommendation:** Leave ambiguous. The story doesn't need to resolve this. The point is that no one knows, and the uncertainty keeps people trapped. If the surface is habitable, the silo is a prison. If it's uninhabitable, the silo is a refuge with an unjust internal structure. Either way, the Breath Ceiling is still extraction.
- **Awaiting decision:** Resolving this would shift genre (toward escape thriller or revolution plot). Leaving it ambiguous keeps focus on the internal constraint dynamics.
