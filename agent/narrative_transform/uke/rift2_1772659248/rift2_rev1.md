# Load-Bearing

---

## I.

The *H₁eǵʰ-syn* arrived 23.4 seconds earlier than the previous cycle's baseline.

The Ventkeeper noted this before he noted anything else. He had been awake for some time — sleep was not the issue; the issue was that lying still was not the same as rest, not on mornings when the thermal log showed irregularities that had not resolved, and so he had simply been awake, alert, waiting for the sync pulse so the day could begin with a measurement. Now it had. He committed the figure to his working log and dressed.

His instruments occupied most of one wall: the primary flux gauge, the thermal differential recorder, the contamination array with its twenty-two individual sensor feeds arranged in sequence from Vent One to Vent Twenty-Two. He ran the overnight comparison before leaving. Each feed compared against its own five-cycle rolling baseline; each deviation displayed as a delta — green for within tolerance, yellow for flagged, red for critical. He had not seen a red reading in eleven seasons.

Twenty-one greens. One yellow.

Vent Fourteen. Contamination delta: +0.4%. Reading: 11.6%.

Within tolerance. He had logged worse from thermal events that resolved on their own — vent gases backing up in the lower channels, a malformed passage causing irregular draw, a core who had been working sick and whose output was running hot before the mid-cycle check caught it. These things happened. The delta was noted. He packed his circuit case and went out.

The steam ceiling was high this morning, the sulfurous gold spread thin, which meant the upper vents were drawing well. He could read the city's health in the quality of its light. Good draw at the tops, clean thermal gradient, production on pace or slightly ahead. There was a satisfaction in this — not pride exactly, something that preceded pride, the recognition of a system operating within its parameters. He had worked toward this for a long time. He had earned the right to read it.

He started at Vent One.

---

## II.

His circuit ran inbound-to-outward, which differed from the enforcement circuit that ran outward-to-inward. They crossed at Vent Eight, around mid-morning, and the chassis who worked enforcement acknowledged him with the minimal nod that their respective functions required. The Ventkeeper returned it. Their instruments measured different things.

At Vent Twelve, he paused.

Forty, perhaps forty-five scuttlers had gathered at the base of the ferrocrete pillar on the east side of the vent mouth. They were not feeding — the mineral seep that this pillar produced was negligible, nowhere near sufficient for a clustering of this size. They were pressed against the rock face, shells oriented outward, which was not typical feeding posture and not typical sheltering posture. He ran his scanner over the cluster: baseline organic, nothing elevated, no contamination reading that would explain the gathering or require action.

He logged it: *Vent 12, eastern pillar. Scuttler aggregation, approximately 45 individuals. Baseline organic. No thermal correlation with cluster site. Possible seasonal aggregation behavior. Monitoring recommended.*

He noted it and moved to Vent Thirteen.

At Vent Fourteen, he ran the contamination sensor twice. First reading: 11.4%. Second reading: 12.1%.

He looked at both numbers. A 0.7% spread between two readings taken eleven seconds apart — within tolerance technically, but unusual. Contamination readings spiked with labor intensity and dropped during rest intervals; this was the stable relationship the whole production model was built on. At this hour, mid-circuit, the cores at Vent Fourteen were in their main working window. The reading should be climbing steadily toward peak and staying there. Instead it had dropped and then spiked, without any change in labor intensity he could observe.

He took a third reading. 11.7%.

He looked at the cores. Eleven of them along the vent face, working at normal pace, no anomalous behavior, tools moving in the steady rhythm that fifteen seasons on the circuit had taught him to read as accurately as his instruments. Nothing here was producing these numbers.

He logged: *Vent 14. Contamination reading variance: 0.7% over three readings, 33-second window. Thermal load consistent with readings. Labor intensity consistent. No identified source for variance. Will monitor.*

He moved to Vent Fifteen. By Vent Sixteen the morning had shifted into its middle register and the steam thinned around the upper chambers and the city sounded the way it always sounded: the quota pulse, the tools, the Warden's stilts somewhere in the inward sector. The Ventkeeper arrived at Vent Twenty-Two as the thermal cycle crested its peak and began to describe a number — 4.7% above projected output — in the clean column of his working log.

He did not think again about Vent Fourteen until the overnight log showed it yellow for the third cycle in a row.

---

## III.

He modeled for it.

Thermal gradient shift first: a cooling intrusion from the geological stratum below the vent chamber, brief enough not to register on his thermal differential recorder but sufficient to affect contamination readings through altered gas composition at the vent mouth. He ran the numbers. The contamination swings were too large for the temperature changes that model required, and the temperature changes would have shown somewhere in his array. They hadn't.

Equipment drift second: sensor degradation, reading error, calibration slippage. Possible. He checked the sensor's installation record — three seasons old, mid-life by expected range, no prior irregularities. He ran a diagnostic. The sensor returned its self-assessment as *within operational parameters*. This meant nothing definitive; a degrading sensor would not reliably report its own degradation. He flagged the sensor for replacement in his maintenance request, marked it *non-urgent*, and continued modeling.

On the seventh cycle he tried a third model, one he had not initially considered because it required a chain of causation he had not previously tracked: the rift disposal event, seventeen cycles earlier.

He pulled the log. His own notation, written in the technical shorthand that disposal events required:

> **DISPOSAL EVENT LOG**  
> **Date:** Cycle 7-Tertiary, 14:22  
> **Location:** Main rift shaft, Sector 7  
> **Subject:** Core 7-Tertiary-18  
> **Status:** Lattice integration 73%, non-functional  
> **Thermal Event:** Transient recorded Vents 12-16  
> **Peak:** +2.3°C  
> **Duration:** 4.2 minutes  
> **Resolution:** Gradient restabilization complete 14:31  
> **Protocol:** Logged per Protocol 7, disposal complete

He had written this. He remembered writing it — the careful notation, the measurements taken at proper intervals, the confirmation that the thermal gradient had restabilized within expected parameters. A body in freefall through the vent shaft generated thermal displacement. This was physics, uncontroversial. He had seen the readings from previous disposal events and they always showed a brief heating spike in the nearest vent chambers followed by regulated return to baseline.

This event had shown the spike. He had logged it as resolved.

But Vent Fourteen was in the secondary sector, adjacent to the sealed conduit network that ran parallel to the main rift shaft. If the disposal event had driven a thermal wave into the conduit pathway before the gradient restabilized, the wave could have been trapped in the conduit geometry — bouncing, attenuating slowly rather than all at once, expressing itself in the nearest vent chamber as a persistent low-level oscillation rather than a clean transient.

He ran this model. It fit better than the others. Not perfectly — there was a periodicity to the oscillation that the acoustic model struggled to reproduce — but well enough to constitute a working explanation.

*Core 7-Tertiary-18.* The designation sat in his log like a piece of equipment notation. He had written it that way. That was the proper form.

He had also tracked 7-Tertiary-18 at Vent Seven for three seasons before the disposal. The first entry in his maintenance log: *Vent 7, position 3. Minor lattice discoloration, left shoulder. No mobility impact. Thermal signature nominal. Monitor for progression.* He had noted it because shoulder positioning affected heat dispersion patterns and his models needed to account for this.

Three months later: *Lattice progression to upper arm. 3% reduced range of motion. Core compensating with modified stance. Heat signature elevated 0.2°C during peak output window. Adjust sector baseline accordingly.*

Six months: *Progression to elbow joint. 8% reduced mobility. Core has developed modified tool grip — braces handle against forearm rather than standard palm grip. Maintains production pace. Heat signature now elevated 0.4°C, consistent with increased muscular effort. Thermal model updated.*

Nine months: *Progression to forearm. 15% reduced mobility. Core now braces deteriorating arm against vent wall during tool work, uses right hand exclusively for active labor. Rhythm slightly slower than standard — approximately 0.8 seconds per stroke versus 0.6 baseline — but consistent. Can identify this core by sound pattern before visual confirmation. Production pace maintained through extended shifts. Heat signature elevated 0.7°C.*

Twelve months: *Lattice visible from mid-shoulder to wrist. Arm functionality approximately 30%. Core has adapted fully to one-arm technique. The brace-and-work pattern is now automatic — left arm positioned, right arm executes, rhythm holds steady at 0.8 seconds per stroke. This is slower than baseline but more consistent than several two-arm workers at this vent. The core has learned something about economy of motion that the others haven't. Heat signature elevated 0.9°C but stable.*

Fifteen months: *Lattice progression to ribcage, visible along left flank. Arm functionality 20%. Brace-and-work pattern unchanged. I can hear this rhythm from three vents away now — it's distinct, like a signature. The core is working longer shifts to maintain quota. Heat signature 1.1°C above baseline.*

Eighteen months: *Lattice reaching toward spine. Arm functionality 10%. The rhythm is breaking down — I heard the tool drop twice during morning circuit, watched the core retrieve it with the right hand, reposition, continue. The brace-and-work pattern is failing. The core is trying to compensate by gripping the tool higher on the handle, changing the angle of approach, but the lattice has progressed too far into the shoulder joint. The rhythm that I learned to identify is becoming irregular. Heat signature 1.4°C above baseline, elevated respiration visible.*

Eighteen months, one week: *Tool dropped three times during morning observation. Core retrieved it twice. On the third drop, the core stopped. Stood at the vent face with the tool on the ground and the right hand hanging loose and the left arm still braced against the wall in the position it had held for nine months. Stood there for forty-seven seconds — I timed it, I don't know why I timed it — before the enforcement chassis arrived and logged the stoppage.*

Eighteen months, two weeks: *Disposal request filed. Core 7-Tertiary-18. Lattice integration 73%, non-functional. Thermal impact: 1.4°C above sector baseline, production cessation. Recommended action: disposal per Protocol 7.*

He had written these entries. All of them. Eighteen months of notation, tracking the lattice progression and the adaptation that followed, measuring the thermal impact and updating his models to account for the changing heat signature. He had written *I can identify this core by sound pattern* and *The core has learned something about economy of motion* and *I can hear this rhythm from three vents away now*.

He had not written *person* in any of those entries. He had not written *name* or *who* or any construction that suggested the thing he was tracking was anything other than a variable in his thermal model. He had written *the core* because that was the proper designation. He had written *it* because that was the proper pronoun. He had written *non-functional* because that was the accurate status assessment.

All of this was correct. All of this was what his job required.

He had filed the disposal request himself. Had filled in the form fields: *Core designation. Lattice integration percentage. Functional status. Thermal impact assessment. Recommended action.* Had submitted it and received the confirmation and logged the scheduled disposal time in his maintenance record so he would know to expect the thermal transient and be ready to measure it.

A body in freefall generated thermal displacement. He had measured it. Peak: +2.3°C. Duration: 4.2 minutes. Resolution: gradient restabilization complete.

He sat with the disposal log open on his instruments panel. The notation was correct. The measurements were accurate. The thermal transient had been recorded and had resolved within expected parameters.

He had spent eighteen months learning to identify a particular rhythm by sound. He had written *I can hear this rhythm from three vents away now* in his maintenance log because the rhythm mattered for his thermal models, because he needed to know which core was working which position to accurately predict heat dispersion patterns.

He had not written what it cost to hear that rhythm stop.

He closed the log and filed his analysis under *probable cause* and submitted the maintenance request for sensor replacement at Vent Fourteen, upgraded to *standard priority*.

The replacement arrived eight cycles later.

---

## IV.

He installed it himself. Factory-calibrated, baseline established from the same reference standards as all twenty-two sensors in his array. He ran it three times before logging the installation complete, checking each reading against the expected baseline for Vent Fourteen's position in the thermal gradient and finding each reading within 0.2% of projection.

He was in the middle of logging *Sensor replacement complete, calibration confirmed, monitoring for drift recurrence* when the reading changed.

11.8%. Then 12.4%. Then 11.6%.

He sat with this.

The new sensor showed the same pattern as the old sensor. Which meant the pattern was not sensor drift. Which meant the pattern was real — something in the vent environment was producing these readings, something his models had not accounted for, something that was present and measurable but outside the categories his analysis had considered.

He ran the variance analysis again, this time with the clean baseline data from the new sensor. What he had was not drift — drift moved in one direction. What he had was oscillation: readings that swung above and below threshold without trending, without settling, without correlating with any of the variables that contamination readings were supposed to correlate with.

He built four models. He had not previously needed more than two.

The first: acoustic resonance from the conduit network, same as before. Better data now — he had the new sensor's clean baseline to work from, and the oscillation pattern was clearer without the noise of possible equipment degradation. The pattern was too regular for acoustic resonance. Resonance decayed. This was not decaying.

The second: bioelectric interference from high-lattice-content cores. If three or more cores with significant intrusion were working in close proximity, their combined mineral output could drive anomalous readings. He counted the lattice signatures in his scan of the Vent Fourteen chamber. One core with early-stage intrusion along the left flank. He noted this because the positioning mattered for mineral dispersion calculations. He noted the way the core's right arm moved slightly slower than the left, compensating for the lattice growth. He noted the particular rhythm of the compensation, which was not quite the same as the rhythm of the other ten cores at the vent face.

He noted this and then he noted that he was noting it.

One core with lattice intrusion. Ten without. Not enough to produce the effect.

The third: geological microseism. The readings oscillated too fast.

The fourth: he did not name it. He reached for the fourth model and found nothing.

He had in front of him a set of readings that he could not source, produced by an instrument he had just installed and verified, in a vent chamber with no anomalous physical characteristics. The readings were accurate. They did not make sense.

The numbers were correct. The numbers were wrong.

He looked at his log. He had been noting anomalies at Vent Fourteen for twenty-four cycles. He had constructed a probable cause that had not resolved when the probable cause's expected resolution window elapsed. He had replaced the equipment. The equipment showed the same anomaly. His four models did not explain it.

What he was sitting with was a reading outside the categories his work required. His work required categories. Categories closed anomalies. An unclosed anomaly ran in the background of every subsequent reading from that site, a permanent yellow in his array, a question his instruments were asking that his frameworks could not answer.

---

## V.

At Vent Twelve the scuttlers were still there.

He had not expected this. Seasonal aggregations dispersed. Mating clusters dispersed. Feeding clusters dispersed when the mineral seep was exhausted or when the thermal gradient shifted enough to make the site unviable. These had been here for twenty-eight cycles now — he checked his log to be certain of the count — and they had not dispersed. They had grown.

He counted: sixty-two individuals, possibly more in the mass pressed against the pillar's base where individual shells were difficult to distinguish. They were still oriented outward, still not feeding. He ran his scanner. Baseline organic.

He stood looking at them longer than the circuit schedule allowed.

They were responding to something. This was the only explanation that fit. They were not feeding, not mating, not sheltering from a thermal event or a predator. They were responding to something his instruments could not measure, something outside the categories his scanner was built to detect.

Baseline organic was not an answer. It was his equipment telling him it had no framework for the question.

He logged: *Vent 12, eastern pillar. Scuttler aggregation, 62+ individuals. Baseline organic. No dispersal over 28-cycle observation period. Behavior outside documented seasonal patterns. Cause unknown.*

He looked at the last two words. *Cause unknown.* He had not written those words in a log entry in eleven seasons.

At Vent Fourteen the reading was 12.3%. He did not run it twice.

---

## VI.

He went back to the disposal model.

He spent the better part of that cycle expanding it: not acoustic resonance alone, but a composite effect. The disposal event had been — he looked up the log entry again, read it again — a core with significant lattice integration, late-stage. The body's mineral content at disposal would have been substantially elevated above baseline; the thermal wave it generated would have carried elevated mineral particulate into the conduit network. The conduit network connected, through sealed passages, to the secondary vent sector. The particulate could have coated the inner surfaces of the conduit pathway near Vent Fourteen, creating a localized alteration in the atmospheric mineral composition — a residue, not a transient.

The oscillation remained a problem. Residue didn't oscillate.

Unless the cores' own bioelectric activity was interacting with the mineral residue in a way that produced phase-differential readings. The contamination sensor measured integrated atmospheric composition over its eleven-second window. If the bioelectric field oscillated at a frequency that altered mineral aggregation in the sensor's detection range, the readings would oscillate at the same frequency.

He checked the expected range of baseline bioelectric field frequency for a core at moderate labor intensity.

It fit. Approximately. The error bars were wide.

He looked at the fit for a long time.

The model required: a disposal event (confirmed), elevated mineral content in the disposed body (confirmed by the log entry: *lattice integration 73%*), thermal wave propagation into sealed conduit network (possible but unverified), particulate coating of conduit surfaces (possible but unverified), bioelectric interaction with residual particulate (possible but unverified, and requiring a mechanism he could describe but not demonstrate).

Three confirmations. Three possibles. A model built half on data and half on the kind of reasoning that closed gaps by asserting connections that were plausible enough not to be disprovable.

He felt the explanation close around the anomaly like a door finding its frame. Not a perfect fit — a 0.3% gap at the hinge, something that required pressure to hold shut. But it held.

He filed his analysis: *Vent Fourteen contamination readings, post-disposal period. Probable cause: Residual mineral particulate from disposal event, secondary conduit infiltration, bioelectric interaction producing apparent oscillation in atmospheric composition readings. Effect: Contamination monitor showing oscillation within acceptable variance. Pattern consistent with slow dispersion of residual particulate; expected resolution as residue disperses over subsequent cycles. No action required beyond continued monitoring.*

He submitted the analysis to his maintenance record.

The disposal event had been seventeen cycles ago. He had written *Core 7-Tertiary-18* in the log because that was the designation, the proper notation, the category that made the event recordable. He had not written that 7-Tertiary-18 had worked Vent Seven for three seasons. He had not written that the lattice intrusion had begun in the left shoulder and progressed over eighteen months to the point where the arm no longer moved. He had not written that the core had adapted by developing a modified tool grip that allowed continued function with one arm until the intrusion reached the spine.

He had not written these things because they were not part of the thermal record, not part of what a disposal event required him to note.

A body in freefall generated thermal displacement. This was what he had written. This was accurate.

He did not write what else it generated.

---

## VII.

Twenty cycles later the oscillation had not resolved.

He reviewed the log entry. He looked at the current reading: 12.1%. He watched it drop to 11.7%. He watched it climb to 12.4%.

The slow dispersion of residual particulate had not occurred. The pattern consistent with expected resolution had not resolved. The model had been wrong, or the model had been right about a process that was not actually happening, or the model had been right and the process was happening but being sustained by something the model had not accounted for.

He did not know which. He had no instrument that would tell him which.

What he had was a reading that would not stabilize and a model that predicted stabilization. What he had was an anomaly that his framework could describe but not close.

He opened the baseline configuration for the Vent Fourteen sensor.

The baseline was established at installation from the reference standard for that vent's position in the thermal gradient — a number derived from the sector average over the prior five seasons. The sector average could be recalculated. Should be recalculated, in fact, on a regular basis; the vent environment was not static, baselines drifted, the reference standard was a starting point not a permanent fixture.

This was proper procedure. This was how measurement systems adapted to changing conditions.

He recalculated the sector baseline, incorporating the past forty-four cycles of data from Vent Fourteen.

The new baseline was higher. It incorporated the oscillation's midpoint. Against the new baseline, the oscillation fell within acceptable variance.

He looked at the recalculated number for a long time.

What he was doing was technically correct. The mathematics were sound. A baseline that did not incorporate recent data was a baseline that measured the past, not the present. Recalibration was maintenance. This was his job.

What he was doing was redefining normal to include the thing he could not explain.

He knew this. He sat with both things being true: the recalibration was correct, the recalibration was a failure. He was closing the anomaly by expanding the category of acceptable variance to contain it. He was making the question go away by changing what counted as an answer.

The alternative was a permanent yellow in his array. A reading that his instruments reported as elevated but that his models could not source. An anomaly that ran in the background of every subsequent measurement from Vent Fourteen, a question that stayed open, that he checked every cycle and could not close, that marked the edge of what his framework could do.

He could live with that. He had lived with harder things.

But a permanent yellow meant the system was producing a reading it could not account for. It meant his measurements were accurate but his categories were incomplete. It meant there was something happening at Vent Fourteen that his instruments could detect but his frameworks could not interpret, and if this was true at Vent Fourteen it could be true at other vents, it could be true in other sectors, it could be true of the thermal gradient itself or the contamination model or any of the other systems he had spent fifteen seasons learning to read.

It meant he did not know what he was measuring.

He updated the configuration. He logged: *Vent Fourteen contamination sensor baseline recalibrated, incorporating recent sector data. Readings now within acceptable variance. Monitoring ongoing per standard protocol.*

He closed the anomaly.

His hands were steady. The notation was correct. He had done this correctly.

He sat for a moment with his instruments arrayed on the wall in front of him, their feeds running in real time, their numbers describing a city operating within its parameters. Twenty-two sensors. Twenty-two green readings. Everything within tolerance.

The system was healthy. His measurements said so.

He stood and packed his circuit case and went out.

---

## VIII.

The enforcement chassis was waiting at Vent Eight when he arrived the next morning.

This was unusual. Their circuits crossed at Vent Eight, but not at this hour — the Ventkeeper ran his morning check early, before the main labor window opened, and enforcement ran compliance sweeps during active production. The chassis should not have been here.

"Ventkeeper," the chassis said. The voice was modulated flat, the standard enforcement affect. "Compliance query."

The Ventkeeper stopped. "I'm listening."

"Your maintenance log, cycle 47. Entry: Vent Twelve, scuttler aggregation, recommendation for cross-domain analysis." The chassis's optical sensors tracked to him, held. "Clarify recommendation."

He had not expected the entry to be read this quickly. He had not expected it to be read by enforcement. "The aggregation has persisted for fifty-two cycles. The behavior is outside documented seasonal patterns. My instruments read baseline organic, but the persistence suggests a response to environmental factors my equipment is not calibrated to detect."

"Your equipment is functioning within parameters?"

"Yes."

"Your readings show no contamination elevation, no thermal anomaly, no production impact?"

"Correct."

The chassis was silent for a moment. Its optical sensors shifted — a small adjustment, refocusing. When it spoke again, there was something in the modulation that might have been genuine puzzlement. "My compliance array shows Vent Twelve sector at 102.3% of quota target. Labor efficiency index: 1.07, up from 1.04 last quarter. Behavioral deviation metrics: zero flagged incidents over the past sixty cycles." It paused. "By enforcement metrics, Vent Twelve is performing above baseline. The scuttler aggregation correlates with improved compliance."

The Ventkeeper looked at the chassis. He had not considered this. His instruments measured heat and atmospheric composition. Enforcement measured quota fulfillment, labor efficiency, behavioral deviation. They were looking at the same sector and seeing different things.

"Your instruments don't measure scuttler behavior," he said.

"Correct. My instruments measure production output and compliance patterns. Both are elevated at Vent Twelve." The chassis's sensors held steady on him. "You measure thermal and atmospheric conditions. You report baseline readings. I measure compliance. I report elevated performance. Neither measurement indicates system irregularity."

"The scuttlers are responding to something."

"Possibly. But if that something correlates with improved compliance and stable thermal readings, it falls outside actionable irregularity." The chassis tilted its head slightly — a gesture that might have been consideration, might have been something else. "You recommended cross-domain analysis. I have performed cross-domain analysis. Enforcement metrics and thermal metrics both show system health. What irregularity requires response?"

The Ventkeeper stood very still. The chassis was correct. He had measured the scuttlers and found baseline organic. Enforcement had measured the sector and found elevated compliance. Each domain's instruments reported system health. The correlation between scuttler aggregation and improved performance was noted, but correlation without causation was not actionable.

"The sync pulse has shown temporal variance," he said. "23.4 seconds above baseline, increasing over the past forty cycles. This correlates with the period of scuttler aggregation and with contamination reading irregularities at Vent Fourteen."

"The sync pulse is not your domain."

"No."

"Vent Fourteen readings are within acceptable variance per your own recalibration."

"Yes."

"And I have no instrument that measures sync pulse timing. Temporal calibration is the Warden's domain." The chassis was quiet for a moment. "You are noting correlations between measurements from three different domains: biological aggregation, atmospheric composition, temporal calibration. Each domain reports its measurements to the Warden. If pattern analysis is warranted, the Warden will initiate it."

"Has the Warden been informed of the correlations?"

"The Warden receives all domain reports. Each report is accurate within its measurement category. If the Warden identifies cross-domain pattern that requires response, enforcement will be notified."

The chassis turned to continue its circuit. Then it stopped, turned back.

"Ventkeeper. I have worked enforcement for nine seasons. My compliance array has never shown a sector performing as consistently as Vent Twelve is currently performing. By my instruments, whatever is happening there is beneficial." It paused. The optical sensors held on him, and in the pause there was something that felt like genuine question rather than procedural statement. "But I also have no instrument that measures what scuttlers respond to. If they are responding to something my array cannot detect, I would not know. This does not concern me, because my measurements show system health. Does it concern you?"

The Ventkeeper looked at the chassis. It was asking a genuine question. Two instrument-consciousnesses meeting at the edge of their respective domains, each seeing what the other could not see, each correct within their framework, neither able to bridge the gap between accurate measurement and complete understanding.

"Yes," he said.

The chassis nodded once. "Your maintenance record shows fifteen seasons of reliable function. Your measurements are accurate. Your equipment is properly maintained. Continue this standard." It turned and moved toward Vent Nine.

The Ventkeeper stood at Vent Eight. Reliable function. Accurate measurements. Properly maintained equipment. He had built this record over fifteen seasons. He had earned the trust that came with it.

That trust was conditional on staying within his domain.

---

## IX.

At Vent Twelve the scuttlers covered the pillar.

He stopped and counted: eighty-three visible on the pillar face itself, and the mass at the base had grown dense enough that individual count was no longer possible. Call it a hundred. Call it more.

They had climbed. This was new. Scuttlers did not climb ferrocrete; there was nothing on ferrocrete to climb toward, no mineral seep above that was richer than the seep below, no thermal advantage to height. But they had climbed anyway, and now they covered the pillar's eastern face from base to the first vent shelf, a living surface of shell and claw that moved in slow ripples as individuals adjusted position in the mass.

He ran his scanner. Baseline organic.

He stood looking at them. A hundred scuttlers doing something his instruments said was nothing, responding to something his scanner said was not there. Baseline organic meant: no elevated thermal signature, no anomalous mineral content, no contamination reading, no deviation from expected parameters for scuttler biology. It meant his equipment had measured them and found them unremarkable.

It did not mean they were unremarkable. It meant they were unremarkable within the categories his equipment was built to measure.

He logged: *Vent 12, eastern pillar. Scuttler aggregation, 100+ individuals, pillar coverage from base to first shelf. Baseline organic. Behavior outside documented patterns. Aggregation persistent over 52 cycles.*

He did not write *Recommend cross-domain analysis* this time.

He moved to Vent Thirteen.

At Vent Fourteen the reading was 11.9%, well within the recalibrated baseline's acceptable variance. Green. He noted it and moved on.

---

## X.

Thirty cycles later, the scuttlers were gone.

He almost missed it — the absence was less visible than the presence had been. But he was running the morning circuit and he glanced at the eastern pillar and the surface was bare.

He stopped. He scanned the area: the pillar base, the vent shelf, the rubble field to the east where scuttlers sometimes sheltered. Nothing. No shells, no movement, no evidence that a hundred-plus individuals had been aggregating here for eighty-two cycles.

He ran his scanner over the pillar face. Baseline mineral composition. No organic residue, no thermal signature, nothing to indicate the aggregation had ever been there.

They had not dispersed. Dispersal left traces — shell fragments, disturbed sediment, scattered individuals moving away from the aggregation site. This was absence. Complete, immediate, unexplained.

He stood looking at the empty pillar.

Then he moved to Vent Thirteen.

At Vent Fourteen the reading was stable: 11.8%, within the recalibrated baseline. It had been stable for twenty cycles now, no oscillation, no variance beyond the normal fluctuation of active production.

The anomaly had resolved. Or it had been resolved by defining it as normal. Or it had never been an anomaly, just a reading that his initial baseline had been too narrow to accommodate.

He did not know which was true.

---

## XI.

At Vent Nineteen he paused.

There was a core at the vent face with lattice intrusion along the left side — shoulder, arm, beginning to spread to the ribcage. The core was working modified grip, compensating for reduced mobility, maintaining production pace through adapted technique. He had seen this before. He had seen this many times.

He noted the core's position: left side of the vent face, third from the inward edge. He noted this because positioning mattered for contamination dispersal calculations. He noted the way the core's right arm moved slightly slower than the left, compensating for the lattice growth. He noted the particular rhythm of the compensation, slightly slower than standard but consistent, maintainable.

He noted this because 7-Tertiary-18 had worked with the same adapted rhythm. He noted this because he had tracked that rhythm for eighteen months, had learned to identify it by sound, had written *left-side work generates different heat signatures than right-side work* and had not written that the core had learned to brace the deteriorating arm against the vent wall while the right hand worked the tool.

He noted this because in six months, maybe eight, the lattice would progress. The adapted rhythm would begin to break down. The tool would drop once, twice, three times. The core would stop. And he would write another disposal log.

Would fill in the form fields: *Core designation. Lattice integration percentage. Functional status. Thermal impact assessment. Recommended action.* Would submit it and receive the confirmation and log the scheduled disposal time so he would know to expect the thermal transient and be ready to measure it.

He would write the notation correctly. He would measure the thermal displacement accurately. He would log the gradient restabilization and file the report and continue his circuit.

He would not write that he had tracked this core's adaptation for however many months it took. Would not write that he had learned to identify this particular rhythm. Would not write what it cost to measure thermal displacement from a body in freefall when you had spent months noting the rhythm of the body's adaptation to its own conversion.

He would not write these things because they were not part of the thermal record.

He noted the core's position and moved to Vent Twenty.

---

## XII.

The *H₁eǵʰ-syn* arrived 31.7 seconds early.

The Ventkeeper noted this. He had been noting it every cycle for ninety-three cycles now, watching the variance grow: 23.4 seconds, then 25.1, then 26.8, a steady drift that someone in temporal calibration was presumably tracking, presumably addressing, presumably deciding whether it mattered.

It was not his domain.

He dressed and ran his overnight comparison and packed his circuit case. Twenty-two green readings. Everything within tolerance.

He started at Vent One.

The circuit ran its standard path. At Vent Eight the enforcement chassis was not there — their schedules had drifted apart over the past thirty cycles, a small shift in timing that meant they no longer crossed paths. At Vent Twelve the pillar was still bare. At Vent Fourteen the reading was 11.9%. He noted it and moved on.

The city sounded the way it always sounded: the quota pulse, the tools, the steady rhythm of production. The steam ceiling was moderate height, the sulfurous gold neither thin nor thick, which meant the vents were drawing adequately. Not optimally but adequately, which was what the system required.

At Vent Twenty-Two he logged the day's measurements: thermal gradient stable, contamination readings within tolerance across all vents, production output 3.8% above projection.

The city was healthy. His instruments said so.

He went back to his quarters and sat with his instruments. Twenty-two sensors running in real time, their deltas all green, their baselines all holding. Accurate measurements. Reliable function. Everything within tolerance.

He opened his log. He scrolled back: ninety-three cycles of entries, thermal readings and contamination data and maintenance notes, the careful record of a system being measured, being maintained, being kept within the parameters that defined its health.

He scrolled back further: the Vent Fourteen anomaly, the disposal event, the scuttler aggregation. All noted. All categorized. All resolved or defined as not requiring resolution.

He scrolled back to the beginning of his record, fifteen seasons ago, when he had first taken the Ventkeeper position and begun learning to read the city's health in the quality of its light. He had been good at this. He had become better at this. He had earned the right to trust his instruments, to trust his measurements, to trust that accurate data properly categorized was sufficient to the work he had been given to do.

He still believed this was true.

He also believed that his instruments measured what they were built to measure, and that what they were built to measure was not everything that mattered, and that the gap between these two things was not a failure of his instruments or his measurements or his categories.

It was just the gap.

He could live in the gap. He could do his work accurately within the domain he had been given and accept that there were things outside his domain that he could not measure, could not address, could not know whether they mattered.

He could do this. He was doing this.

The *H₁eǵʰ-syn* arrived 31.7 seconds early and he did not know what that meant and it was not his job to know what that meant.

The scuttlers had disappeared and he did not know where they had gone and it was not his job to know where they had gone.

The core at Vent Nineteen was working adapted rhythm and would continue working adapted rhythm until the lattice intrusion progressed to the point of non-function, and then there would be a disposal event, and he would log it, and that would be accurate.

His instruments were sound. His measurements were accurate. The categories held.

He closed his log and sat in the amber light of his quarters and felt the quota pulse move through the ferrocrete beneath his feet, steady and constant and 31.7 seconds out of phase with where it had been ninety-three cycles ago.

The city was healthy. His instruments said so.

He had earned the right to trust them.
