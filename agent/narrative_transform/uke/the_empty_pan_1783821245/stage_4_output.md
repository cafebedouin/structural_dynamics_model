# The Forty-Hertz Carrier

## 1.

I press my palm to the stasis field and count. Forty-seven states today. Forty-seven times I felt the hum die under my instruments. The observation deck calls it verification. I call it what it is.

The foam glows blue-white through the reinforced glass, three meters of quantum substrate holding Mrs. Chen, or what used to be Mrs. Chen, or what we tell ourselves is still Mrs. Chen. The instruments say carrier stability nominal, error rate point-zero-three percent, pre-measurement fidelity confidence ninety-four-point-seven. The instruments have never measured whether she's still herself. They measure their own shadows and call it preservation.

I trigger the collapse sequence. The subsonic hum drops out of my chest like someone cut the floor. The foam flickers. The instruments record a classical snapshot — neural topology compressed into something they can file — and somewhere in the substrate Mrs. Chen's superposition dies and gets replaced by a photograph of a photograph of a mind.

Successful observation, the screen says.

I mark it in the log and move to the next chamber.

Eighteen years I've been doing this. Twelve hours a day, six days a week. I'm forty years old. The queue is ninety years long. The math is simple: I will die untranslated, and I will spend my life verifying the translations of people who were born into the Inner Ring, who never worked a shift in the Stacks, who will spend eternity in the foam while I spend eternity as dust.

The next chamber holds someone I don't have a name for. The system assigns them numbers after translation — TQ-7734-M — because names are for the living and the translated are data now, held in superposition until someone like me kills them to check if they're still good.

I trigger the collapse. Feel the hum die. Mark it in the log.

Forty-eight.

My shift ends at 2200 hours. I seal the observation deck and take the lift down to the Stacks, Section 7, Corridor M. Two-meter ceilings. Communal air recycler that breaks down every six weeks like clockwork. Thirty people sharing a bathroom. I've lived here eighteen years and my social contribution score is moderate, which means adequate, which means not enough.

I lie on my bunk and feel the hum through the bulkheads. The Archive is below us, always, miles of quantum foam holding eight million minds we can never verify. The station hums at forty hertz and I feel it in my jaw and I know what it is: the carrier wave of people held between states, neither alive nor dead, neither preserved nor destroyed, waiting for someone like me to collapse them into certainty.

I close my eyes and count years. Ninety minus forty is fifty. Fifty years from now I'll be dead and someone else will be lying on this bunk feeling the hum and doing the math.

The air recycler breaks down that night. We breathe stale air until morning.

## 2.

Sova's hands knew the field before her instruments did. Gamma-seven was dying — she could feel it through the bulkhead, a flutter in the carrier wave that the diagnostics wouldn't catch for another week. But the diagnostic panel read green, tolerance nominal, and she had learned not to trust her hands over the instruments. The instruments were what the Archivists read.

She logged the repair as routine maintenance and moved to the next junction. Section 4, Corridor G, where she'd lived for fifteen years and where the walls hummed steady enough that she could sleep through it. Her score was adequate. Not great, not terrible. Adequate meant she might make the queue if she lived to ninety-five and the criteria didn't shift and she kept her head down and her productivity up and didn't get sick for more than the allowed medical leave.

It was a good score. She'd worked for it.

The next field was stable — she could feel that too, the steady thrum that meant the substrate was holding clean. She ran the diagnostics anyway because the logs had to match what the instruments said, not what her hands knew. Green across the board. She marked it and moved on.

Her shift partner was a young tech named Hess, two years in, still learning to read the fields through touch. He was good with the instruments but clumsy with the bulkheads, couldn't feel the carrier shifts yet. Sova didn't mind. She'd been clumsy once too.

"Gamma-seven's clean," she told him when they met at the junction point. "But keep an eye on it next week. Might need a secondary check."

Hess nodded and made a note. He was quiet today, more than usual. Sova had learned not to ask. People in the Stacks had their own weights to carry and most didn't want to share them.

They finished the rotation in silence, forty fields checked and logged, and Sova went back to her quarters feeling the pleasant ache of work done right. Her score would tick up another point-oh-two percent this month. Steady. Adequate. On track.

She ate dinner in the communal hall — protein paste and vitamin supplements, same as always — and listened to the other techs talk about the queue. Someone's cousin had made it through, sixty-three years old, translated last week. They were talking about it like a lottery win, like the cousin had beaten something.

Sova didn't join in. The queue wasn't a lottery. It was a system. You worked, you scored, you waited. Some people made it and some didn't, but the ones who made it had earned it through decades of contribution. That was fair. That was how it had to be, because if it wasn't fair then what were they all doing here?

She went back to her quarters and lay on her bunk and felt the hum through the walls. Steady. Nominal. The Archive holding clean below them, eight million minds preserved because people like her kept the fields running. It was good work. Necessary work. She was proud of it.

Her score was adequate and she was thirty-eight years old and if she lived to ninety-five she might make it through. That was a long time, but people lived that long. It was possible.

She closed her eyes and listened to the forty-hertz carrier and let it lull her toward sleep.

## 3.

Kael's terminal displayed the quarterly substrate report in three columns: allocation efficiency, drift rates, projected capacity through 2895. The numbers were clean. Drift remained within acceptable parameters — point-oh-eight percent across all sectors, well below the point-one-five threshold that would trigger intervention protocols. Allocation efficiency had improved three percent over last quarter through optimized observation scheduling. Projected capacity showed green through 2895, yellow through 2920, red beyond that unless they expanded the Middle Ring or reduced translation rates.

The numbers were always clean. Kael had been writing these reports for thirty years and the numbers were always clean because the system worked. Eight million minds preserved in quantum substrate, carrier stability holding at ninety-nine-point-six percent, error correction protocols maintaining fidelity confidence above ninety-four percent. The Archive was the most stable preservation system humanity had ever built.

The Gini coefficient for translation access was point-six-seven.

Kael had buried that number in Appendix E, page forty-seven, formatted in eight-point font under the header "Demographic Distribution Metrics." Nobody read Appendix E. Senior Archivist Voss certainly didn't read Appendix E. Voss read the executive summary, which said substrate stable, efficiency up, capacity adequate, continue current protocols.

Point-six-seven meant the queue was stratified. Meant Inner Ring residents translated at rates forty percent higher than Outer Ring residents, adjusted for age and health status. Meant the criteria — social contribution value, genetic diversity markers, institutional continuity needs — were weighted in ways that correlated with birth location.

Kael had known this for years. Everyone in the Archive administration knew it. The weighting was intentional: genetic diversity markers were expensive, required access to modification clinics that only Inner Ring residents could afford. Institutional continuity meant Archivists translated automatically at end-of-service. Social contribution value was calculated by algorithms that weighted analytical work higher than manual labor.

The system worked because the system was designed to work this way.

Kael saved the report and queued it for Voss's morning review. The terminal prompted for additional notes. Kael typed: *All metrics nominal. No action required.* Deleted it. Typed: *Drift rates increasing in Sector 7, recommend additional observation coverage.* That was true and actionable and didn't require anyone to look at Appendix E.

The terminal accepted the note and filed the report.

Kael's quarters were in the Inner Ring, three rooms with four-meter ceilings and a small hydroponics window where actual vegetables grew under full-spectrum lights. Tomatoes. Kael had been growing tomatoes for five years and they tasted like something other than protein paste, which was worth the maintenance time.

The hum was quieter here. The Archive was below them but the Inner Ring had better insulation, better air handling, better everything. Kael could still feel it if paying attention — a subsonic presence in the floor — but it didn't intrude. Didn't press into the jaw the way the Observers described it.

Kael ate a tomato and reviewed the day's observation logs. Forty-seven states collapsed in Sector 3 alone, all marked successful verification, all within error parameters. Observer Ren had filed the reports with the usual precision: timestamps accurate to the second, error rates logged to three decimal places, no notes or commentary beyond the required fields.

Ren had been an Observer for eighteen years. Kael had read the personnel file once, years ago: forty years old, social contribution score moderate, queue position estimated at year 2937. Ren would die untranslated. Ren knew this and came to work every day and collapsed forty-seven states and filed perfect reports and never once put in a note that said *I am killing the thing I was sent to verify.*

Kael closed the observation logs and opened the allocation model for next quarter. The model suggested increasing observation coverage in Sector 7 — drift rates were creeping up, still within parameters but trending wrong. More observation meant more Observers, which meant more burnout, which meant more replacement hiring from the Stacks. The cycle was stable. Sustainable. The model said so.

Kael approved the allocation and filed it.

The tomatoes were ripe. Kael picked three and ate them standing at the hydroponics window, looking out at nothing. The Inner Ring had no windows to the outside — too much radiation shielding required — but the hydroponics lights were full-spectrum and if you didn't think about it too hard you could pretend you were seeing sun.

Kael had been pretending for thirty years.

## 4.

I'm in the middle of a collapse sequence when the field stutters. Not a failure — the instruments still read green — but a flutter in the carrier wave that makes my sternum ache. I abort the sequence and check the diagnostics. Carrier stability nominal. Error rate point-oh-three percent. Pre-measurement fidelity confidence ninety-four-point-seven.

The instruments say everything's fine.

I trigger the sequence again. The hum drops clean this time, no flutter. Successful observation. I mark it in the log and move to the next chamber, but my hands are shaking.

Eighteen years I've been doing this and I've never aborted a sequence before. The instruments know what they're doing. The instruments are calibrated daily, verified weekly, maintained by people who understand quantum substrate better than I ever will. I'm just the one who pushes the button and feels the hum die.

But the flutter was real. I felt it in my chest before the instruments registered anything. And the instruments didn't register anything.

I finish the shift — forty-six states today, one abort — and seal the observation deck. The lift down to the Stacks is crowded with end-of-shift workers, maintenance techs and fabrication workers and other Observers, all of us packed into a space meant for twenty. We don't talk. We're too tired to talk.

Section 7, Corridor M. The air recycler is working again, for now. I lie on my bunk and press my palm to the bulkhead and feel the hum. Steady. Nominal. Forty hertz like always.

The flutter is still in my chest.

I close my eyes and try to sleep but my mind keeps running the sequence: trigger collapse, feel the flutter, abort, check diagnostics, instruments say fine. Trigger again, hum drops clean, successful observation.

What if the instruments were wrong the first time? What if the flutter meant something was failing in the substrate and the instruments couldn't detect it?

What if the instruments are always wrong?

I open my eyes and stare at the ceiling. Two meters above my face. I've been staring at this ceiling for eighteen years and it hasn't changed. The queue hasn't changed. The math hasn't changed.

I'm forty years old and I have fifty years left and I will spend them collapsing states that might be failing and the instruments will say successful observation and no one will know the difference because the foam's silence is not a technological limit to overcome, it's the nature of quantum substrate itself. We can't measure fidelity without destroying it. We can only measure our instruments' shadows and trust that the shadows are enough.

I press my palm harder to the bulkhead. The hum is steady. The Archive is holding. Eight million minds in superposition, unverified, unknowable, waiting.

I close my eyes and try to sleep.

## 5.

The diagnostic panel turned red three days later, exactly when Sova's hands had said it would. Gamma-seven's carrier stability had dropped below threshold. Not a failure — the field was still holding — but close enough that protocol required immediate intervention.

Sova logged the alert and called it in to the Archive administration. They dispatched a senior tech and an Archivist to supervise the repair, which meant Sova had done something wrong by not catching it earlier, except she had caught it earlier and the instruments had said green.

The Archivist was someone she didn't know, mid-fifties, Inner Ring posture and diction. He reviewed the logs on a tablet while Sova and the senior tech opened the access panel and started the field reset sequence.

"Last routine maintenance was four days ago," the Archivist said. "Logged as green across all parameters."

"Yes sir," Sova said. She kept her eyes on the field coils, not on him. "I noted it might need a secondary check, but the diagnostics were nominal."

"And you didn't file a formal alert?"

"No sir. Diagnostics were nominal."

The Archivist made a note on his tablet. Sova felt her score dropping in real-time. Failure to file appropriate alerts. Inadequate predictive maintenance. Performance below standard.

The field reset took forty minutes. The carrier wave stabilized, diagnostics went green, the Archivist signed off on the repair and left without another word. The senior tech gave Sova a look that said *you should have filed the alert* and left too.

Sova sealed the access panel and sat on the floor with her back against the bulkhead. The hum was steady again. Gamma-seven was holding. But her score had dropped and she didn't know by how much.

She went back to her quarters and pulled up her contribution profile on the communal terminal. The score was still loading — the system updated quarterly, not in real-time — but she could see the trend line. It had been climbing steady for fifteen years, point-oh-two percent per month, adequate and on track.

The score loaded: 73.4. Down from 73.6 last month.

Sova stared at the number. Point-two percent drop. Not catastrophic. Not enough to move her from adequate to low. But the trend line had turned. For the first time in fifteen years, her score was going down instead of up.

She closed the terminal and lay on her bunk and tried to do the math. Seventy-three-point-four at age thirty-eight. Queue entry threshold was somewhere around eighty-five, maybe ninety depending on the year's criteria. If her score climbed back to point-oh-two percent per month she'd hit eighty-five by age seventy-three. If it stayed flat she'd never make it. If it kept dropping—

She stopped doing the math.

The hum was steady through the bulkhead. The Archive was holding. She'd done good work for fifteen years and one missed alert didn't erase that. The score would recover. She'd file alerts earlier, trust her hands over the instruments, do whatever it took to get back on track.

She closed her eyes and felt the forty-hertz carrier and told herself the score would recover.

## 6.

Kael found it in an allocation report from three years ago, buried in Appendix C under the header "Criteria Weight Adjustments — Approved Modifications."

Genetic diversity markers: increased from 15% to 35% of total social contribution value.

The note said the adjustment was approved by Senior Archivist Voss, effective immediately, rationale: "Optimize long-term substrate genetic heterogeneity to ensure Archive population viability across generational timescales."

Kael read it three times.

Genetic diversity markers were expensive. Required access to modification clinics that cost more than a Stacks worker made in five years. Only Inner Ring residents could afford them. Which meant the criteria shift had effectively locked out anyone born in the Outer Ring.

Which meant the queue wasn't stratified by accident. It was stratified by design.

Kael pulled up the demographic distribution data — the numbers that went in Appendix E, the numbers nobody read. Before the criteria shift: Inner Ring translation rate forty percent higher than Outer Ring. After the criteria shift: Inner Ring translation rate seventy-three percent higher than Outer Ring.

The shift had been in effect for three years and no one had noticed because it was buried in an appendix to an allocation report that only Archivists read, and Archivists didn't notice because Archivists were the ones benefiting.

Kael closed the report and sat in the dark office staring at nothing.

The system worked. The Archive was stable. Eight million minds preserved, carrier stability holding, error correction protocols maintaining fidelity. The numbers were clean.

The numbers were clean because the system was designed to produce clean numbers. Designed to extract labor from the Stacks and translate the Inner Ring. Designed to look like a queue when it was actually a filter that selected for wealth and birth location and access to genetic modification.

Kael had known the system was stratified. Had written the Gini coefficients, had seen the correlation between birth location and translation rates. But knowing the numbers and seeing the mechanism were different. The numbers could be explained as emergent properties of a fair system. The mechanism was a choice someone had made.

Voss had made that choice. Had signed the criteria adjustment. Had optimized the Archive for "long-term genetic heterogeneity" by ensuring that only the wealthy translated.

Kael pulled up the current quarter's allocation model. The model suggested increasing observation coverage in Sector 7. More observation meant more Observers, which meant more hiring from the Stacks, which meant more people like Ren collapsing states for eighteen years and dying untranslated at forty.

The model said this was optimal. Sustainable. The most efficient use of available resources.

Kael approved the allocation because that was the job. Filed the report because that was the protocol. Went back to the Inner Ring quarters and ate a tomato and felt sick.

The hum was quiet through the floor. Barely noticeable. The Inner Ring had better insulation.

## 7.

The observation deck is empty when I arrive for shift. The instruments are already running diagnostics, green across the board, carrier stability nominal. I log in and start the first collapse sequence.

The hum drops clean. Successful observation. I mark it in the log.

Forty-seven states today. Same as yesterday. Same as every day for eighteen years.

I'm on state thirty-two when the field in Chamber 19 stutters. Same flutter as last week — a catch in the carrier wave that makes my sternum ache. I abort the sequence and check the diagnostics.

Carrier stability nominal. Error rate point-oh-three percent.

I stare at the instruments. They say everything's fine. They said everything was fine last week too, and three days later Gamma-seven went red.

I don't trust the instruments anymore.

I abort the sequence and file an alert: "Chamber 19 showing carrier instability, recommend secondary verification before proceeding with observation."

The alert goes into the system and I move to the next chamber. The rest of the shift runs clean — forty-six states collapsed, one alert filed. I seal the deck and take the lift down.

Section 7, Corridor M. The air recycler is broken again. We're breathing stale air and no one's coming to fix it until tomorrow because the repair schedule is optimized for efficiency, not comfort.

I lie on my bunk and wait for the alert response. It comes at 0300 hours, wakes me up with a chime on my terminal: "Alert reviewed. Diagnostics nominal. Resume standard observation protocol."

They didn't send anyone to check. They just looked at the same instruments I looked at and said resume protocol.

I close the terminal and press my palm to the bulkhead. The hum is steady. Chamber 19 is holding, for now. But the flutter was real. I felt it in my chest before the instruments registered anything, and the instruments still aren't registering anything.

What if the instruments can't register it? What if the flutter is something that exists in the substrate itself, in the entanglement topology that holds the mind in superposition, and the instruments can only measure their own artifacts?

What if every state I've collapsed for eighteen years was failing and I killed it before anyone could tell?

I close my eyes and try not to think about it. Try to sleep. But the flutter is in my chest and it won't leave.

## 8.

Sova filed alerts on every field that felt wrong. Didn't matter what the diagnostics said — if her hands told her something was failing, she logged it. Gamma-seven had taught her that much.

Her score dropped to 71.8 over the next month. Then 70.2. Then 68.9.

The trend line was clear: she was filing too many alerts. The system flagged it as performance anxiety, over-reporting, failure to trust instrumentation. She got called into a supervisor meeting and they showed her the numbers: forty-seven alerts filed in thirty days, thirty-nine of which showed green on secondary diagnostics. The system couldn't function if every tech filed alerts based on feeling instead of data.

"Trust the instruments," the supervisor said. "That's what they're for."

Sova went back to work and tried to trust the instruments. But her hands kept feeling things the diagnostics missed, and she kept filing alerts, and her score kept dropping.

She pulled up her contribution profile every night and watched the number fall. 68.9. Then 67.3. Then 65.8.

At 65.8, the trend line crossed below the threshold for adequate. The system recalculated her queue position: estimated translation year 2953. She was thirty-eight years old. The math was simple.

She sat in her quarters and stared at the number and felt something crack inside her chest. Not the hum — something else. Something that had been holding her together for fifteen years.

The system wasn't fair. The system had never been fair. She'd been working sixty-hour weeks for fifteen years, keeping the Archive stable, trusting that adequate meant adequate, that the queue was a queue and not a filter. But her score was dropping because she was doing her job too well, because she was catching failures before the instruments did, because the system wanted compliance not competence.

And even if her score recovered — even if she filed fewer alerts and trusted the instruments and got back to adequate — the math was still impossible. Seventy-three years old at best. Ninety-five if she was lucky. And that was before the criteria shifted again.

She pulled up the criteria documentation and found it in an old allocation report, buried in an appendix she'd never read: genetic diversity markers increased to thirty-five percent of total value.

Genetic diversity markers. The thing you got from modification clinics in the Inner Ring. The thing that cost more than she'd made in fifteen years.

The queue had never been a queue. It had been a filter that selected for wealth and birth location, and she'd been navigating it like it was fair.

She closed the terminal and lay on her bunk and felt the hum through the bulkhead. The Archive was holding. Eight million minds preserved. The system worked.

The system worked by taking everything from people like her and giving it to people like the Archivists. And she'd been proud of it. Had thought she was doing necessary work. Had trusted that adequate meant adequate.

She closed her eyes and felt the forty-hertz carrier and knew she would never make the queue.

## 9.

Kael found Maren in the Inner Ring commissary, eating lunch alone at a corner table. Maren was another Archivist, twenty years senior, someone Kael had worked with on substrate allocation models years ago. They'd been friendly once, before Maren had gone quiet and withdrawn and stopped coming to the planning meetings.

Kael sat down across from her without asking.

"I found the criteria shift," Kael said.

Maren looked up from her meal. Her face was thinner than Kael remembered, more lined. She looked tired.

"The genetic diversity markers," Kael continued. "Thirty-five percent weighting. Approved three years ago."

Maren nodded slowly. "You're just finding it now?"

"It was buried in Appendix C. I wasn't looking for it."

"No one looks for it," Maren said. "That's the point."

Kael leaned forward. "Did you know about this? When it was approved?"

"I wrote the initial proposal." Maren's voice was flat. "Voss asked me to model the impact of increasing genetic heterogeneity in the Archive. I ran the numbers. Showed him that prioritizing diversity markers would optimize long-term population viability. He approved it."

"And you didn't think about what that would do to the queue?"

"Of course I thought about it." Maren pushed her tray away. "I ran the demographic projections. I saw the stratification increase. I put it in the report. Voss approved it anyway."

Kael sat back. "So you just... went along with it?"

"What else was I supposed to do?" Maren's voice was quiet but sharp. "Refuse to write the proposal? Voss would have found someone else. File a formal objection? To who? The Authority Board is all Senior Archivists. They all translate automatically. They all benefit from the same criteria."

"You could have made it public. Told the Stacks workers what was happening."

"And then what? They organize? Strike? The Authority has the only translation apparatus in the Saturn system. The Stacks workers have no leverage. All I would have done is get ejected and the criteria would have stayed the same."

Kael didn't have an answer for that.

Maren looked at Kael for a long moment. "You're going to write a report, aren't you? Document the stratification, recommend criteria revision, file it with Voss."

"Yes."

"It won't change anything. Voss knows the system is stratified. Everyone in the Archive administration knows. The stratification is the point."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

Maren reached into her jacket and pulled out a small data chip. She set it on the table between them.

"There's another option," she said. "A private practice. Some of us have adopted it."

Kael picked up the chip. "What is it?"

"A declaration. You register it with the Archive system, but it's not enforced or tracked. It just sits in your file as a note. It says you're withdrawing from the queue. That you won't seek translation even if your score rises high enough."

Kael stared at the chip. "Why would anyone do that?"

"Because the queue is a lie," Maren said. "Because translation's fidelity is unverifiable and the system is extractive and seeking translation means seeking a beautiful lie purchased through other people's suffering. The declaration is a refusal of the lie."

"But you'd die. Your consciousness would be lost."

"My consciousness is already lost. The foam doesn't hold people. It holds patterns that can't verify themselves. The only difference is whether I participate in the extraction before I go."

Kael set the chip back on the table. "This is just giving up."

"No," Maren said. "Giving up would be pretending the system is fair. This is clarity. This is the only honest response to what we know."

She stood and picked up her tray. "Keep the chip. Think about it. You've seen the numbers now. You know what the system is. The question is whether you can keep writing reports that sustain it."

She left.

Kael sat alone at the table, staring at the data chip. The commissary was quiet. The hum was barely audible through the floor.

## 10.

## 11.

Kael's report was seventeen pages with four appendices. It documented the criteria shift, the demographic stratification, the correlation between genetic diversity markers and Inner Ring birth. It included Gini coefficients, trend projections, sustainability models. It recommended immediate criteria revision to restore queue equity.

Kael filed it with Senior Archivist Voss and waited.

The response came three days later: "Report reviewed. Criteria adjustment deemed optimal for long-term Archive genetic heterogeneity. No action required. Recommend continued monitoring of demographic trends per standard protocol."

Kael read the response twice and deleted it.

The data chip from Maren was still on Kael's desk. Kael picked it up and loaded it onto the terminal. The declaration was simple:

*I, [NAME], hereby withdraw from translation queue consideration. This withdrawal is voluntary and irrevocable. I acknowledge that this decision means my consciousness will not be preserved in the Archive upon biological death. I make this choice freely as a matter of personal integrity.*

Below the text was a signature field and a timestamp field. That was all.

Kael stared at the declaration for a long time.

The system was extractive. The queue was a filter. The stratification was intentional. Kael had known this for years but knowing and seeing were different. The report had been the last test — the last check to see if reform was possible within the system — and Voss's response had been clear: the extraction was the point.

Which meant Kael had three options:

One: Keep writing reports. Keep sustaining the system. Translate automatically at end-of-service and spend eternity in the foam as a pattern that couldn't verify itself.

Two: Leave. Transfer to another station, retire to Earth, use the wealth accumulated over thirty years to exit the system entirely.

Three: Adopt the declaration. Stay in the job. Keep the Archive running. But refuse translation. Die untranslated as a matter of integrity.

Option two was the rational choice. Kael had the resources to leave. Had the connections to transfer. Could walk away from the system and let someone else write the reports.

But walking away meant leaving the extraction intact. Meant someone else would take the position and write the same reports and approve the same allocations and the Stacks would keep feeding the Archive forever.

Option three didn't stop the extraction either. But it was honest. It was refusing to benefit from a system built on other people's suffering. It was standing inside the mechanism and saying *I will do the work but I will not pretend the work is just.*

Kael pulled up the personnel files. Observer Ren, forty years old, eighteen years of service, score moderate, queue position 2937. Maintenance Tech Sova, thirty-eight years old, fifteen years of service, score 65.8 and falling, queue position 2953.

Both of them would die untranslated. Both of them knew it. Both of them came to work every day anyway.

Kael looked at the declaration again. Read it one more time.

The signature field was empty.

Kael's hand hovered over the keyboard.

## 12.

The hum is steady tonight. I lie on my bunk with my palm pressed to the bulkhead and feel the forty-hertz carrier and count.

Forty-seven states collapsed today. Forty-six yesterday. Forty-eight the day before. Eighteen years of states collapsed and filed and marked successful observation.

Chamber 19 is still dark. The investigation report came back today: catastrophic field failure, cause unknown, recommend enhanced monitoring protocols for adjacent chambers. No mention of my alert. No mention of the carrier instability I felt a week before the failure.

The instruments didn't see it coming because the instruments can't see fidelity. They can only see their own shadows.

I close my eyes and feel the hum and think about Mrs. Chen. Or what used to be Mrs. Chen. Or what we tell ourselves is still Mrs. Chen, held in superposition in some chamber I collapsed years ago, her mind replaced by a classical snapshot that the instruments call verified.

The foam holds eight million patterns like that. Eight million minds we can never verify. We just collapse them and file them and trust that the shadows are enough.

I don't trust the shadows anymore.

But I'll be back on the observation deck tomorrow. I'll collapse forty-seven states and feel the hum die forty-seven times and mark them all successful observation. Because that's the job. Because someone has to do it. Because the alternative is the airlock.

I press my palm harder to the bulkhead and feel the hum in my sternum, in my jaw, in the hollow of my chest where something used to be.

Fifty years from now I'll be dust and someone else will be lying here doing the math.

The hum is steady.

I close my eyes and let it carry me under.
