# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## REVISED NARRATIVE

# The Forty-Hertz Carrier

## PART ONE

### 1.

I press my palm to the stasis field and the hum dies under my hand. Mrs. Chen flickers in the foam—or what used to be Mrs. Chen, or what we tell ourselves is still Mrs. Chen. The instruments record a classical snapshot. Neural topology compressed into something they can file.

The screen says: *Successful observation.*

I mark it in the log and move to the next chamber.

Eighteen years I've been doing this. The queue is ninety years long. I am forty years old.

My shift ends at 2200 hours. I take the lift down to the Stacks, Section 7, Corridor M. Two-meter ceilings. Thirty people sharing a bathroom. The station hums at forty hertz and I feel it in my jaw, in my sternum, in the hollow behind my breastbone.

I lie on my bunk and close my eyes. The hum carries eight million minds held between states. Neither alive nor dead. Waiting for someone like me to collapse them into certainty.

Tomorrow I will do it again.

---

### 2.

Sova's hands read the field before her instruments caught up. Gamma-seven was stable—she could feel it through the bulkhead, steady thrum that meant the substrate held clean. She ran the diagnostics anyway because the logs had to match what the instruments said, not what her hands knew.

Green across the board. She marked it and moved on.

Forty fields checked and logged. Her score would tick up another point-oh-two percent this month. Steady. On track.

She ate dinner in the communal hall. Someone's cousin had made the queue, sixty-three years old, translated last week. They were talking about it like winning something.

Sova didn't join in. The queue wasn't luck. It was work. You contributed, you scored, you waited. Some people made it and some didn't, but the ones who made it had earned it.

She checked her contribution profile that night. Score: 73.6. Queue position: estimated year 2921. She was thirty-eight years old. If she lived to eighty-three and the criteria held steady, she'd make it through.

Eighty-three was old but people lived that long.

She lay on her bunk and felt the forty-hertz carrier through the walls. The Archive holding clean below them. Eight million minds preserved because people like her kept the fields running.

She closed her eyes and let the hum lull her toward sleep.

### 3.

Sova ran diagnostics on Section 4's primary fields, checked carrier stability, logged the readings. Everything nominal.

Hess met her at junction seven with coffee—real coffee, not the synthetic stuff from the commissary.

"Heard your score ticked up again," he said.

"Point-oh-two percent. Same as last month."

"That's good though. Consistent."

They split up. Sova moved through the corridors, palm to each bulkhead, feeling the carrier waves. Steady. Nominal.

At lunch she sat with Maren, one of the senior techs. Maren had been doing this for thirty years, had the highest contribution score in the Stacks.

"You ever think about what it's like?" Sova asked. "After translation?"

Maren stirred her protein paste. "The Archive says it's like dreaming. Consciousness preserved in superposition, experiencing time non-linearly."

"But how do they know? They can't measure it without collapsing the state."

"The instruments measure carrier stability. If those hold, the mind holds."

"But they don't measure whether you're still *you*."

Maren set down her spoon. "The instruments are calibrated daily. They're more sensitive than we are. If they say the mind is preserved, the mind is preserved."

Sova nodded. Maren was right.

She went back to work and didn't think about it again.

### 4.

I collapse forty-seven states on Tuesday. Forty-six on Wednesday. Forty-eight on Thursday.

The work is simple. Trigger the sequence. Feel the hum die. Mark it in the log.

The instruments say: *Successful observation.*

I've been doing this for eighteen years and I still don't know what successful means. The foam goes dark. The carrier wave flatlines. Whatever was in there becomes a classical snapshot that the Archive can file and store and call preserved.

On Friday I'm assigned to Chamber 19. Standard observation, nothing unusual. I trigger the collapse sequence and feel the hum drop—

And catch.

Not a failure. Just a flutter in the carrier wave. A hesitation.

I abort the sequence and check the diagnostics.

Carrier stability: nominal. Error rate: point-oh-three percent.

The instruments say everything's fine.

I trigger the sequence again. The hum drops clean this time. Successful observation.

I mark it in the log but my hands are shaking.

### 5.

Kael's quarterly report was due Friday. Substrate allocation efficiency, drift rates, projected capacity through 2895. The numbers were clean.

Kael typed: *All metrics nominal. Recommend continued monitoring per standard protocol.*

The cursor blinked.

Kael had been writing these reports for thirty years. The Gini coefficient for translation access was point-six-seven.

Kael had buried that number in Appendix E, page forty-seven, eight-point font. Nobody read Appendix E.

Point-six-seven meant the queue was stratified. Inner Ring residents translated at rates forty percent higher than Outer Ring residents. The criteria—social contribution value, genetic diversity markers, institutional continuity needs—were weighted in ways that correlated with birth location.

Everyone in Archive administration knew this.

Kael saved the report and queued it for review.

Kael's quarters were in the Inner Ring. Three rooms with four-meter ceilings. A hydroponics window where tomatoes grew under full-spectrum lights. The hum was quieter here.

Kael ate a tomato and reviewed the day's observation logs. Forty-seven states collapsed in Sector 3, all marked successful. Observer Ren had filed the reports with usual precision.

Ren had been an Observer for eighteen years. Score: moderate. Queue position: 2937.

Ren would die untranslated.

Kael closed the logs and stood at the hydroponics window, looking at nothing.

Kael had been pretending for thirty years.

### 6.

Gamma-seven's diagnostic panel turned red on Monday morning, exactly when Sova's hands had said it would. Carrier stability below threshold.

Sova logged the alert and called it in. Archive administration dispatched a senior tech and an Archivist to supervise.

The Archivist reviewed the logs. "Last routine maintenance was four days ago. Logged as green across all parameters."

"Yes sir," Sova said. "I noted it might need a secondary check, but diagnostics were nominal."

"And you didn't file a formal alert?"

"No sir. Diagnostics were nominal."

The Archivist made a note.

The field reset took forty minutes. The Archivist signed off and left without another word.

Sova sealed the access panel and sat on the floor with her back against the bulkhead.

Her score had dropped.

She pulled up her contribution profile that night. The score loaded: 73.4.

Down from 73.6 last month.

Sova stared at the number. For the first time in fifteen years, her score was going down instead of up.

She closed the terminal and lay on her bunk and tried to do the math. Seventy-three-point-four at age thirty-eight. Queue threshold was around eighty-five, maybe ninety. If her score climbed back to point-oh-two percent per month she'd hit eighty-five by age seventy-three.

If it stayed flat she'd never make it.

The score would recover. She'd file alerts earlier. Trust her hands over the instruments.

The score would recover.

### 7.

I'm collapsing state thirty-two when Chamber 19 stutters again.

Same flutter as last week. A catch in the carrier wave that makes my sternum ache. I abort the sequence and check diagnostics.

Carrier stability: nominal. Error rate: point-oh-three percent.

The instruments say everything's fine.

They said everything was fine last week too. Three days later Gamma-seven went red.

I file an alert: "Chamber 19 showing carrier instability, recommend secondary verification before proceeding with observation."

They didn't send anyone. They just looked at the same instruments I looked at and said resume protocol.

I close the terminal and press my palm to the bulkhead. Chamber 19 is holding. For now.

But the flutter was real.

What if the instruments can't register it? What if every state I've collapsed for eighteen years was failing and I killed it before anyone could tell?

I close my eyes. Try to sleep.

The flutter is in my chest and won't leave.

## PART TWO

### 8.

Sova filed alerts on every field that felt wrong. Didn't matter what the diagnostics said—if her hands told her something was failing, she logged it.

Her score dropped to 71.8 over the next month. Then 70.2. Then 68.9.

She got called into a supervisor meeting. They showed her the numbers: forty-seven alerts filed in thirty days, thirty-nine of which showed green on secondary diagnostics.

"Trust the instruments," the supervisor said.

Sova went back to work.

Her score kept dropping.

68.9. Then 67.3. Then 65.8.

At 65.8, the trend line crossed below adequate. The system recalculated her queue position: estimated translation year 2953.

She was thirty-eight years old.

She sat in her quarters and stared at the number. Fifteen years of sixty-hour weeks. Fifteen years of trusting that adequate meant adequate. That the queue was real.

She pulled up the criteria documentation. Found it buried in an old allocation report: *Genetic diversity markers increased from 15% to 35% of total social contribution value.*

Genetic diversity markers. The thing you got from modification clinics in the Inner Ring. The thing that cost more than she'd made in fifteen years.

The queue had never been real.

She'd been working toward a threshold that moved. Navigating a filter designed to select for wealth and birth location while pretending it measured contribution.

And she'd been proud of her score.

Heat in her face. Nausea in her gut. Fifteen years of believing she was earning something when she was just being sorted. Fifteen years of looking at people who didn't make the queue and thinking they hadn't worked hard enough.

She'd been a fool.

She closed the terminal. Lay on her bunk. Pressed both palms to the bulkhead and felt the hum.

The Archive was holding. Eight million minds preserved. The system worked.

The system worked by taking everything from people like her.

And she'd been proud of it.

All wrong. All green. All marked and logged and filed.

"The diagnostics. They measure carrier stability but they don't measure whether the mind is still preserved. They just measure their own shadows and call it verification."

"I've been filing alerts on fields that feel wrong and my score's been dropping because the diagnostics say green. But the diagnostics don't know. They can't know."

She stood and left.

Went back to her quarters.

Pulled up the contribution profile one more time.

Score: 65.8. Queue position: 2953.

She would never make the queue.

She closed the terminal and lay on her bunk and pressed both palms to the bulkhead.

The hum was steady. Forty hertz. Eight million minds held in superposition.

She felt them. All of them. Not metaphor—actual presence. Consciousness bleeding through the substrate. The Inner Ring had better insulation but down here in the Stacks you felt it. Every shift. Every night.

She closed her eyes and felt them and knew: the Archive wasn't preserving anyone. It was holding patterns that couldn't verify themselves.

And she'd been maintaining it for fifteen years.

She got up. Went to find Maren.
