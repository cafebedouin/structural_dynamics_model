## [CONSTRAINT-ANALYSIS #1]

**Recommendation:** "We should pursue an AI nonproliferation treaty as a strategic alternative to export controls" (Mallaby's core proposal, analyzed critically in document)

### CONSTRAINT-MAP

**Mountain (Natural Constraints):**
- Information physics: AI models are copyable information, not physical objects
- Verification impossibility: Cannot verify training/deployment without compromising competitive security
- Coordination problem: Adversary cooperation requires aligned incentives that don't exist

**Rope (Coordination Mechanisms):**
- Diplomatic frameworks (NPT precedent)
- Bilateral dialogue channels (existing US-China communication)
- International institutions (potential AI governance body)

**Noose (Extractive Constraints):**
- Treaty framework benefits diplomatic establishment regardless of enforceability
- Creates institutional roles/processes that serve professional class
- Shifts risk to populations without exit options while concentrating benefits among treaty architects

**Zombie Rope (Obsolete Coordination):**
- NPT verification mechanisms designed for physical weapons
- Cold War arms control assumptions about detectable violations
- Inspection regimes that assume observable infrastructure

### FEASIBILITY ASSESSMENT

**Classification:** FANTASY
**Confidence:** High
**Reasoning:** 

This recommendation violates Mountains in three ways:

1. **Information physics violation:** Treats copyable information (AI models) as if it has the verification properties of physical objects (nuclear weapons). The document correctly identifies this as a category error, not a technical gap.

2. **Coordination impossibility:** Requires adversary cooperation when structural incentives point toward defection. Chinese AI labs face the same competitive pressure as US labs—slowing down for safety while adversaries race ahead is unilateral disarmament. No mechanism exists to make mutual restraint credible.

3. **Verification impossibility:** Cannot verify compliance without either (a) perfect transparency that no state grants adversaries, or (b) perfect trust that doesn't exist between great powers. This isn't "difficult verification"—it's structurally impossible verification.

The document's analysis is correct: this is diplomatic theater masquerading as strategic alternative. The NPT precedent actually undermines the proposal—it worked *because* nuclear weapons are physical, detectable, and require rare materials. AI models are none of these.

**Critical signal:** When proponents say "we just need better verification technology," they're committing Type A reclassification (treating structural impossibility as technical challenge). The document correctly identifies this move and rejects it.

### IMPLEMENTATION MAP

**Required Preconditions:**
- Political: US-China consensus on existential AI risk (does not exist)
- Economic: Willingness to sacrifice competitive advantage for safety (no evidence)
- Technical: Verification mechanism that preserves competitive security (structurally impossible)
- Social: Public pressure after catastrophic AI incident (has not occurred)
- Temporal: Sustained commitment across presidential transitions (Trump reversal demonstrates impossibility)

**Energy Cost:**
- Person-hours: ~500,000 (treaty negotiation, verification protocol design, institutional setup—comparable to NPT)
- Political capital: Extreme (requires presidential-level commitment, Senate ratification, sustained diplomatic pressure)
- Opportunity cost: Sacrifices concrete chip controls (imperfect but measurable) for unenforceable promises

**Timeline:**
- Optimistic: 5-7 years (unprecedented for adversarial treaty)
- Realistic: Never (verification problem is structural, not technical)
- Catastrophe-contingent: 2-3 years IF major AI disaster creates political opening AND solves verification problem (disaster creates urgency but doesn't solve information physics)

**Veto Points:**
1. **Chinese government**
   - Status: Loses (constrains AI development during strategic competition)
   - Compensation: Not viable (sovereignty/competitive advantage non-tradeable)
   - Bypass: No (requires bilateral consent)

2. **US Senate**
   - Status: Mixed (some gain diplomatic framework, others lose competitive advantage)
   - Compensation: Possible (if verification credible—but it's not)
   - Bypass: No (treaty requires ratification)

3. **AI industry (US and China)**
   - Status: Loses (deployment constraints reduce competitive advantage)
   - Compensation: Unlikely (safety measures cost money/speed)
   - Bypass: Possible (regulatory arbitrage, offshore deployment)

**Assessment:** FANTASY—violates Mountains, creates new Noose (diplomatic establishment extraction), assumes verification mechanisms that cannot exist.

### SCAFFOLD REQUIREMENTS

**Load-bearing:** No—chip controls are imperfect but independent; removing them doesn't require scaffold because treaty doesn't replace their function (controls constrain hardware; treaty would constrain behavior—different mechanisms).

**However:** If treaty were viable (it's not), it would require:
- Type: Institutional bridge during transition
- Specification: Maintain chip controls while testing verification protocols
- Duration: 3-5 years (proof of concept)
- Sunset clause: If verification fails, controls remain; if verification works, controls phase out
- Anti-calcification: Independent technical review every 6 months

**Critical note:** Document correctly identifies that Mallaby proposes *replacing* controls with treaty, not complementing. This is the fatal flaw—trading concrete constraints for elegant impossibilities.

### CONSTRAINT-MAP

**Mountain:**

**Noose:**

- Allied coordination costs (diplomatic friction with partners)
- Potential for regulatory arbitrage (firms routing through third countries)

**Zombie Rope:**

### FEASIBILITY ASSESSMENT

**Classification:** VIABLE (with caveats)
**Confidence:** Medium-High
**Reasoning:**

1. **Navigates physics:** Chips are physical objects with detectable supply chains. Controls constrain hardware access, which is verifiable through customs, end-use checks, and supply chain monitoring.

2. **Addresses real coordination problem:** Allied semiconductor producers control chokepoints (ASML lithography, TSMC manufacturing). Coalition coordination is difficult but structurally possible.

3. **Produces measurable effects:** Document acknowledges 10x-30x compute gap exists, even if capability convergence is faster than hoped. This is evidence controls have teeth.

**Caveats:**
- Capability gaps smaller than compute gaps suggest (Chinese workarounds effective)
- Trump administration reversal demonstrates policy volatility risk
- Allied coordination fragile (economic incentives favor defection)
- Degradation over time as Chinese firms develop alternatives

**Critical insight:** Document correctly notes controls are *imperfect*, not *failing*. Imperfect constraints with measurable effects beat elegant frameworks with unverifiable commitments.

- Economic: Willingness to sacrifice semiconductor revenue (demonstrated by 2022 controls)
- Technical: Enforcement capacity (customs, end-use verification—exists)
- Social: Public support for China competition (exists in US, mixed in allies)
- Temporal: Sustained commitment across administrations (Trump reversal is warning sign)

**Energy Cost:**
- Person-hours: ~50,000 annually (enforcement, monitoring, allied coordination)
- Political capital: Medium (requires allied diplomacy, industry management)
- Opportunity cost: Semiconductor industry revenue (~$8B annually in lost China sales)

**Timeline:**
- Optimistic: Immediate (controls already exist, just strengthen enforcement)
- Realistic: 1-2 years (allied coordination, tighter enforcement mechanisms)
- Catastrophe-contingent: N/A (not dependent on crisis)

**Veto Points:**
1. **Allied governments (Netherlands, Japan, South Korea)**
   - Status: Mixed (security gain vs. economic loss)
   - Compensation: Possible (US market access, defense commitments)
   - Bypass: Difficult (require allied chokepoint cooperation)

2. **US semiconductor industry**
   - Status: Loses (China revenue)
   - Compensation: Partial (domestic subsidies, but doesn't offset China market)
   - Bypass: No (government has regulatory authority)

3. **Chinese government**
   - Status: Loses (constrained AI development)
   - Compensation: Not applicable (adversarial relationship)
   - Bypass: Possible (workarounds, indigenous development—but slow)

**Assessment:** VIABLE—works with Mountains, uses existing Ropes, accepts Noose (revenue sacrifice) as necessary cost. Imperfect but structurally sound.

### SCAFFOLD REQUIREMENTS

**Load-bearing:** Yes—current controls support US technological advantage and allied coordination.

**If strengthening:**

- Specification: Market access guarantees, defense commitments, R&D partnerships

- Sunset clause: N/A (controls are strategic, not transitional)

**Critical note:** Document's argument is that controls should be *strengthened*, not abandoned. This requires maintaining current scaffold (allied coordination) while adding enforcement mechanisms.
