## THE ONE-INCH FRAME

In November 2024, US and Chinese officials agreed that humans, not AI, should control nuclear weapons. The agreement required no verification, no enforcement, no sacrifice of competitive advantage. It was diplomatic theater — a statement of shared values that cost nothing to sign and nothing to break. Sebastian Mallaby wants to build a treaty on this foundation. The question is not whether the foundation is beautiful. The question is whether it can bear weight.

---

## SITUATION: THE CONTROL REGIME IS FAILING

The Biden administration's October 2022 chip export controls aimed to maintain US technological dominance by denying China access to advanced semiconductors. The theory was straightforward: AI capabilities scale with compute; compute requires cutting-edge chips; control the chips, control the capability gap. Two and a half years later, the evidence suggests the theory was wrong about what matters.

**The compute gap exists but the capability gap doesn't.** Critics of Mallaby's proposal cite a 10x-30x US advantage in available compute as evidence the controls are working. But Chinese AI models trail American ones by months, not years. DeepSeek, ByteDance, and Alibaba are approaching state-of-the-art performance using older chips, rented foreign data centers, and rapid copying of US advances. The structural question is which metric matters for strategic competition: the hardware advantage we can measure or the capability convergence we can observe.

This is not a measurement error. It is a category error. The controls constrain one thing (chip access) while competition operates on another (model capability). Chinese firms have found the gap between the constraint and the competition — they stack older chips, they rent compute abroad, they optimize algorithms to run on inferior hardware. The 10x compute advantage produces a sub-1-year capability lag because compute is a sufficient condition for AI progress, not a necessary one.

**The Trump administration's reversal compounds the failure.** In July 2025, the Trump administration reversed Biden's controls and approved H200 chip sales to China. This is not merely policy volatility. It is a signal about US commitment credibility in long-term agreements. If chip controls — a unilateral policy requiring no Chinese cooperation — cannot survive one presidential transition, what does that say about a bilateral treaty requiring sustained mutual restraint?

The controls were supposed to slow China's military AI progress, boost US economic security, and prevent unsafe AI development. They have produced measurable hardware gaps without corresponding capability gaps, sacrificed semiconductor industry revenue without corresponding security gains, and created diplomatic friction without corresponding leverage. The question Mallaby asks is: if the controls are failing anyway, why not trade them for something that might work?

## COMPLICATION: COOPERATION REQUIRES CREDIBLE MUTUAL INTEREST

Mallaby's proposal rests on a claim about Chinese incentives: that China shares US concerns about AI alignment risk and would accept meaningful restraints in exchange for lifting export controls. The evidence for this claim comes from "a dozen conversations with Chinese AI leaders" who expressed concern about AI systems acting against human interests.

**This is where elite rhetoric meets structural incentives, and the gap is wide.** Stated concern for AI safety is cheap. Every major AI lab claims to prioritize safety. The question is revealed preference: when safety measures conflict with competitive advantage, which gives way?

The structural incentives point one direction. China is in a race for AI dominance with the United States. That race has military, economic, and geopolitical dimensions. Slowing down to implement safety measures while your adversary races ahead is not caution — it is unilateral disarmament. The only scenario where mutual restraint makes sense is if both sides believe the risk of catastrophic failure outweighs the risk of falling behind. That belief requires either extraordinary foresight or a near-miss that makes the danger visceral.

**We have neither.** No AI system has yet produced catastrophic harm at scale. The risks Mallaby cites — wonder weapons, alignment failures, proliferation to rogue actors — are prospective, not demonstrated. Prospective risks produce statements of concern. Demonstrated risks produce behavior change. The gap between the two is the gap between what Chinese officials say in bilateral dialogues and what Chinese AI labs do when deployment decisions arrive.

The cooperation_credibility constraint analysis reveals this as a tangled_rope from moderate and analytical perspectives: it appears functional (a basis for negotiation) from positions with power to enforce agreements, but extractive (a trap) from positions without enforcement capacity. Mallaby's conversations with Chinese AI leaders occurred at the elite level where stated commitments are costless. The test is whether those commitments survive contact with competitive pressure, regulatory arbitrage, and the structural incentive to defect.

**The historical precedent Mallaby cites — the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty — actually undermines his case.** The NPT worked because nuclear weapons are physical objects requiring rare materials, specialized facilities, and detectable testing. Verification was possible because enrichment plants are large, uranium supply chains are traceable, and weapons tests produce seismic signatures. AI models are none of these things. They are copyable information that can be trained in secret, transferred instantly, and deployed without physical infrastructure. The verification asymmetry is not a detail to be solved later. It is the structural difference between a problem that admits cooperative solutions and one that may not.

## QUESTION: CAN VERIFICATION MECHANISMS TRANSFER FROM PHYSICAL TO INFORMATIONAL CONSTRAINTS?

The nonproliferation_feasibility analysis exposes the core structural problem. The constraint appears as a snare from moderate and analytical perspectives — an extractive trap that benefits the diplomatic establishment (Mallaby's institutional home at the Council on Foreign Relations) while sacrificing US technological advantage. From institutional perspectives with immediate time horizons, it appears as a rope (functional coordination). This is not disagreement about facts. It is disagreement about what kind of problem AI governance is.

**If AI models were like nuclear weapons, Mallaby's proposal would be coherent.** You could verify compliance through inspections, material accounting, and satellite surveillance. You could detect violations before they produced decisive advantage. You could enforce agreements through sanctions, isolation, and credible retaliation. The NPT worked because the physical constraints on nuclear weapons made verification and enforcement structurally possible, even between adversaries.

**AI models are not like nuclear weapons.** They are like software — infinitely copyable, instantly transferable, trainable in facilities indistinguishable from commercial data centers. The verification problem is not "difficult but solvable with sufficient technical sophistication." It is "structurally different from the problems verification mechanisms were designed to solve."

Consider the operational requirements:

- **Training verification:** How do you verify that a data center is not training a prohibited model without revealing your own intelligence capabilities? Inspections leak information about what you're looking for. Monitoring requires access to internal systems. Both create intelligence vulnerabilities that sophisticated actors will exploit.

- **Deployment verification:** How do you verify that a model is not deployed when deployment is software installation? Nuclear weapons require delivery systems, command structures, and observable positioning. AI models require servers and API keys. The deployment signature is invisible.

- **Capability verification:** How do you verify capability limits when capabilities emerge from training runs that can happen in secret? Nuclear yield is measurable. AI capability is a distribution over tasks that shifts continuously as models improve. The thing you're trying to verify is not a discrete state but a moving target.

The omega_verification_asymmetry question generated by the analysis asks directly: can AI model capabilities be verified like nuclear warheads? The answer appears to be no, not with existing mechanisms, and possibly not with any mechanism that preserves competitive security. If verification is structurally impossible, the nonproliferation proposal collapses from a strategic alternative to a diplomatic gesture — beautiful, unenforceable, and ultimately irrelevant.

## ANSWER (PART 1): THE TREATY SERVES THE DIPLOMATIC CLASS, NOT STRATEGIC STABILITY

The beneficiary analysis reveals who gains from Mallaby's proposal even if it fails to constrain AI development. The mallaby_cfr_diplomatic_establishment benefits from treaty frameworks regardless of enforceability because treaties create roles, processes, and institutional structures that diplomatic professionals inhabit. A nonproliferation regime generates conferences, working groups, verification protocols, and expert panels. It creates a domain where diplomatic skill matters and technical enforcement is someone else's problem.

**This is not cynicism. It is structural analysis.** Mallaby's proposal is coherent from the position of someone who believes negotiated frameworks are the appropriate response to great power competition on emerging technology. That belief is genuinely held and institutionally reinforced. The Council on Foreign Relations exists to promote diplomatic solutions to international problems. Mallaby is a two-time Pulitzer finalist, a serious thinker, and an honest advocate for a position his institutional context makes structurally legible.

The problem is that the position may be wrong. Not wrong in the sense of "motivated by bad faith," but wrong in the sense of "assumes verification mechanisms that cannot exist." The treaty framework is elegant. The verification problem is intractable. Elegance does not make intractability tractable.

**From powerless and moderate perspectives, the proposal looks different.** It looks like trading a concrete constraint (chip controls, however imperfect) for an unenforceable promise. It looks like sacrificing the one mechanism that produces measurable hardware gaps for a diplomatic framework that produces statements of concern. The extraction is not that Mallaby personally benefits — it is that the diplomatic class gets institutional validation while those without power to enforce treaties bear the risk of Chinese defection.

The constraint reports show this as extraction_accumulation drift: the gap between institutional and powerless perspectives is widening over time as the extractive nature of unenforceable agreements becomes more apparent. The purity score (0.271, degraded) indicates this is not a clean coordination problem. It is coordination-washing — hiding extraction behind distributed enforcement and behavioral defaults that favor the powerful.

## ANSWER (PART 2): THE CONTROLS ARE FAILING, BUT THE ALTERNATIVE IS WORSE

Mallaby is right that chip export controls are not producing the capability gaps their architects hoped for. The 10x-30x compute advantage exists on paper but translates to months, not years, of capability lag. Chinese firms are finding workarounds. The Trump administration's reversal demonstrates policy volatility that undermines long-term strategic coherence.

**But "the current approach is failing" does not imply "the proposed alternative would succeed."** The choice is not between effective controls and effective treaties. The choice is between imperfect constraints with measurable effects and elegant frameworks with unverifiable commitments.

The chip controls produce:
- A measurable hardware gap (10x-30x compute advantage, even if capability convergence is faster than expected)
- Revenue sacrifice for US semiconductor firms (a real cost that indicates the policy has teeth)
- Diplomatic friction (a signal of commitment, not just rhetoric)
- Degradation over time through workarounds (a genuine limitation)

The proposed treaty would produce:
- Statements of mutual concern (costless to sign, costless to violate)
- Institutional frameworks for dialogue (valuable for relationship management, irrelevant for capability constraint)
- Verification protocols that cannot verify (because the thing being verified is copyable information, not physical infrastructure)
- Enforcement mechanisms that cannot enforce (because defection is invisible until it produces decisive advantage)

**The asymmetry is structural.** Controls constrain hardware, which is observable and slow-moving. Treaties constrain behavior, which is unobservable and fast-moving. When the constrained thing is information, verification requires either perfect transparency (which no state will grant an adversary) or perfect trust (which does not exist between great power competitors).

Mallaby's proposal asks us to trade imperfect hardware constraints for unenforceable behavioral commitments. The trade might make sense if Chinese cooperation were credible, if verification were possible, or if the risks of unilateral AI development were so catastrophic that any attempt at coordination is worth the sacrifice. We have evidence for none of these conditions.

## IMPLICATIONS: WHAT FOLLOWS IF THE VERIFICATION PROBLEM IS STRUCTURAL

If AI model capabilities cannot be verified like nuclear warheads — not because we lack technical sophistication but because information is categorically different from physical objects — then several positions become indefensible:

**Indefensible Position 1:** "We should pursue an AI nonproliferation treaty as a strategic alternative to export controls."

If verification is structurally impossible, a treaty is not an alternative strategy. It is diplomatic theater that sacrifices concrete constraints for unenforceable promises. The only coherent version of this position is: "We should pursue a treaty as a confidence-building measure that complements, rather than replaces, export controls." Mallaby does not argue this. He argues for scrapping controls in exchange for a treaty. That trade is indefensible if verification cannot work.

**Indefensible Position 2:** "Chinese statements of AI safety concern are sufficient evidence of willingness to accept meaningful restraints."

Stated concern is not revealed preference. Elite rhetoric is cheap. The test is whether safety commitments survive competitive pressure. We have no evidence they would. The cooperation_credibility analysis shows this as a tangled_rope: functional from institutional perspectives with enforcement power, extractive from perspectives without it. Until Chinese AI labs demonstrate willingness to sacrifice competitive advantage for safety measures — not in conversations with visiting scholars but in deployment decisions under pressure — the stated concern is hypothesis, not evidence.

**Indefensible Position 3:** "The current export control regime is failing, therefore we should abandon it."

The controls are producing smaller capability gaps than hoped, but they are producing measurable hardware gaps. Abandoning them does not unlock cooperation — it removes the one constraint that has observable effects. The coherent position is: "The controls are imperfect and should be strengthened, not scrapped." Strengthening might mean tighter enforcement, broader coalitions, or acceptance that hardware gaps are the best available constraint even if they don't prevent capability convergence.

## THE POSITIONAL ANALYSIS: WHO SHOULD ACCEPT MALLABY'S ARGUMENT?

**This proposal serves agents with:**
- Institutional power to shape diplomatic frameworks (State Department officials, CFR fellows, treaty negotiators)
- Long time horizons that discount near-term competitive disadvantage (academic strategists, think tank researchers)
- Analytical sophistication to engage verification debates (AI safety researchers, arms control experts)
- Faith that great power competition can be managed through negotiated restraint (the diplomatic establishment)

**This proposal fails agents with:**
- Moderate power facing direct consequences of Chinese AI advancement (DoD planners, semiconductor firms, allied governments)
- Immediate time horizons where capability gaps matter (military commanders, intelligence analysts)
- Constrained exit options if the treaty fails (populations subject to AI-enabled surveillance or autonomous weapons)
- Structural skepticism about adversary compliance absent verification (national security professionals, competitive strategists)

The gauge-variance is not a bug. It is the structure of the problem. From positions where treaties create institutional roles and verification problems are technical challenges to be solved, Mallaby's proposal is coherent strategic reorientation. From positions where capability gaps are existential and verification is structurally impossible, the proposal is sacrificing concrete constraints for diplomatic elegance.

**I write from the analytical position with civilizational time horizons.** From here, the verification problem is not a technical gap to be closed but a category difference between physical and informational constraints. The proposal asks us to trade hardware gaps (measurable, slow-moving, imperfect) for behavioral commitments (unverifiable, fast-moving, unenforceable). That trade might be worth making if the catastrophic risks of unilateral AI development were demonstrated rather than prospective. They are not demonstrated. The treaty is a bet that Chinese cooperation is credible, that verification can work, and that diplomatic frameworks can constrain information flows. All three premises are unproven. The bet risks US technological advantage on unverified assumptions.

From moderate perspectives with national scope and biographical time horizons, the analysis is simpler: the controls have measurable effects; the treaty has unverifiable promises; trading the former for the latter is strategic malpractice. From institutional perspectives with immediate time horizons and arbitrage exit options, the treaty looks like functional coordination — a framework for managing competition through dialogue rather than denial. The perspectival fracture is genuine. This is not disagreement about facts. It is disagreement about what kind of problem AI governance is and whether verification mechanisms can bridge the gap between physical and informational constraints.

## THE UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS (Ω)

**Ω_E (Empirically resolvable):** Can AI model capabilities be verified without intelligence leakage? This is not currently resolved but could be tested through pilot verification protocols with allied nations. If verification requires access to internal systems that compromise competitive security, the structural impossibility claim is confirmed. If cryptographic or statistical methods can verify capability limits without system access, the treaty becomes more feasible. The measurement would resolve the debate.

**Ω_C (Conceptually underspecified):** Is the US-China capability gap best measured by compute advantage or model performance? The chip control critics cite 10x-30x compute gaps; Mallaby observes sub-1-year capability gaps. Both are true. The question is which metric matters for strategic competition. Specify the index: if the goal is military advantage, capability gaps matter more than compute gaps. If the goal is economic security, compute gaps may matter because they affect deployment scale. The apparent tension dissolves when you specify what "working" means.

**Ω_P (Structurally irresolvable):** Should the US prioritize maintaining competitive advantage or preventing catastrophic AI risk through cooperation? This is a values question, not an analytical one. Different stakeholders will weight these risks differently based on their threat models, time horizons, and institutional positions. The diplomatic establishment weights cooperation higher; the national security establishment weights advantage higher. No amount of analysis resolves this — it requires stakeholder decision about acceptable risk trade-offs.

**Ω_E (Empirically resolvable):** What is the durability of chip export controls as Chinese firms develop workarounds? This can be measured through tracking Chinese model performance over time, monitoring hardware acquisition pathways, and assessing the effectiveness of enforcement mechanisms. If capability convergence continues despite tightened controls, the controls are degrading faster than enforcement can adapt. If capability gaps widen with stronger enforcement, the controls remain viable constraints.

**Ω_C (Conceptually underspecified):** Does Chinese AI safety concern reflect elite rhetoric or institutional commitment? This dissolves when you specify the observer position. From the position of Chinese AI lab leadership in conversation with foreign scholars, stated concern is genuine — they face the same alignment problems as Western labs. From the position of Chinese government officials balancing safety against competitive advantage, revealed preference matters more than stated concern. Both can be true simultaneously. The question is which position has decision authority when safety and speed conflict.

## CLOSING

The nonproliferation gambit is elegant, coherent from diplomatic perspectives, and probably wrong. It assumes verification mechanisms that worked for physical weapons can transfer to copyable information. It treats stated concern as evidence of willingness to sacrifice competitive advantage. It proposes trading concrete constraints for unenforceable promises. These are not minor technical details to be resolved through negotiation. They are structural differences between problems that admit cooperative solutions and problems that may not.

Mallaby's diagnosis is partially correct: the chip export controls are not producing the capability gaps their architects hoped for. But the prescription does not follow from the diagnosis. "The current approach is imperfect" does not imply "an unverifiable treaty is the alternative." It implies "strengthen enforcement, broaden coalitions, or accept that hardware constraints are the best available option even if they don't prevent capability convergence."

The treaty serves the diplomatic class whether or not it constrains AI development. It creates frameworks, processes, and institutional roles. It produces statements of mutual concern and venues for dialogue. These are valuable for relationship management. They are not substitutes for verification and enforcement. When the thing being governed is information rather than physical infrastructure, verification requires either perfect transparency or perfect trust. Great power competitors grant neither. The verification asymmetry is not a technical gap. It is a structural limit. Elegance does not make the limit disappear.

From positions without power to enforce treaties, the proposal looks like extraction: trading measurable constraints for diplomatic gestures that benefit the establishment while shifting risk to those who cannot exit if cooperation fails. From positions with institutional power and long time horizons, the proposal looks like strategic reorientation toward managing competition through frameworks rather than denial. Both perspectives are structurally coherent. The gauge-variance is the problem, not a measurement error.

The question is not whether we should cooperate with China on AI safety. The question is whether cooperation is structurally possible when verification cannot work and competitive incentives point toward defection. Until someone solves the verification problem — not by asserting it is solvable but by demonstrating a mechanism that preserves competitive security while detecting violations — the nonproliferation treaty is a beautiful framework built on an impossible foundation. We should not trade imperfect constraints for elegant impossibilities. The risks are too high and the evidence for cooperation too thin.
