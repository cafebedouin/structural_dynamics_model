# The Glass Between Us

I write the petition in Persian because that's the only language that reaches him. My Arabic sits in my throat—the language my grandfather used to describe the *saql*, the polishing motion that brings light through glass—but al-Shirazi needs formal words, administrative words, words that acknowledge he controls what I need.

*'Arz-e man barā-ye hamkārī bā Maryam al-Raqqi dar kār-e 'adasa-ye kabūd.*

My petition for collaboration with Maryam al-Raqqi on blue lens work.

The ink dries on my fingers while I wait. Iron gall, black as the Euphrates mud. It'll stain for hours. I used to wash it off immediately, back when I still thought the stains were temporary.

Outside my window, the quarter is waking. Layla will be finishing her shift soon—twelve hours at the furnace, night work because that's when fuel allocation is lowest. I can see the glow from here, orange against the pre-dawn sky. The Friday Mosque is still rubble. They haven't bothered rebuilding it. Why would they? The call to prayer still sounds, echoing off broken walls, and that's enough to keep us marking the hours.

My grandfather knew the whole process. Sand to glass to lens. He could take a handful of Euphrates sand and show you finished work that would let you see the moons of Jupiter. That knowledge died with him in the siege. Or rather, it shattered. I have the grinding, the polishing. Layla has the fire. Maryam has the cobalt formulas. But we can't share without permission, and permission costs time and petition ink and the small death of writing in Persian.

The petition sits on my workbench, waiting for me to walk it to al-Shirazi's house. Three stories, private courtyard, servants who speak Persian better than I do. He'll read it, maybe today, maybe next week. He'll grant it or deny it based on calculations I can't see. And I'll wait, because waiting is what we do here.

I pick up yesterday's lens blank instead. Unfinished. The glass came from Layla's furnace three days ago—I can tell by the color, that particular shade of clarity she achieves when the fuel allocation is good. But I can't grind it until I have Maryam's cobalt, and I can't get the cobalt until al-Shirazi approves the petition, and the petition is still drying on my fingers.

The lens is for an astrolabe. Some merchant in Samarkand needs it to calculate prayer times, or that's what the order says. Maybe he does. Maybe he just wants something beautiful. Either way, I'll be paid only if the finished piece passes al-Shirazi's inspection, and finished means cobalt, and cobalt means waiting.

I should be used to it by now. Two years in this room, two years of petitions. But my hands remember my grandfather's hands, remember when the knowledge was whole, when we didn't have to ask permission to do what we knew how to do.

The sun rises. The call to prayer sounds. I wash the ink off my fingers, but it doesn't come clean.

---

Layla bint Khalil leaves the furnace at dawn and the heat follows her home. It sits in her chest, in her throat, in the cough she's been hiding for three weeks now. The night shift is twelve hours of fire that never dies because letting it die means losing her fuel allocation, and fuel comes from al-Shirazi's office, rationed by the week, never enough to waste.

She walks past Yusuf's workshop. His lamp is lit. He's always awake at this hour, always working or waiting to work. She knows what he's waiting for—the same thing everyone waits for. Permission. Access. The *muqaddam*'s approval to do what you already know how to do.

Her room is two doors down from the furnace. Close enough that she never really leaves the heat. Her mother is awake, mixing bread dough. Her aunt is still sleeping. They don't ask about the cough anymore. What would they ask? Whether she's dying? They know she's dying. The furnace takes everyone eventually. Her father lasted twenty years. She's on year six.

"Did the glass come out well?" her mother asks.

Layla nods. She doesn't have the breath for more. The glass came out perfect—she knows fire the way her father knew it, knows exactly when to add fuel, when to let it burn hot, when to bank it. But knowing doesn't matter if no one uses the glass. Yusuf needs it for his lenses, but he can't grind without Maryam's cobalt, and Maryam can't provide without al-Shirazi's permission, and the glass sits in the workshop, waiting.

Everything waits.

She lies down without washing. The heat is still in her skin. She dreams of cool water, of the Euphrates before the siege, when her brothers were alive and her father could still breathe and the glass quarter made things that went everywhere, to Baghdad and Damascus and Cairo. She dreams of making glass that someone actually uses.

She wakes four hours later, coughing. Blood on her hand, small spots, easy to hide. She washes it away before her mother sees.

---

Maryam al-Raqqi doesn't need to petition. Al-Shirazi grants her requests automatically because she's the last person alive who knows the complete cobalt process—the blue that makes astronomical instruments valuable, the blue that keeps the glass quarter producing. She has leverage. She has savings. She could leave.

She stays because she's a colorant specialist from a family of colorant specialists, and if she leaves, the knowledge leaves with her. That's what she tells herself. But this morning, watching Layla walk home from the night shift, watching the way the young woman moves like every step costs something, Maryam knows there's more to it.

She stays because leaving would mean admitting what this place has become.

Her workshop is in her family's house, three rooms on the second floor, windows that catch the morning light. She's working on a cobalt batch when Yusuf's petition arrives—not to her, to al-Shirazi, but she knows about it anyway. Everyone knows about everyone's petitions. The quarter is small and the walls are thin and gossip moves faster than permissions.

Yusuf wants to collaborate. He wants her cobalt for his lens. It's a reasonable request. She has the cobalt. He has the grinding skill. Together they could produce a finished piece in three days. But together requires al-Shirazi's approval, and al-Shirazi will take a week to grant it, maybe two, because delay is how he maintains control.

She could just give Yusuf the cobalt. Walk it down to his workshop, hand it over, let him work. But working without permission means losing her privileged access to the furnaces, means losing the fuel allocation that keeps her comfortable, means becoming like Layla—trapped in night shifts and rationed resources and the slow consumption of the work itself.

So she waits for al-Shirazi's approval, and Yusuf waits, and Layla makes glass no one can use, and the system continues because no one can afford to break it.

Except Maryam can afford to break it. That's the problem. She has savings. She has a house. She has options. And she's choosing not to use them.

The cobalt sits in its jar, perfect blue, waiting for permission to be useful.

---

Hasan al-Shirazi reads Yusuf's petition over breakfast. The Arabic is competent but stiff—the grammar of someone who learned Persian as an adult, who has to think about every formal phrase. The request itself is straightforward. Yusuf wants to work with Maryam. He needs cobalt. The lens is for a Samarkand merchant. The work is already commissioned.

Al-Shirazi sets the petition aside. He'll approve it, eventually. The work needs to be done, and Yusuf is a skilled grinder. But approval doesn't need to happen today. A week's delay costs al-Shirazi nothing and reminds Yusuf who controls access.

That's the arrangement: the artisans have their craft, the administration has resources and permissions, and al-Shirazi mediates between them. It's efficient. It's fair. Everyone gets what they've earned.

He moves to the next petition. This one is from a furnace operator requesting daytime shifts. He denies it immediately. Night shifts exist because daytime fuel allocation goes to priority work, and priority is determined by output, and output is determined by completed pieces, and this operator—he checks the name, Layla bint Khalil—hasn't completed a piece in three months because the grinders can't get colorant approvals fast enough.

The system works. If it didn't work, Timur's court wouldn't keep commissioning instruments. If it didn't work, the quarter would have been razed like the rest of the city.

Al-Shirazi speaks Arabic with the artisans. It's a courtesy, a way of showing accessibility. But all binding decisions are in Persian, all official records are in Persian, and when he writes his reports to Timur's court, he writes in the language of power. That's not cruelty. That's administration.

He finishes breakfast and moves to the day's inspections. Three pieces waiting for approval. He'll inspect them this afternoon, maybe approve two, maybe none. Quality must be maintained. Standards exist for a reason.

Outside his window, the quarter is working. Furnaces glow. Grinding wheels turn. Petitions pile up in his office. The system continues.

---

The secret meetings start three weeks after I submit the petition. I still haven't received approval. The lens blank sits on my bench, perfect glass from Layla's furnace, waiting for cobalt I can't access. I've written two follow-up petitions. Al-Shirazi hasn't responded to either.

Somewhere between the second petition and the third, I stop believing in the process.

The first meeting is after night prayers, in the empty workshop two doors down from mine. The building survived the siege but the artisan didn't, and no one has claimed the space. Five of us gather: me, Layla, two other grinders, a polisher. We speak only in Arabic. It's the first time in two years I've talked about my craft without switching to Persian mid-sentence.

"I know the grinding," I say. "Layla knows the fire. If we pool what we know—"

"We'll still need cobalt," one of the other grinders interrupts. "And cobalt is Maryam."

"Then we ask Maryam."

"She won't risk it."

"We don't know that."

But we do know that. Maryam has too much to lose. She has her house, her savings, her privileged position. Why would she risk that for us?

Layla speaks for the first time. Her voice is rough, furnace-scarred. "I can get you fire. I can't get you cobalt. But I can get you fire that doesn't go through al-Shirazi's allocation."

We all look at her.

"How?" I ask.

"There's fuel in the market. Black market. Expensive, but it exists. If someone paid for it—"

"Who's going to pay for it?"

Silence. None of us have money. We're paid by the piece, and pieces require al-Shirazi's approval, and approval is slow enough that we're always behind on rent, on food, on everything.

"I could pay," someone says.

We turn. Maryam is standing in the doorway. I don't know how long she's been there.

"I could pay for fuel," she says again. "I could set up a furnace in my courtyard. Private. Outside al-Shirazi's allocation."

"Why would you do that?" Layla asks.

Maryam looks at her for a long moment. "Because you're dying," she says finally. "And I'm the reason you're dying. Not the only reason. But part of it."

---

Maryam al-Raqqi buys fuel on the black market. It costs three times what al-Shirazi pays, but she has the savings, and the fuel dealer doesn't ask questions. She has the furnace built in her courtyard—small, private, enough for one person to work. She tells her mother it's for experimental batches. Her mother doesn't believe her, but her mother also doesn't object.

Layla comes at night. Not during her shift—she still works al-Shirazi's furnace because quitting would draw attention—but after, when she should be sleeping. Maryam shows her the fuel, the setup, the schedule.

"You don't have to do twelve-hour shifts here," Maryam says. "Make what you need. Make what Yusuf needs. Rest."

"Why are you doing this?"

Maryam doesn't have an answer that doesn't sound like guilt. "Because I can," she says finally.

Layla works the private furnace three nights a week. The glass she produces doesn't go through al-Shirazi's inspection. It goes straight to Yusuf, who grinds it using techniques he's been teaching himself from memory and experimentation. Maryam provides cobalt without petitioning. They work in Arabic, speak in Arabic, share knowledge in Arabic.

They produce their first lens in five days. It's not perfect—Yusuf's grinding is slightly off, Layla's annealing schedule needs adjustment—but it's complete. Start to finish. No permissions. No petitions. No Persian.

They produce the second lens in three days. The third in two.

Al-Shirazi notices when Yusuf stops submitting petitions.

---

I don't submit the third petition. I stop writing them entirely. My fingers are clean of ink for the first time in two years, and the absence feels like freedom and terror simultaneously.

Al-Shirazi summons me to his office. I go because refusing a summons would mean arrest, and arrest would mean the end of everything we're building. But I go slowly, and I don't bring a petition.

His office is on the second floor of his house, overlooking the courtyard. The room is cool—thick walls, high ceilings, a servant who brings tea without being asked. He speaks to me in Arabic. He always starts in Arabic.

"You haven't submitted a petition in three weeks," he says.

"I haven't needed to."

"You have commissions. The Samarkand merchant. The Damascus observatory. How are you completing work without collaboration approvals?"

I don't answer. He knows how. The quarter is small. He knows about the private furnace, about Layla's night work, about the lenses we're producing outside his inspection system. The question isn't about information. It's about whether I'll acknowledge what I'm doing, whether I'll submit to the fiction that his permission is necessary.

"The arrangement exists for quality control," he says. "For coordination. Without oversight, standards slip. Work becomes inconsistent."

"The work is consistent."

"By whose measurement?"

"By the measurement of the people who commissioned it."

He switches to Persian. It's a subtle shift, but I feel it—the language of power, of binding decisions. "You are violating the administrative structure of this quarter. That structure exists to serve everyone. Your actions undermine collective benefit."

I stay in Arabic. "The structure serves you. It extracts from us. We're finding another way."

"There is no other way. The fragmentation of knowledge requires mediation. Without coordination, you cannot produce complete work."

"We're producing complete work right now."

"Without inspection. Without quality assurance. Without—"

"Without your permission," I interrupt. "That's what you mean. Without your control."

The silence that follows is long enough that I hear the fountain in his courtyard, the call to prayer from the ruined mosque, the distant sound of Layla's official furnace still burning because she can't quit that work entirely, not yet.

"You will submit your work for inspection," al-Shirazi says finally. "You will resume the petition process. You will comply with the administrative structure. Or you will lose your workshop, your tools, and your authorization to work in this quarter."

"Then I lose them."

I leave before he can respond. My hands are shaking. I've just refused the system that's kept me alive for two years. I've just chosen transformation over survival.

I don't know if it was the right choice. But I know my fingers are clean.

---

Layla bint Khalil collapses during her official shift two weeks later. The heat, the smoke, the exhaustion—they take her the way they took her father, the way they take everyone eventually. She wakes in Maryam's house, in a real bed, with clean water and bread that isn't rationed.

"You can't go back," Maryam says. "Your body can't take it."

"I don't have a choice."

"You do. You work my furnace. Only my furnace. I'll pay you what al-Shirazi was paying. More, if I can."

"That's not sustainable. You're buying black market fuel. You're supporting me and Yusuf and the others. You'll run out of money."

"Then we'll find another way."

Layla looks at her. "Why are you doing this?"

Maryam sits down on the edge of the bed. "Because I've been complicit in your extraction for six years. Because I had choices and I chose comfort. Because I can't anymore."

"Can't what?"

"Can't pretend the system is fair. Can't pretend my privilege isn't built on your pain. Can't keep participating in something that's killing you."

Layla coughs. Blood on her hand, more than before. "I'm already dying."

"Then die in a bed. Die rested. Die knowing you helped build something different."

Layla closes her eyes. The room is cool. The bed is soft. She can hear the fountain in Maryam's courtyard, the call to prayer, the absence of furnace roar.

"Okay," she says.

---

Hasan al-Shirazi revokes Yusuf's workshop three weeks after the confrontation. The order is in Persian, formal, binding. Yusuf has violated administrative protocol. His authorization to work in the quarter is terminated. His tools are to be surrendered to the administration for redistribution.

Yusuf doesn't surrender them. He moves his tools to Maryam's house, sets up in a ground-floor room, continues working. Two other grinders join him. Then a polisher. Then a colorant specialist who's been trying to learn Maryam's formulas.

They work in Arabic. They share knowledge without petitioning. They produce lenses and mirrors and prisms that go straight to customers, bypassing al-Shirazi's inspection entirely.

Al-Shirazi reports the situation to Timur's regional administrator. The report is in Persian, detailed, emphasizing the breakdown of quality control, the undermining of administrative structure, the need for enforcement.

The response takes four weeks. When it comes, it's brief: *Maintain production. Method is secondary to output.*

Al-Shirazi reads it three times. The administration doesn't care about his mediation. They care about finished instruments. If the artisans can produce without him, his position becomes unnecessary.

He approves thirty petitions the next day. He reduces furnace allocation fees. He offers collaboration permissions without delay. But the artisans who've already left don't come back. They're working in Maryam's house, working in Arabic, working without him.

The quarter splits. Half the artisans still petition, still work through al-Shirazi's system, still write in Persian and wait for permissions. Half work collectively, pool knowledge, produce outside the official structure.

Both halves produce. Both halves survive.

The system continues, but it's no longer the only system.

---

Six months after the first secret meeting, I'm grinding a lens in Maryam's workshop when a young artisan knocks on the door. He's maybe twenty, still has the ink stains on his fingers from this morning's petition.

"I heard you're working here," he says in Arabic. "I heard you don't need permissions."

"We don't use al-Shirazi's permissions," I correct. "We still need each other's permission. We still coordinate. We just do it directly."

"Can I learn?"

I look at Maryam. She nods.

"Come in," I tell him. "What do you know?"

"Polishing. My father taught me before the siege."

"Then teach us. We'll teach you the rest."

He comes in. His fingers are still stained with petition ink, but by evening, he's working on a grinding wheel, learning the motion my grandfather taught me, speaking in Arabic about the craft his father knew.

The knowledge is still fragmented. But we're putting it back together, piece by piece, without asking permission to do what we already know how to do.

---

Layla bint Khalil will probably die young. The furnace took too much. But she dies working four-hour shifts instead of twelve. She dies rested. She dies teaching three other people how to read fire, how to know when the glass is ready, how to keep the knowledge alive.

Maryam al-Raqqi will run out of money eventually. Black market fuel is expensive. Supporting six artisans is expensive. But she's using her privilege to create exits for people who had none, and when the money runs out, maybe they'll have built something sustainable. Maybe they won't. Either way, she's no longer complicit.

Hasan al-Shirazi still runs the official system. Half the quarter still petitions. He still approves and denies, still mediates, still writes reports in Persian. But his position is precarious now. The administration only cares about output, and output is happening with or without him.

I still live in one room above a workshop I no longer own. I still have no savings. I still can't leave the quarter. But I can make a complete lens without writing a petition, and that's something. That's a crack in the wall.

The call to prayer sounds from the ruined mosque. I wash my hands. The water runs clear.

My fingers are clean.
