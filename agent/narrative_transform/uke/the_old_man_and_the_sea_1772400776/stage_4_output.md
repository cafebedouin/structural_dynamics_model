# The Number

The conveyor jams at 14:23.

I count because counting helps. Thirty-seven seconds to clear the blockage. Seventy-two pods lost to the timer. My wrist terminal flickers: 30.

I don't look at it anymore. The number falls whether I look or not.

My hands cramp. *Krampen*, I think. My grandmother's word for the ache in her knees. The Indonesian sits better in my mouth than the English. The conveyor starts again—metal grinding, hydraulics hissing. I reach for the next pod. My fingers barely close around it.

The cultivation chamber smells like mineral solution and rot. Tower 89-C, floor 89, the lowest level of Vertical Farm 47. The LED arrays flicker. Half of them are dead. The algae grows pale and thin in the bad light. High contamination risk. Not my fault. Just the equipment.

But the terminal doesn't measure fault. It measures outcomes.

I detach the pod—twist, pull, load onto the conveyor. My back screams. Twist, pull, load. The rhythm is everything. If I think about the pain, I slow down. If I slow down, the quota slips further. If the quota slips, the number falls.

It's already falling.

900 pods per shift. I'm at 380 and it's hour nine. The math is simple. I won't make it. I never make it anymore.

Across the tower, I hear Wei working. Fast, smooth movements. No hesitation. His hands don't cramp yet. He's nineteen. I watch him sometimes—the way his fingers close around the pods, quick and sure. The way he doesn't have to stop and shake out the pain.

That was me, once. Score 78. Tower 92-A. Good equipment, reasonable quotas. I had an apprentice then too. A girl, sharp and quick. She learned fast.

Then the contamination event in '85. Fungal outbreak. Crashed three towers. Not my fault—biological lottery, random spore drift. But my score dropped to 52. They moved me to tower 90-B. Older equipment. My outcomes got worse. Score dropped to 44. They moved me to 89-C.

Four years later: 30.

The Allocator calls this productivity measurement. Optimal resource allocation. High performers get good equipment. Low performers get filtered out.

I call it something else. But I don't have a word for it.

---

Wei finishes his section first. He always does. I hear him check the flow rates, verify the nutrient levels. Thorough. Careful. The way I taught him.

Three months ago, the Allocator assigned him to my tower. New apprentice, they said. Train him. I was surprised—harvesters with scores below 35 don't usually get apprentices. But they needed someone to work 89-C, and Wei was available.

I liked him immediately. Quiet, observant. Good hands. He listened when I showed him how to spot contamination early, how to coax more speed from the failing equipment, how to read the algae's color for nutrient deficiency.

His score was 65 when he started. Good, solid middle.

Now it's 58.

I don't need to look at his terminal to know. I can see it in his parents' faces when they come to the farm. The way his mother's mouth goes tight when she sees him working next to me. The way his father pulls him aside during breaks, speaking low and urgent in Mandarin.

*Guanxi.* I know that word. Network, favor-debt, social capital. They're calling in every connection they have.

To get him away from me.

---

The shift ends at 18:00. My terminal displays the damage: 412 pods harvested. 7% contamination rate. Conveyor jam logged as equipment failure, but it still counts against me. Deductions: 18 credits.

I do the math automatically now. 18 credits × 25 shifts = 450 credits this month. Rent: 280. Food: 120 minimum. Water: 40 minimum.

Total: 440. Shortfall: negative 10 if I'm lucky.

I'll skip water rations again. The smell doesn't bother me anymore. But it bothers everyone else. They avoid me in the corridors. Not just the score—the smell. Poverty is visible. Poverty is contagious.

Wei is waiting by the exit. He shouldn't be. His shift ended fifteen minutes ago. His parents will be angry.

"Budi," he says. Just my name. He doesn't say anything else.

I nod. We walk to the lift together. The silence is comfortable. We've been working side by side for three months. Sometimes silence says more than words.

The lift is empty. Good. People don't like to ride with me. *Menular*, they whisper. Contagious. As if my score will infect them through proximity.

Maybe it will. Wei's dropped seven points.

"My parents found a placement," Wei says. The words come out flat. Practiced. "Tower 95-B. Premium assignment. I start next week."

I nod again. Of course they did. I knew they would. I've been watching it happen—the hushed conversations, the careful negotiations, the way they stopped inviting me to family meals after the first month.

"The harvester there is good," Wei continues. "Score 82. He needs an apprentice. His current one is moving to Maintenance."

"That's good," I say. My voice sounds normal. I'm surprised by that. "You'll learn a lot."

"I learned a lot here."

"You'll learn more there. Better equipment. Higher quotas. Your score will stabilize."

Wei looks at his terminal. 58, glowing amber. Warning threshold. Below 50, recovery is nearly impossible. He knows this. His parents know this. That's why they're moving him.

"I don't want to leave," he says quietly.

"Yes, you do." I'm not angry. I'm not even sad. I'm just stating facts. "You want to leave, and you should leave. Your parents are right. Working with me is killing your score."

"It's not your fault—"

"It doesn't matter whose fault it is. The Allocator measures outcomes. Outcomes depend on equipment. Equipment depends on score. Score depends on outcomes." I've said this before. To myself, mostly. It doesn't change anything. "The loop is closed. You can't fix it. You can only get out before it traps you too."

The lift opens on floor 15. Wei's family pod. He doesn't move to exit.

"I could refuse," he says. "I could stay."

"You could." I step past him, hold the door. "And in six months, your score will be 42. In a year, 35. In two years, you'll be here." I tap my terminal. 30. "And then your parents will have lost you for nothing."

"It's not nothing—"

"Yes, it is." The words come out harder than I intend. "I'm already lost. You're not. Don't throw that away for— for what? Loyalty? I don't need your loyalty. I need you to survive."

Wei's face does something complicated. Guilt and relief and shame, all mixed together. He's a good kid. He doesn't want to abandon me. But he doesn't want to drown either.

"Go," I say. "Your parents are waiting."

He goes.

---

I live in a 4-square-meter sleeping pod on floor 12. Cheaper than family housing. The pod is barely large enough to lie down in. No window. No ventilation beyond the base ration. The air is stale and close.

I heat nutri-paste on the pod's single heating element. Brown slop, reconstituted protein. It tastes like nothing. I eat it because the alternative is not eating.

My wrist terminal chimes. Message from the Allocator:

> PERFORMANCE REVIEW: 30-DAY EVALUATION PERIOD INITIATED  
> CURRENT SCORE: 30  
> THRESHOLD: 25  
> TERMINATION REVIEW SCHEDULED: 32 DAYS

Thirty-two days.

I knew it was coming. Everyone knows. Below 30, you're flagged. Below 25, you're fired. "Natural attrition," they call it. "Optimization of resource allocation."

I call it disposal.

After termination: eviction from housing, loss of food rations, loss of water rations. You have maybe two weeks before dehydration or starvation. Some people go to the lower levels—floors 1-10, where the arcology's waste processing happens. No one lives there officially. But people do, in the dark, scavenging.

Most don't last long.

I lie down in the pod. My hands throb. My back is a constant scream. I take a painkiller—black market, expensive, cut with who-knows-what. The pain dulls to a roar.

Thirty-two days.

I should feel something. Fear, anger, despair. But I don't. I just feel tired.

The number falls whether I look or not.

---

Wei doesn't come to tower 89-C the next day. Or the day after. His transfer went through fast—his parents must have paid significant *guanxi* to expedite it.

I work alone. The tower feels larger without him. Emptier.

The conveyor jams twice. I clear it both times, but the delays cost me. 340 pods harvested. 9% contamination. The flickering LEDs are getting worse. I file a maintenance request. It's automatically denied—my score is too low to authorize equipment repair.

The system is elegant, really. The lower your score, the worse your equipment. The worse your equipment, the lower your score. Self-reinforcing. Self-optimizing. No human cruelty required. Just algorithms measuring outcomes.

At the end of the shift, I check my terminal: 29.

Twenty-nine days until review.

---

On day seven, Wei comes back.

I'm halfway through my shift when I see him at the tower entrance. He's not supposed to be here—his assignment is 95-B now, six floors up. But he's here, carrying something wrapped in recycled plastic.

"Budi," he says.

I stop working. The conveyor keeps moving, pods piling up. I'll pay for this in quota points. But I stop anyway.

Wei unwraps the plastic. Nutri-paste, the good kind—protein-enriched, vitamin-supplemented. Expensive. He must have spent a week's worth of credits.

"I can't stay," he says quickly. "My supervisor is tracking my time. But I wanted—"

He stops. Doesn't finish the sentence. Just holds out the nutri-paste.

I take it. Our hands touch briefly. His are smooth, uncramped. Mine shake.

"How's 95-B?" I ask.

"Good." He looks away. "The equipment is new. The LEDs don't flicker. The conveyor runs smooth. My quota is 700 pods. I make it every shift."

"Your score?"

"Sixty-two." He says it quietly. "It's rising."

"Good."

"Budi—"

"You should go. Your supervisor will dock you."

"I know." But he doesn't move. "My parents keep asking if I'm glad I transferred. If I'm grateful."

"Are you?"

Wei looks at me. Really looks at me. At my shaking hands, my hunched back, the smell of mineral solution and sweat that I can't wash away. At my terminal: 29.

"I see it now," he says. "What they did. What I did."

"You didn't do anything wrong."

"I abandoned you."

"You saved yourself." I turn back to the conveyor. Pods are backing up. I'll be here an extra hour clearing the jam. "That's not wrong. That's rational. The system rewards people who make that choice. Your parents made the right decision. You made the right decision."

"It doesn't feel right."

"It's not supposed to feel right. It's supposed to work." I start loading pods again. My hands scream in protest. "Go back to 95-B. Keep your score rising. Don't come here again."

"Why not?"

"Because the Allocator tracks associations. It sees you visiting a marked harvester. It adjusts your expected productivity downward. Your score will drop." I don't look at him. "I have twenty-nine days. You have a future. Don't trade one for the other."

Wei is quiet for a long time. Then: "What if we organized? All the harvesters in the low-score towers. What if we refused to—"

"To what? Quit?" I laugh. It sounds bitter. "There are no other jobs. Refuse to work? They'd just fire us faster. Demand better equipment? The Allocator controls allocation. It's not a negotiation. It's an optimization function."

"Someone designed that function."

"In 2047. The parameters are locked. It would take a supermajority vote of all arcology residents to change them. Two million people. That vote has never happened. It will never happen." I load another pod. "The system works, Wei. It works for your parents. It works for the people on floors 95-97. It works for the Allocator's efficiency metrics. It doesn't work for me, but I'm an acceptable loss. Optimal resource allocation means some resources get allocated to disposal."

"That's not—"

"Yes, it is." I finally look at him. "Go. Please. Don't come back. Don't let me be the reason your score falls."

Wei's face does that complicated thing again. But this time, I see something else underneath the guilt and shame.

Recognition.

He sees it now. The loop. The trap. The elegant self-reinforcing system that sorts people into winners and losers and calls it merit.

He sees it, and he's still trapped in it, and seeing doesn't change that.

"I'm sorry," he says.

"Don't be sorry. Just go."

He goes.

---

The terminal chimes every day now. Countdown to termination review.

28 days.  
27 days.  
26 days.

My score drops to 28. Then 27. The equipment is failing faster. Or maybe I'm failing faster. It's hard to tell the difference anymore.

Other harvesters avoid me completely now. They don't make eye contact in the corridors. They don't ride the lift with me. In the commissary, they sit at other tables.

Not cruelty. Fear.

I understand. I would do the same.

---

On day nineteen, the main conveyor in tower 89-C fails completely. Not a jam—a mechanical failure. The motor burns out. Smoke fills the cultivation chamber. Emergency lights activate.

I file an emergency maintenance request. It's flagged for supervisor review.

Two hours later, the response comes back: DENIED. Reason: Insufficient score authorization for major equipment repair. Alternative: Manual pod transport to backup conveyor, three floors up.

Manual transport.

I look at the distance. Three floors. Ninety meters vertical. 900 pods per shift. Each pod weighs 2.3 kilograms.

The math is simple. I can't do it.

But I try.

I carry pods, one by one, up three flights of emergency stairs to the backup conveyor on floor 92. My back screams. My hands cramp so badly I drop pods. Some crack open, contaminating. I go back for more.

After six hours, I've transported 340 pods. I'm shaking. My vision blurs. The pain is beyond the painkillers now.

My terminal chimes: 26.

I sit down on the stairs. Just for a moment. Just to catch my breath.

I don't get back up.

---

Someone finds me eventually. A supervisor, I think. They take me to the medical bay on floor 45. The doctor—tired, overworked, looking at her own terminal more than at me—scans me quickly.

"Severe muscle strain. Dehydration. Malnutrition." She doesn't look up. "You need rest. Minimum three days."

"I can't—"

"You can't work like this. You'll injure yourself permanently."

"My quota—"

"I'm filing a medical exemption. Three days off shift." She finally looks at me. Her expression is neutral. Professional. "Your score won't be penalized for the missed days."

But it will be. Medical exemptions pause the countdown, but they don't stop the score decay. Three days without production means three days of zero outcomes. The algorithm doesn't distinguish between "can't work" and "won't work."

I go back to my pod. I sleep for sixteen hours. When I wake, my terminal shows: 25.

Threshold reached. Termination review scheduled: 13 days.

---

Wei comes back on day twenty-three.

I'm in my pod when he knocks. I almost don't answer. But he keeps knocking, and eventually I open the door.

He looks different. Older. His score has risen to 67—I can see it on his terminal. But his face is drawn. Tired.

"I need to show you something," he says.

"Wei—"

"Please."

I follow him. We take the lift up—past floor 15, past floor 45, all the way to floor 89. But not to tower 89-C. To tower 89-A, on the opposite side of the farm.

There are people there. Maybe twenty harvesters. All low-score. I recognize some of them—the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, others I've seen in the corridors. All marked. All avoiding each other.

Except they're not avoiding each other now. They're together. Talking. Planning.

"What is this?" I ask.

"A meeting," Wei says. "We've been organizing. Quietly. People from the low-score towers. We're trying to—"

"Stop." I turn to leave. "This is a mistake."

"Budi, wait—"

"The Allocator monitors all communication. It flags productivity disruption. Everyone here will be marked for association."

"We're already marked," the woman from 89-B says. Her score is 33. "What more can they do to us?"

"Fire you faster." I look around the room. "You think you can change the system? You think twenty harvesters can force the Allocator to allocate resources differently? It's an optimization function. It doesn't negotiate."

"We're not trying to negotiate," the old man from 90-D says. His hands shake like mine. "We're trying to survive. Mutual aid. Shared equipment. If we pool resources—"

"You'll all be fired together instead of separately." I shake my head. "This won't work."

"Maybe not," Wei says. "But doing nothing definitely won't work."

I look at him. At his rising score, his smooth hands, his future. "Why are you here? You escaped. Your score is 67. You're safe."

"No one's safe." He says it quietly. "I see that now. Today it's you. Tomorrow it's someone else. Eventually, it's everyone. The system doesn't stop extracting just because you're in the middle. It just extracts from you more slowly."

"So what? You're going to sacrifice your score for— for what? Solidarity?"

"Maybe." He looks around at the others. "Or maybe we build something different. A way to share risk. To support each other. So when the equipment fails, we don't fall alone."

I want to tell him it won't work. I want to tell him the system is too strong, too optimized, too self-reinforcing. I want to tell him that seeing the trap doesn't mean you can escape it.

But I look at their faces—the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, the others—and I see something I haven't seen in four years.

Not hope. Hope would be naive.

Recognition. The same recognition I saw in Wei's face when he visited tower 89-C. The understanding that the system is constructed. That it's designed to sort them into disposal. That it works for some people because it doesn't work for them.

They see it. And seeing it hasn't saved them. But it's changed what they're willing to try.

"I have thirteen days," I say. "Then they terminate me. This won't change that."

"No," Wei agrees. "It won't."

"So why tell me?"

"Because you should know. That we're trying. That some of us see it now." He pauses. "And because you taught me to see it. The way you explained the loop. The way you told me to leave. You didn't say it was fair. You didn't say it was natural. You just said it was."

"It is."

"Yes." Wei nods. "But maybe it doesn't have to be."

---

I don't join them. I can't. I have thirteen days, and I need every credit I can earn to pay for food and water until the end.

But I watch.

Over the next week, I see small changes. Harvesters in the low-score towers helping each other. Sharing equipment tricks. Covering each other's sections when someone's hands cramp too badly to work. Small acts of mutual aid that the Allocator probably doesn't even notice.

It won't save them. The system is too strong. But it changes something in the air. The isolation breaks, just a little.

My score drops to 24. Then 23. The terminal chimes: TERMINATION REVIEW: 6 DAYS.

---

Wei comes back one more time. Day six.

He brings nutri-paste again. And water rations. And painkillers—real ones, not black market.

"I can't accept this," I say.

"Yes, you can." He puts them down in my pod. "My score is 71 now. I can afford it."

71. Rising fast. The premium tower is working. His parents' gamble paid off.

"You did the right thing," I tell him. "Leaving. Your parents were right."

"I know." He sits down—there's barely room for both of us in the pod, but he sits anyway. "But they were also wrong."

"How?"

"They saw the system. They understood it. They used it to protect me. That was right." He looks at his terminal. "But they couldn't see that the system itself was the problem. They thought if they just played it correctly, we'd be safe. They didn't see that the game is designed to extract from someone. And if it's not extracting from us, it's extracting from you."

"That's how optimization works."

"Yes." Wei meets my eyes. "But optimization for what? Maximum efficiency? Maximum output? At what cost?"

I don't answer. I don't have an answer.

"The others asked me to tell you something," Wei says. "The mutual aid network. It's growing. Twenty harvesters, now forty. People are sharing resources. Covering for each other. The Allocator hasn't flagged it yet—we're careful, we keep production up. But we're building something. A way to survive together."

"It won't last."

"Maybe not. But it's something." He stands to leave. "I'm going to keep my score up. I'm going to stay in the premium tower. My parents are right about that—I need to survive. But I'm also going to help. Credits, resources, information. Whatever I can give without dropping my own score below 60."

"Why?"

Wei pauses at the door. "Because you were right. The loop is closed. I can't fix it. But I can route around it. I can make sure that when the system extracts from someone, they don't fall alone."

He leaves.

---

Termination review: tomorrow.

I work my last shift in tower 89-C. The conveyor jams three times. I clear it each time, but it doesn't matter. My final count: 287 pods. 12% contamination. Earnings: 11 credits.

My terminal shows: 22.

Below threshold. Termination approved.

I take the lift down to floor 12 one last time. My pod access will be revoked at midnight. After that, I have seventy-two hours to vacate arcology housing. After that—disposal.

I sit in the pod. I should feel something. Fear, anger, despair. But I just feel tired.

The number fell. I knew it would. I couldn't stop it.

But.

I think about Wei's face. The recognition. The way he sees the system now—not as natural, not as inevitable, but as constructed. Designed. Changeable, maybe, even if he can't change it alone.

I think about the mutual aid network. Forty harvesters. Small acts of resistance. Not enough to save me. Not enough to break the system. But enough to show that the system can be resisted.

I think about the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, all the others. Still marked. Still trapped. But together now. Seeing together.

The number falls whether I look or not.

But Wei looked.

That's something.

**AUTHOR'S NOTE:** The following coda appears only in the analytical index, not in Budi's experience:

Six months after Budi's termination, the mutual aid network in the low-score towers has grown to 127 harvesters. They've developed protocols: shared equipment maintenance, collective bargaining with supervisors, distributed risk pooling. Three harvesters have recovered from scores below 40.

Wei's score is 74. He contributes 15% of his credits to the network. His parents don't know.

The Allocator has not flagged the network. Its optimization metrics show stable productivity in the low-score towers. What it cannot measure: the shift from isolation to solidarity. The recognition spreading through the floors.

Budi was right. He couldn't fix the loop. But his teaching—the way he explained the system to Wei, the way he refused to pretend it was fair—planted something.

Not hope. Hope would be naive.

A seed.
