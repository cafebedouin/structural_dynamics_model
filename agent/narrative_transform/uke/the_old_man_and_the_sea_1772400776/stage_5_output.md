# REVISED STORY

The conveyor jams at 14:23.

I count because counting helps. Thirty-seven seconds to clear it. My wrist terminal flickers: 30.

I don't look anymore. The number falls whether I look or not.

My hands cramp. *Krampen*, my grandmother called it. The ache in her knees. The conveyor starts—metal grinding, hydraulics hissing. I reach for the next pod. My fingers barely close.

Tower 89-C smells like mineral solution and rot. The LED arrays flicker. Half are dead. The algae grows pale in bad light. Not my fault. Just the equipment.

The terminal doesn't measure fault. It measures outcomes.

Twist, pull, load. My back screams. Twist, pull, load. If I think about the pain, I slow down. If I slow down, the quota slips. If the quota slips, the number falls.

900 pods per shift. I'm at 380, hour nine. I won't make it. I never make it anymore.

Across the tower, Wei works. Fast, smooth. His hands don't cramp yet. He's nineteen. I watch sometimes—the way his fingers close around pods, quick and sure. The way he doesn't have to stop and shake out the pain.

That was me once. Score 78. Tower 92-A. Good equipment. I had an apprentice then. A girl, sharp and quick.

Then the contamination in '85. Fungal outbreak. Three towers crashed. My score dropped to 52. They moved me to 90-B. Older equipment. Worse outcomes. Score dropped to 44. They moved me to 89-C.

Four years later: 30.

---

Wei finishes first. He always does. I hear him check flow rates, verify nutrients. Thorough. The way I taught him.

Three months ago, they assigned him here. New apprentice. I was surprised—harvesters below 35 don't usually get apprentices. But they needed someone for 89-C.

I liked him immediately. Quiet. Good hands. He listened when I showed him contamination signs, how to coax speed from failing equipment, how to read the algae's color.

His score was 65 when he started.

Now it's 58.

I can see it in his parents' faces when they visit. The way his mother's mouth goes tight. The way his father pulls him aside during breaks, speaking low in Mandarin.

*Guanxi.* Network, favor-debt. They're calling in every connection.

To get him away from me.

---

Shift ends at 18:00. Terminal displays the damage: 412 pods. 7% contamination. Conveyor jam logged as equipment failure, but it still counts. Deductions: 18 credits.

18 credits × 25 shifts = 450 this month. Rent: 280. Food: 120 minimum. Water: 40 minimum.

Total: 440. I'll skip water rations again. The smell doesn't bother me anymore. But it bothers everyone else. They avoid me in corridors. Not just the score—the smell. Poverty is visible.

Wei waits by the exit. He shouldn't be. His shift ended fifteen minutes ago.

"Budi," he says. Just my name.

I nod. We walk to the lift. The silence is comfortable.

The lift is empty. Good. People don't like to ride with me. *Menular*, they whisper. Contagious.

Maybe it is. Wei's dropped seven points.

"My parents found a placement," Wei says. Flat. Practiced. "Tower 95-B. Premium assignment. I start next week."

I nod. Of course they did. I've been watching—the hushed conversations, the careful negotiations, the way they stopped inviting me to meals after the first month.

"The harvester there is good. Score 82. He needs an apprentice."

"That's good. You'll learn a lot."

"I learned a lot here."

"You'll learn more there. Better equipment. Your score will stabilize."

Wei looks at his terminal. 58, glowing amber. Below 50, recovery is nearly impossible. His parents know this.

"I don't want to leave."

"Yes, you do. You want to leave, and you should. Your parents are right. Working with me is killing your score."

"It's not your fault—"

"It doesn't matter whose fault. Outcomes depend on equipment. Equipment depends on score. Score depends on outcomes." I've said this before. To myself, mostly. "You can't fix it. You can only get out before it traps you too."

The lift opens on floor 15. Wei's family pod. He doesn't move.

"I could refuse. I could stay."

"You could. And in six months, your score will be 42. In a year, 35. In two years, you'll be here." I tap my terminal. 30. "And your parents will have lost you for nothing."

"It's not nothing—"

"Yes, it is. I'm already lost. You're not. Don't throw that away for loyalty. I don't need your loyalty. I need you to survive."

Wei's face does something complicated. Guilt and relief and shame, all mixed.

"Go. Your parents are waiting."

He goes.

---

I live in a 4-square-meter pod on floor 12. The pod is barely large enough to lie down. No window. No ventilation beyond base ration. The air is stale.

I heat nutri-paste on the single element. Brown slop. It tastes like nothing. I eat it because the alternative is not eating.

My terminal chimes:

> PERFORMANCE REVIEW: 30-DAY EVALUATION INITIATED  
> CURRENT SCORE: 30  
> THRESHOLD: 25  
> TERMINATION REVIEW: 32 DAYS

Thirty-two days.

I knew it was coming. Below 30, you're flagged. Below 25, you're fired. After termination: eviction, loss of rations. You have maybe two weeks before dehydration or starvation. Some people go to the lower levels—floors 1-10, where waste processing happens. No one lives there officially.

Most don't last long.

I lie down. My hands throb. My back screams. I take a painkiller—black market, expensive, cut with who-knows-what. The pain dulls.

Thirty-two days.

I should feel something. Fear, anger, despair. But I just feel tired.

---

Wei doesn't come to 89-C the next day. Or the day after. His transfer went through fast—his parents must have paid significant *guanxi*.

I work alone. The tower feels larger without him.

The conveyor jams twice. I clear it both times, but the delays cost me. 340 pods. 9% contamination. The flickering LEDs are getting worse. I file a maintenance request. Automatically denied—my score is too low to authorize repair.

End of shift, my terminal shows: 29.

Twenty-nine days until review.

---

On day seven, Wei comes back.

I'm halfway through shift when I see him at the entrance. He's not supposed to be here—his assignment is 95-B now. But he's here, carrying something wrapped in plastic.

"Budi."

I stop working. The conveyor keeps moving, pods piling up. I'll pay for this in quota points. But I stop.

Wei unwraps the plastic. Nutri-paste, the good kind—protein-enriched, vitamin-supplemented. Expensive.

"I can't stay. My supervisor is tracking my time. But I wanted—"

He stops. Just holds out the paste.

I take it. Our hands touch briefly. His are smooth. Mine shake.

"How's 95-B?"

"Good. The equipment is new. The LEDs don't flicker. My quota is 700. I make it every shift."

"Your score?"

"Sixty-two. It's rising."

"Good."

"Budi—"

"You should go. Your supervisor will dock you."

"I know." But he doesn't move. "My parents keep asking if I'm glad I transferred."

"Are you?"

Wei looks at me. Really looks. At my shaking hands, my hunched back, the smell I can't wash away. At my terminal: 29.

"I see it now. What they did. What I did."

"You didn't do anything wrong."

"I abandoned you."

"You saved yourself. That's not wrong." I turn back to the conveyor. Pods are backing up. "Go back to 95-B. Keep your score rising. Don't come here again."

"Why not?"

"Because they track associations. They see you visiting a marked harvester. They adjust your expected productivity. Your score will drop." I don't look at him. "I have twenty-nine days. You have a future. Don't trade one for the other."

Wei is quiet. Then: "What if we organized? All the harvesters in the low-score towers. What if we—"

"To what? Quit? There are no other jobs. Refuse to work? They'd just fire us faster. Demand better equipment?" I load another pod. "Someone designed this in 2047. The parameters are locked. It would take a supermajority vote to change them. Two million people. That vote has never happened."

"That's not—"

"Yes, it is." I finally look at him. "Go. Please. Don't come back. Don't let me be the reason your score falls."

Wei's face does that complicated thing again. But this time, I see something else underneath.

He sees it now. The loop. The trap.

He sees it, and he's still trapped in it, and seeing doesn't change that.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just go."

He goes.

---

The terminal chimes every day.

28 days. 27 days. 26 days.

My score drops to 28. Then 27. The equipment is failing faster. Or maybe I'm failing faster.

Other harvesters avoid me completely now. They don't make eye contact. They don't ride the lift with me. In the commissary, they sit at other tables.

Not cruelty. Fear.

I understand. I would do the same.

---

On day nineteen, the main conveyor in 89-C fails completely. Not a jam—mechanical failure. The motor burns out. Smoke fills the chamber. Emergency lights activate.

I file an emergency maintenance request. Flagged for supervisor review.

Two hours later: DENIED. Insufficient score authorization. Alternative: Manual pod transport to backup conveyor, three floors up.

Manual transport.

I look at the distance. Three floors. Ninety meters vertical. 900 pods per shift.

I can't do it.

But I try.

I carry pods, one by one, up three flights to the backup conveyor on floor 92. My back screams. My hands cramp so badly I drop pods. Some crack, contaminating. I go back for more.

After six hours, I've transported 340 pods. I'm shaking. My vision blurs.

My terminal chimes: 26.

I sit down on the stairs. Just for a moment. Just to catch my breath.

I don't get back up.

---

Someone finds me eventually. A supervisor. They take me to medical bay on floor 45. The doctor scans me quickly.

"Severe muscle strain. Dehydration. Malnutrition. You need rest. Minimum three days."

"I can't—"

"You can't work like this. I'm filing a medical exemption. Three days off shift."

But it will count. Medical exemptions pause the countdown, but they don't stop the score decay. Three days without production means three days of zero outcomes.

I go back to my pod. I sleep for sixteen hours. When I wake, my terminal shows: 25.

Threshold reached. Termination review: 13 days.

---

Wei comes back on day twenty-three.

I'm in my pod when he knocks. I almost don't answer. But he keeps knocking.

He looks different. Older. His score has risen to 67. But his face is drawn.

"I need to show you something."

"Wei—"

"Please."

I follow him. We take the lift up to floor 89. But not to 89-C. To 89-A, opposite side.

There are people there. Maybe twenty harvesters. All low-score. I recognize some—the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, others I've seen in corridors. All marked. All avoiding each other.

Except they're not avoiding each other now. They're together. Talking.

"What is this?"

"A meeting. We've been organizing. Quietly. People from the low-score towers. We're trying to—"

"Stop." I turn to leave. "This is a mistake."

"Budi, wait—"

"They monitor all communication. They flag productivity disruption. Everyone here will be marked."

"We're already marked," the woman from 89-B says. Her score is 33. "What more can they do?"

"Fire you faster." I look around. "You think twenty harvesters can force different allocation? It doesn't negotiate."

"We're not trying to negotiate," the old man from 90-D says. His hands shake like mine. "We're trying to survive. Mutual aid. Shared equipment. If we pool resources—"

"You'll all be fired together instead of separately. This won't work."

"Maybe not," Wei says. "But doing nothing definitely won't."

I look at him. At his rising score, his smooth hands, his future. "Why are you here? You escaped. Your score is 67. You're safe."

"No one's safe. I see that now. Today it's you. Tomorrow it's someone else."

"So what? You're going to sacrifice your score for solidarity?"

"Maybe. Or maybe we build something different. A way to share risk. So when the equipment fails, we don't fall alone."

I want to tell him it won't work. I want to tell him the system is too strong.

But I look at their faces—the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, the others—and I see something I haven't seen in four years.

Recognition.

They see it. And seeing it hasn't saved them. But it's changed what they're willing to try.

"I have thirteen days. Then they terminate me. This won't change that."

"No," Wei agrees. "It won't."

"So why tell me?"

"Because you should know. That we're trying. That some of us see it now." He pauses. "And because you taught me to see it. The way you explained the loop. The way you told me to leave. You didn't say it was fair. You didn't say it was natural. You just said it was."

"It is."

"Yes. But maybe it doesn't have to be."

---

I don't join them. I can't. I have thirteen days, and I need every credit I can earn.

But I watch.

Over the next week, I see small changes. Harvesters in the low-score towers helping each other. Sharing equipment tricks. Covering sections when someone's hands cramp too badly. Small acts the system probably doesn't even notice.

It won't save them. But it changes something in the air. The isolation breaks, just a little.

My score drops to 24. Then 23. The terminal chimes: TERMINATION REVIEW: 6 DAYS.

---

Wei comes back one more time. Day six.

He brings nutri-paste again. And water rations. And painkillers—real ones.

"I can't accept this."

"Yes, you can." He puts them down. "My score is 71 now. I can afford it."

71. Rising fast. The premium tower is working. His parents' gamble paid off.

"You did the right thing. Leaving. Your parents were right."

"I know." He sits—there's barely room for both of us, but he sits. "But they were also wrong."

"How?"

"They saw the system. They understood it. They used it to protect me. That was right. But they couldn't see—" He stops. Looks at his terminal. "They thought if they just played it correctly, we'd be safe. They didn't see that it extracts from someone. And if it's not us, it's you."

I don't answer.

"The others asked me to tell you something. The mutual aid network. It's growing. Twenty harvesters, now forty. People are sharing resources. Covering for each other. We're careful, we keep production up. But we're building something."

"It won't last."

"Maybe not. But it's something." He stands. "I'm going to keep my score up. I'm going to stay in the premium tower. My parents are right about that—I need to survive. But I'm also going to help. Credits, resources, information. Whatever I can give without dropping below 60."

"Why?"

Wei pauses at the door. "Because you were right. The loop is closed. I can't fix it. But I can route around it. I can make sure that when it extracts from someone, they don't fall alone."

He leaves.

---

Termination review: tomorrow.

I work my last shift in 89-C. The conveyor jams three times. I clear it each time, but it doesn't matter. Final count: 287 pods. 12% contamination. Earnings: 11 credits.

My terminal shows: 22.

Below threshold. Termination approved.

I take the lift down to floor 12 one last time. My pod access will be revoked at midnight. After that, I have seventy-two hours to vacate. After that—disposal.

I sit in the pod. I should feel something. Fear, anger, despair. But I just feel tired.

The number fell. I knew it would. I couldn't stop it.

But.

I think about Wei's face. The recognition. The way he sees the system now—not as natural, not as inevitable, but as constructed.

I think about the mutual aid network. Forty harvesters. Small acts of resistance. Not enough to save me. Not enough to break the system. But enough to show that it can be resisted.

I think about the woman from 89-B, the old man from 90-D, all the others. Still marked. Still trapped. But together now. Seeing together.

The number falls whether I look or not.

But Wei looked.
