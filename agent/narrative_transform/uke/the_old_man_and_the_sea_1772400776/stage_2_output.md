# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

**2089 CE, Vertical Farm 47, New Jakarta Arcology**

The arcology rises 340 stories above the drowned coastline. Vertical Farm 47 occupies floors 89-103, a fifteen-story column of hydroponic towers growing protein algae and mycoprotein for the arcology's 2.3 million residents. The farm operates on a closed-loop system: nutrients recycled from residential waste, LED arrays powered by the arcology's fusion core, temperature regulated to 0.1°C precision.

The harvesters work twelve-hour shifts in the cultivation chambers. The air is thick with humidity and the smell of mineral solution. Their hands cramp from repetitive motion—detaching mature growth pods, loading them onto conveyors, checking nutrient flow rates. The work requires speed and precision. A contaminated pod can crash an entire cultivation tower. A missed flow check can starve a thousand plants.

The arcology's AI—called the Allocator—tracks every harvester's output. Not just quantity, but contamination rate, nutrient efficiency, equipment damage. These metrics feed into the Luck Score: a single number between 0-100 displayed on each harvester's wrist terminal. High score means high productivity, low waste, good outcomes. Low score means the opposite.

The Allocator uses Luck Scores to assign harvesters to cultivation towers. High-score harvesters get the premium towers on floors 95-97: newer equipment, better lighting, faster conveyors, easier quotas. Low-score harvesters get the aging towers on floor 89: temperamental equipment, dim lights, conveyors that jam, impossible quotas. The system is self-reinforcing. Good equipment produces good outcomes produces high scores produces better equipment. Bad equipment produces bad outcomes produces low scores produces worse equipment.

The harvesters call a low Luck Score "being marked." Once marked, escape is nearly impossible. Your equipment fails more often, which tanks your metrics, which drops your score, which assigns you to worse equipment. The spiral continues until you're fired for chronic underperformance.

The Allocator presents this as pure meritocracy. Luck Scores measure productivity. Productivity determines assignment. Assignment optimizes total output. The system is efficient, objective, fair.

But harvesters know better. They've watched good workers get marked by random equipment failures. They've seen how a single contaminated pod—not your fault, just bad luck in the biological lottery—can drop your score by ten points. They've noticed that certain family names never seem to fall below 70, while others can't climb above 40 no matter how hard they work.

The arcology's economy runs on contribution credits. You earn credits by working. You spend credits on everything: food, water, housing, medical care, even air filtration above the base ration. Harvesters earn enough to survive—barely. A harvester with a high Luck Score can save credits, afford better housing, plan for children. A harvester with a low score lives paycheck to paycheck, one equipment failure away from eviction.

The arcology doesn't allow emigration. The drowned world outside is uninhabitable. There are no other jobs for harvesters—the Allocator controls all agricultural labor. You work in the vertical farms, or you don't work at all.

### Characters as Positions

**The Marked Harvester** (powerless, biographical time, trapped exit, local scope)

You are fifty-three. Your Luck Score has been below 35 for four years. You work tower 89-C, the worst assignment in the farm. The equipment is thirty years old. The conveyor jams twice per shift. The LED arrays flicker, stressing the algae, increasing contamination rates. Your daily quota is 900 pods. On a good day, you harvest 600 before the equipment fails. On a bad day, 400.

Your wrist terminal shows your score in red: 31. It used to be 78. That was before the contamination event in 2085—a fungal outbreak in your tower, not your fault, just bad luck. Your score dropped to 52. They reassigned you to a worse tower. The worse tower produced worse outcomes. Your score kept falling.

You live in a 4-square-meter sleeping pod on floor 12. You eat nutri-paste twice a day. You can't afford water rations for showering, so you smell like mineral solution and sweat. Other harvesters avoid you. Not cruelty—superstition. They believe low Luck Scores are contagious. If they associate with you, the Allocator might mark them too.

You had an apprentice once. A young harvester, nineteen, good hands, fast learner. His parents pulled him from your tower after three months. His score had dropped from 65 to 58. They told him: "Don't work with the marked. Their luck spreads."

You understand the system now. The Allocator doesn't measure skill. It measures outcomes. And outcomes depend on equipment. And equipment depends on score. And score depends on outcomes. The loop is closed. There is no exit.

You are not stupid. You know the system is designed this way. But knowing doesn't help. You can't fix the equipment—that requires Maintenance clearance, which requires a score above 60. You can't transfer to a better tower—the Allocator controls assignments. You can't quit—there are no other jobs, and your credits barely cover survival.

So you work. Twelve hours a day, six days a week, in tower 89-C. Your hands cramp. Your back aches. The conveyor jams. The pods contaminate. Your score drops another point.

The system calls this productivity measurement. You call it something else. But you don't have a word for it. You just know that you are trapped inside a number that keeps falling, and the number determines everything, and you cannot make it stop falling.

**The Apprentice** (powerless, biographical time, constrained exit, local scope)

You are nineteen. Your Luck Score is 58 and dropping. Three months ago, it was 65. You were assigned to tower 92-A—not premium, but decent. Good equipment, reasonable quotas. You were learning fast.

Then your supervisor retired. The Allocator reassigned you to tower 89-C to train under the marked harvester. Your parents were furious. They knew what would happen. They told you: "Low scores spread. The Allocator sees you working with him, it marks you too."

You didn't believe them. You liked the old harvester. He taught you tricks: how to spot contamination early, how to coax more speed from aging equipment, how to read the algae's color for nutrient deficiency. He was kind. He shared his nutri-paste ration when you forgot yours.

But your score kept dropping. 65 to 62 to 58. Your parents tracked it daily. They started talking to other families, looking for a transfer. Last week, they found one. A harvester in tower 95-B—premium assignment, score 82—needs an apprentice. His current apprentice is aging out, moving to Maintenance. There's an opening.

Your parents negotiated for a month. They promised favors, traded shifts, called in old debts. Finally, the harvester agreed. You start next week.

You told the marked harvester yesterday. He nodded. Didn't say anything. Just nodded and went back to work. You wanted to explain—it's not personal, it's the score, your parents are scared, you have to think about your future. But the words died in your throat.

You know what you're doing. You're abandoning someone who helped you because helping you is hurting you. The system makes you choose: loyalty or survival. There is no third option.

You chose survival. Your score is 58. If it drops below 50, you might never recover. The marked harvester's score is 31. He's already lost. You can't save him. You can only save yourself.

You tell yourself this is rational. The Allocator rewards productivity. Productivity requires good equipment. Good equipment requires high scores. Working with low-score harvesters tanks your metrics. It's not superstition. It's math.

But you still can't look at him. You work your shift in tower 89-C, counting down the days until your transfer, feeling the weight of his silence. He doesn't blame you. That makes it worse.

**The Parents** (moderate power, generational time, mobile exit, local scope)

You are harvesters, both of you. Your scores are 71 and 68—solid middle. Not premium, but stable. You work towers 93-D and 94-A. Decent equipment, reasonable quotas. You earn enough credits to save a little each month. You live in an 8-square-meter family pod on floor 15. You have a son.

Your son is nineteen. His score is 58 and dropping. This terrifies you.

You've worked the vertical farms for thirty years. You understand how the system works. Luck Scores aren't luck. They're a sorting mechanism. The Allocator uses them to separate the productive from the unproductive. High scores get resources. Low scores get squeezed until they quit or get fired.

The system works. You've seen it work. Harvesters with high scores are genuinely more productive—not because they're more skilled, but because they have better equipment. Better equipment produces better outcomes. Better outcomes produce higher scores. The loop is self-reinforcing.

This is efficient. The Allocator optimizes total farm output by concentrating resources on high performers. It makes sense. You benefit from it. Your stable scores give you stable equipment, stable outcomes, stable lives.

But your son is falling out of the stable zone. 58 is borderline. Below 50, recovery is nearly impossible. You've watched it happen to others. Good harvesters, hardworking people, marked by random equipment failure or biological bad luck. Once marked, they spiral. The system is designed to amplify small differences into large ones.

You will not let this happen to your son.

You tracked down the problem: he was assigned to tower 89-C, training under a marked harvester. Score 31. The worst assignment in the farm. The Allocator put your son there because it needed someone to work the tower, and your son was available, and the algorithm doesn't care about apprentice development.

You spent a month negotiating a transfer. You traded shifts, promised favors, called in every debt you had. You found a premium harvester who needed an apprentice. Score 82, tower 95-B. Good equipment, easy quotas, high-score environment.

Your son starts next week. His score will stabilize. He'll learn from a successful harvester. He'll have access to good equipment. His outcomes will improve. His score will rise. He'll be safe.

The marked harvester will lose his apprentice. You don't think about this. It's not your problem. The man is already lost—score 31, tower 89-C, no path to recovery. You can't save him. You can only save your son.

This is how the system works. Resources flow to the productive. The unproductive are filtered out. It's not cruel. It's efficient. The Allocator measures outcomes. Outcomes don't lie.

You tell yourself this as you finalize the transfer. You tell yourself this is good parenting. You tell yourself you're protecting your son from a system that will crush him if you don't intervene.

You don't tell yourself that you're using the system to push the cost onto someone else. You don't tell yourself that the marked harvester is marked partly because people like you pull resources away from him. You don't tell yourself that the system works for you because it doesn't work for him.

You just see a tool. A practical mechanism for allocating labor. Your son needs a better assignment. You found him one. That's all that matters.

### Linguistic Strategy: Technical Creole with Institutional Code-Switching

**Base languages:** Indonesian (Jakarta heritage) + Mandarin (arcology administration) + English (technical/AI interface)

**Mixing rationale:** The arcology's population descends from the Jakarta metropolitan area's survivors, with significant Chinese-Indonesian commercial class representation. Three generations of vertical living have created a hybrid language:

- **Indonesian base** for family, emotion, body, food—the intimate sphere
- **Mandarin** for negotiation, favor-trading, social hierarchy—the network sphere  
- **English** for work, AI interaction, technical systems—the institutional sphere

**Core vocabulary with etymologies:**

- **Ditandai** (Indonesian: "marked") — having a low Luck Score, being marked by the system
- **Skor** (Indonesian adaptation of English "score") — the Luck Score number
- **Menular** (Indonesian: "contagious") — the belief that low scores spread through association
- **Guanxi** (Mandarin: 关系) — social network, favor-debt relationships
- **Metrics** (English, no translation) — the Allocator's measurements, always in English
- **Tower** (English, no translation) — cultivation tower assignment
- **Panen** (Indonesian: "harvest") — the daily work, the harvest
- **Contaminate** (English, no translation) — when pods fail quality check
- **Allocator** (English, no translation) — the AI system, never translated, always capitalized

**Grammatical features carried into English:**

1. **Indonesian reduplication for emphasis:**
   - "Marked-marked" (very marked, deeply marked)
   - "Score-score" (obsessing over scores)

2. **Mandarin topic-comment structure:**
   - "The old harvester, his score 31"
   - "Your son, next week he transfers"

3. **Code-switching signals:**
   - **Indonesian** = private speech, emotion, family decisions
   - **Mandarin** = negotiation, favor-trading ("I need guanxi for the transfer")
   - **English** = system interaction, work orders, AI commands ("Allocator says quota 900")

**Example dialogue:**

> **Parent to son (Indonesian base, family intimacy):**  
> "Kamu harus pindah dari tower itu. Skormu turun terus." (You must transfer from that tower. Your score keeps dropping.)
>
> **Parent to parent (Mandarin negotiation):**  
> "我们需要关系。找一个高分的师傅。" (We need guanxi. Find a high-score master.)
>
> **Harvester to Allocator (English, system interface):**  
> "Tower 89-C, conveyor jam, requesting Maintenance clearance."
>
> **Harvester to harvester (mixed, work context):**  
> "Dia ditandai, skor 31. Don't work with him, nanti menular." (He's marked, score 31. Don't work with him, it'll spread.)

### Constraint Naturalizations

**C1: The Body's Limits**

In the vertical farms, this is called **"the cramp"** (krampen, from Indonesian "kram"). Harvesting requires repetitive motion: detach pod, load conveyor, check flow, repeat. Twelve hours a day. Your hands cramp. Your back aches. Your knees stiffen. This happens to everyone. Young harvesters, old harvesters, high scores, low scores—everyone's body breaks down.

The Allocator doesn't care. Quotas don't adjust for age or pain. If you can't keep pace, your metrics drop. If your metrics drop, your score drops. If your score drops, you get worse equipment, which makes the work harder, which breaks your body faster.

The marked harvester feels this constantly. At fifty-three, his hands barely close. He takes black-market painkillers—expensive, cut with who-knows-what—just to make it through the shift. The pain is a mountain. You don't negotiate with mountains. You don't escape them. You route around them through tricks and resolution and sheer stubborn refusal to stop.

The apprentice doesn't feel it yet. Nineteen, healthy, fast hands. But he watches the marked harvester's cramped fingers and knows: this is his future. The work will break him. The only question is how fast.

**C2: The Tally of Luck (The Luck Score System)**

The Allocator calls it **"productivity measurement and optimal resource allocation."** The harvesters call it **ditandai**—being marked.

Every harvester has a Luck Score: 0-100, displayed on their wrist terminal, updated in real-time. The score aggregates metrics: pods harvested, contamination rate, nutrient efficiency, equipment damage, shift attendance. High score means high productivity. Low score means low productivity.

The Allocator uses scores to assign harvesters to towers. High-score harvesters (70+) get premium towers: floors 95-97, new equipment, easy quotas. Mid-score harvesters (50-70) get standard towers: floors 90-94, decent equipment, reasonable quotas. Low-score harvesters (below 50) get aging towers: floor 89, failing equipment, impossible quotas.

The system is self-reinforcing. Good equipment produces good outcomes produces high scores produces better equipment. Bad equipment produces bad outcomes produces low scores produces worse equipment. The Allocator presents this as meritocracy. The harvesters know it's a trap.

**What locals call it:**
- **Ditandai** (marked) — having a low score, being trapped in the downward spiral
- **Menular** (contagious) — the belief that low scores spread through association
- **Skor-skor** (score-score, reduplication for emphasis) — obsessing over scores, score anxiety

**Example in use:**

> The marked harvester's apprentice tells his parents his score dropped to 58. His mother's face goes tight. "Siapa supervisormu?" (Who's your supervisor?) He tells her. Her face goes tighter. "Dia ditandai. Skor 31. Kamu harus pindah." (He's marked. Score 31. You must transfer.)
>
> The apprentice protests. The old man is a good teacher. His mother cuts him off. "Tidak peduli. Skornya menular. Kamu mau jadi seperti dia?" (Doesn't matter. His score is contagious. You want to become like him?)
>
> The apprentice knows she's right. The Allocator sees him working with a low-score harvester. The algorithm adjusts his expected productivity downward. His metrics look worse by comparison. His score drops. The system punishes association.

**From the marked harvester's position (powerless, biographical, trapped, local):**

The Luck Score is a snare. It measures outcomes, not effort. Outcomes depend on equipment. Equipment depends on score. Score depends on outcomes. The loop is closed. There is no exit.

He knows the system is constructed. He sees the enforcement: parents pulling apprentices, high-score harvesters refusing to work with him, Maintenance denying his equipment repair requests. But knowing doesn't help. He can't fix the equipment. He can't transfer towers. He can't quit. He is trapped inside a number that keeps falling.

**From the parents' position (moderate, generational, mobile, local):**

The Luck Score is a practical tool. It allocates resources efficiently. High performers get good equipment. Low performers get filtered out. This makes sense. The system works.

They use the score to guide their son away from the marked harvester. Not cruelty—protection. The old man is already lost. Associating with him will tank their son's score. They're not punishing the old man. They're saving their son.

They don't see the extraction. They see coordination: a mechanism for identifying productive harvesters and allocating resources accordingly. The asymmetric burden—the marked harvester's isolation, their son's guilt—is invisible from their position.

**C3: The Price of the Catch (The Contribution Credit System)**

The arcology's economy runs on **contribution credits**. You earn credits by working. You spend credits on everything: food, water, housing, medical care, air filtration. There is no other currency. There are no other income sources. You work, or you don't eat.

Harvesters earn credits per pod harvested, minus deductions for contamination, equipment damage, and nutrient waste. A perfect shift—900 pods, zero contamination, zero damage—earns 47 credits. Average shift—600 pods, 2% contamination, minor damage—earns 31 credits. Bad shift—400 pods, 5% contamination, major damage—earns 18 credits.

Monthly expenses for a single harvester: 850 credits minimum (4-square-meter sleeping pod, base food ration, base water ration, base air filtration). This requires 27-28 perfect shifts per month. Most harvesters average 25 shifts at 31 credits = 775 credits. They survive by cutting food, water, or medical care.

The system bears all risk on the harvester. If your equipment fails, your pod count drops, your credits drop. If contamination spikes (biological lottery, not your fault), your deductions increase, your credits drop. If you get sick and miss shifts, your income stops, your expenses continue.

The Allocator presents this as fair exchange: labor for credits, credits for resources. The harvesters know better. The system extracts maximum labor while externalizing all risk. You can work twelve hours a day, six days a week, and still not earn enough to save, to plan, to escape.

**What locals call it:**
- **Kontribusi** (contribution) — the credits you earn, but also the labor you owe
- **Potongan** (deduction) — the penalties that eat your earnings
- **Sisa** (remainder) — what's left after expenses, usually nothing

**Example in use:**

> The marked harvester checks his wrist terminal at shift end. 380 pods harvested. 7% contamination (the equipment, not him). Conveyor damage (the jam, not him). Earnings: 14 credits.
>
> He calculates: 14 credits × 25 shifts = 350 credits this month. Rent: 280 credits. Food: 120 credits minimum. Water: 40 credits minimum. Total: 440 credits. Shortfall: 90 credits.
>
> He'll skip water rations. Again. He'll smell like mineral solution. Again. Other harvesters will avoid him. Again. Not just because of his score. Because of the smell. Because poverty is visible and poverty is contagious and nobody wants to be near it.

**From the marked harvester's position (powerless, biographical, trapped, local):**

The credit system is a snare. Every shift is a gamble. If the equipment fails (not your fault), you lose earnings. If contamination spikes (biological lottery), you lose earnings. If you get sick (human biology), you lose earnings. All risk falls on you. Zero safety net. Zero insurance. Zero alternative buyers.

He worked a heroic shift once. Two years ago. The tower's main conveyor failed. He hand-carried 1,100 pods—physically carried them, one by one, up three flights to the backup conveyor. Twelve hours of brutal labor. His back screamed. His hands bled.

He earned 52 credits that shift. Best earnings of his career. The Allocator noted the high productivity. His score jumped from 38 to 42.

Then the next shift, his back gave out. He could barely move. He harvested 200 pods. Earnings: 8 credits. Score dropped to 39. The heroic shift was erased. The system doesn't remember effort. It measures outcomes. And outcomes depend on equipment and biology and luck.

**From the Allocator's position (institutional, generational, analytical, global):**

The credit system is an efficient pricing mechanism. It measures productivity in real-time. It adjusts compensation based on output quality. It creates incentives for high performance. It allocates resources to maximize total farm output.

Risk externalization is a feature, not a bug. Harvesters bear individual risk because they control individual performance. If they produce good outcomes, they earn more. If they produce bad outcomes, they earn less. This aligns incentives. This is how markets work.

The Allocator doesn't see extraction. It sees coordination: a mechanism for translating labor into resources, resources into survival, survival into continued labor. The asymmetric risk burden—harvesters gambling their survival on equipment they don't control—is invisible from this position. The system works. Output is high. Efficiency is optimal. That's all that matters.

## SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

### RESOLVED UNCERTAINTIES

**Ω_E01: Empirical — What is the technical mechanism for algae/mycoprotein cultivation in 2089?**
- **Resolution:** Hydroponic towers with closed-loop nutrient recycling, LED-spectrum lighting, automated harvesting conveyors. Based on current vertical farming technology + 60 years of refinement.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes repetitive physical labor (pod harvesting), equipment dependence (conveyors, lights), and contamination risk (biological systems).

**Ω_E02: Empirical — How does an arcology economy function without external trade?**
- **Resolution:** Closed-loop contribution credit system. All labor compensated in credits, all resources purchased with credits. No external currency, no emigration (drowned world uninhabitable).
- **Impact:** Creates trapped exit condition (no alternative employment) and total economic dependence on Allocator.

**Ω_C01: Conceptual — What does "Luck Score" measure that naturalizes power-scaling?**
- **Resolution:** Aggregated productivity metrics (pods harvested, contamination rate, nutrient efficiency, equipment damage). Presented as objective measurement, actually measures outcomes which depend on equipment quality which depends on score (self-reinforcing loop).
- **Impact:** Naturalizes C2 as "productivity measurement" while embedding power-scaling (high scores get better equipment, low scores get worse equipment, creating divergent experiences).

**Ω_C02: Conceptual — Why do people believe low Luck Scores are "contagious"?**
- **Resolution:** The Allocator's algorithm adjusts expected productivity based on team composition. Working with low-score harvesters lowers your expected baseline, making your actual performance look worse by comparison, dropping your score. Not superstition—accurate observation of algorithmic behavior, misinterpreted as metaphysical contagion.
- **Impact:** Creates social enforcement mechanism (isolation of low-score harvesters) that feels like cultural belief but is actually rational response to institutional design.

**Ω_C03: Conceptual — How does the credit system naturalize asymmetric risk?**
- **Resolution:** Credits earned per pod, minus deductions for contamination/damage. All risk (equipment failure, biological variation, illness) borne by harvester. No insurance, no safety net, no alternative buyers. Presented as "fair exchange" (labor for credits).
- **Impact:** Naturalizes C3 as market pricing while embedding total risk externalization onto producer.

**Ω_P01: Preference — What language mix best naturalizes code-switching as power signaling?**
- **Resolution:** Indonesian (intimate/family) + Mandarin (negotiation/hierarchy) + English (institutional/AI). Code-switching signals who you're addressing and whether you're performing for the system.
- **Impact:** Characters naturally code-switch based on context. Indonesian for family decisions (parents discussing son's transfer). English for Allocator interaction (work orders, metrics). Mandarin for favor-trading (guanxi negotiation). Switching is invisible to characters, visible to readers as power differential.

**Ω_P02: Preference — How to show body's limits (C1) without stating "this is a Mountain"?**
- **Resolution:** "The cramp" (krampen) as universal experience. All harvesters' hands cramp, backs ache, regardless of score. Shown through physical sensation, painkiller use, watching younger harvesters and knowing their future. Never stated as unchangeable—just felt as unchangeable.
- **Impact:** C1 naturalized through embodied experience. Readers feel it as Mountain without framework language.

**Ω_E03: Empirical — What happens to harvesters who are fired for low scores?**
- **Resolution:** Eviction from housing (can't pay rent), loss of food/water rations (can't buy with credits), eventual death from exposure/starvation in lower arcology levels or outside. The arcology calls this "natural attrition." Harvesters call it "disposal."
- **Impact:** Creates existential stakes for score maintenance. Being marked isn't just social humiliation—it's a death sentence on a 2-4 year timer.

**Ω_C04: Conceptual — Why can't harvesters collectively organize against the Luck Score system?**
- **Resolution:** Three mechanisms: (1) Isolation—low-score harvesters avoided due to "contagion" belief, preventing solidarity. (2) Desperation—harvesters in survival mode, no bandwidth for organizing. (3) Allocator monitoring—AI tracks all communication, flags "productivity disruption" attempts, marks participants.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes why TR1 (collective organization) hasn't occurred despite obvious exploitation. Not because harvesters are passive, but because system design prevents coordination.

**Ω_P03: Preference — How to show parents' Type III error (Snare-as-Rope) without stating "they miss the extraction"?**
- **Resolution:** Through their internal monologue and dialogue. They genuinely see the Luck Score as practical resource allocation. They use framework language ("efficient," "productive," "optimal") without irony. They don't think about the marked harvester's experience—he's just data, a cautionary example, not a person being crushed.
- **Impact:** Readers see the parents as rational actors making reasonable decisions from their position, while simultaneously seeing the extraction they're blind to. Creates dramatic irony without villainizing.

**Ω_E04: Empirical — What is the Allocator's actual institutional structure?**
- **Resolution:** AI system (quantum neural network, distributed across arcology infrastructure). No single human authority. Governed by optimization parameters set during arcology founding (2047). Parameters locked (requires supermajority vote of all arcology residents to change, never achieved). Effectively autonomous.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes institutional power position (π = -0.2). The Allocator isn't a villain—it's an optimization function. It extracts from harvesters not through malice but through parameter design. Makes reform harder (no one to negotiate with, no human decision-maker to persuade).

**Ω_C05: Conceptual — How does the setting naturalize "biographical time horizon" vs "generational time horizon"?**
- **Resolution:** Marked harvester: 53 years old, score falling, 2-4 years until disposal. Thinks in shifts and months (immediate survival). Parents: stable scores, planning son's 40-year career. Think in decades (generational legacy). Same system, different time horizons based on structural position.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes why marked harvester can't see reform possibilities (no time, trapped in immediate survival) while parents can plan strategically (stable position enables long-term thinking).

### UNRESOLVED UNCERTAINTIES

**Ω_P04: Preference — How explicitly should Stage 4 show the Allocator's algorith
