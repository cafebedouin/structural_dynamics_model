# STAGE 6: STRATEGY BRIEF

## IMPLIED AUTHOR

A researcher who notices infrastructure labor—who sees the work that keeps systems running and the people who do it. This author lingers on translation exhaustion (Rafael's temple-rubbing, his "*Hindi ko na kaya*"), documents the small moments when coordination costs become visible (Jun's silence before "*Sino ang magbabayad ng presyo?*"), and tracks how solutions create new burdens. The sensibility is documentary but not detached—attentive to what people pay to make things work.

Register: Technical precision when documenting the three-language system, intimacy when inside character experience, analytical distance only in Sarah's academic voice (where it belongs).

## AUDIENCE

Readers interested in how coordination actually works—not the theory of collective action but the grinding practice of it. Assumes familiarity with workplace dynamics (the exhaustion of being the bridge between systems, the way informal solutions become your job) but not with linguistics or orbital engineering. Expects emotional complexity: solutions that work *and* extract costs, characters who choose to maintain infrastructure *and* resent it.

## SCOPE

**Target: 10,000-11,000 words** (currently 11,800)

The story is structurally a novella—three-act arc (problem → solution → decay) with a large cast and 3+ year timespan—compressed into long-story length. This creates the current pacing problem: Acts 1-2 have room to breathe, Act 3 is rushed. The solution is not expansion but strategic redistribution: compress the glossary creation sequence further (currently ~2,500 words), expand the decay sequence (currently ~1,500 words).

**World-building density:** Current level is correct. The technical vocabulary (*bituin na patay*, *pagkasira ng tinig*) establishes operational reality without over-explaining. The three-language system is shown through use, not exposition.

**Ending resolution:** The story should end with Maya's message or shortly after—not with Sarah's extended reflection. The pattern is visible. Naming it is redundant.

## CREATIVE DEEPENING

**From discovery report:** "The story's power is in showing how solutions create new problems—the glossary works but requires maintenance labor that eventually exhausts the maintainers. This is structurally sophisticated but the ending rushes through the decay, losing the texture of how collective will fractures."

**Available deepening:** The decay sequence (Rafael leaves → Elena tries → glossary fails) currently happens in summary. Expanding this to scene—showing one or two specific moments when maintenance fails (a meeting that doesn't happen, a term that goes undefined, Elena's exhaustion)—would let the reader *feel* the fracture rather than being told about it. The story has earned this emotional weight through 10,000 words of showing how maintenance works. The ending should honor that by showing how it stops working.

**Structural note:** The message-based format for Rafael's Earth-side negotiation (Week 1, Week 3, Week 5...) works because the rhythm conveys institutional grinding. Consider similar treatment for decay: not extended scenes but specific moments that accumulate weight.

## FLINCH POINTS

**1. Jun's resistance (currently ~p.28-30)**

**What it's doing:** Jun articulates the maintenance burden ("*Sino ang magbabayad ng presyo?*") and predicts the glossary will fail. The story validates his prediction—the glossary does fail—but doesn't fully pay the cost of his being right. He stops contributing on day 90, but we don't see what that costs *him*. Does he feel guilty? Vindicated? Exhausted by being the only one willing to name the problem?

**What honesty requires:** One scene or moment showing Jun's interiority after he stops contributing. Not explanation—just his experience of watching the thing he predicted happen.

**2. Rafael's extended contract (currently ~p.35)**

**What it's doing:** Rafael pays for the glossary's recognition with 18 more months of translation labor. The story names this ("*Contract extension. They wanted someone to 'ensure consistency'*") but moves quickly past it. The cost is stated but not felt.

**What honesty requires:** The story doesn't need to show all 18 months, but it should show Rafael *knowing* what he's agreed to. One moment of him calculating the cost and choosing it anyway—not nobly, just because someone has to.

**3. The decay sequence (currently ~p.38-40)**

**What it's doing:** Summarizing. Elena tries, gets busy, stops maintaining. The glossary becomes outdated. This is structurally correct but emotionally thin—the story has spent 10,000 words showing how maintenance works, then tells us in 1,500 words how it stops working.

**What honesty requires:** Not full scenes but specific moments. One meeting that doesn't happen (show the empty room or the message thread where people cancel). One term that goes undefined (show someone looking it up and finding nothing). One moment of Elena realizing she's failed and choosing not to try again. The fracture should be visible, not explained.

## PATTERN CHECK

**Pass with concern.**

The story avoids the pipeline's default failure modes—no villains, no organizational hope that overrides material reality, no quiet rebellion that resolves the constraint. The glossary succeeds *and* fails. The crews choose maintenance *and* stop choosing it. Jun is right *and* his rightness doesn't fix anything.

**Concern:** Sarah's final reflection ("*But knowing didn't stop the pattern. It just made it visible*") tips toward dignifying the failure. The story has shown that documentation matters (Maya's words exist, that's real) *and* that documentation isn't enough (the glossary decays). Sarah's reflection risks converting this complexity into a neat thesis about visibility. The ending should trust the reader to see the pattern without Sarah naming it.
