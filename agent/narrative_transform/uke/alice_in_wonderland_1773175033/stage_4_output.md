# The Lexicon Problem

Dr. Sarah Okonkwo had been on Kepler-7 for eleven days when she heard the term that would change her research trajectory. She was in Ring C's maintenance bay, recording device pressed against her palm, watching Maya Reyes recalibrate a quantum node that had been throwing errors for six hours.

"*Ang bituin na ito ay nag-uumpisa nang maging patay*," Maya said to her pod-mate, Jun. The star is starting to die.

Sarah's Tagalog was functional enough to catch the metaphor. She'd spent three months studying technical vocabulary before arriving, but this was new. She pressed record.

Jun handed Maya a diagnostic scanner. "*Kailan mo huling nakita ang ganito?*" When did you last see this?

"*Noong nakaraang buwan. Pero mas mabilis ngayon.*" Last month. But faster now.

Maya's hands moved with the efficiency of someone who'd done this repair two hundred times. She swapped out the node's cooling assembly, checked the entanglement stability readings, then pulled back and wiped her forehead with her sleeve. The temperature in Ring C fluctuated between eighteen and twenty-six Celsius depending on which side faced the sun. Right now it was climbing toward twenty-four.

"*Bituin na patay*," Maya said finally, pointing at the failed node. "Dead star."

Sarah made a note: *bituin na patay — catastrophic node failure. Etymology: star metaphor. First recorded use: Maya Reyes, 2089-03-15, 14:23 station time.*

Later that day, she watched Rafael Santos file the incident report. He sat in his private cabin in Ring B, door open, typing into the console with the careful precision of someone translating between worlds.

"Node experienced critical malfunction," he wrote. "Cooling system failure led to entanglement destabilization. Replacement required."

Sarah leaned against the doorframe. "Maya called it *bituin na patay*."

Rafael looked up. His face showed the particular exhaustion Sarah had started to recognize in the supervisors — the weariness that came from being a bridge between languages that were drifting apart.

"Dead star," he said in English. "Yeah. That's Maya's term. She invents them."

"But you didn't use it in the report."

"Earth-side doesn't speak Technical Tagalog." He turned back to the console. "They need Institutional English. 'Node malfunction' is the term in the manual."

"But it's not the same thing."

Rafael's fingers paused over the keyboard. "No," he said quietly. "It's not."

---

Sarah had come to Kepler-7 to study orbital language evolution. Her previous research had focused on creole formation in isolated communities — island populations, remote research stations, submarine crews. Kepler-7 was the logical extension: a space station where forty-seven people from three countries had been rotating through eighteen-month contracts for twenty-eight years, developing their own operational vocabulary in the absence of a shared linguistic authority.

What she'd found was more complex than simple creole formation. The crew operated in three distinct languages, each serving a different social function, and the boundaries between them were starting to blur.

Technical Tagalog was the work language. Filipino-based but heavily modified with invented terms for orbital engineering problems that had no Earth-side names. It was the language of maintenance logs, shift handovers, emergency protocols. It changed constantly as crews encountered new malfunctions and coined new terms to describe them.

Institutional English was the administrative language. Formal, frozen, preserved from the station's 2061 founding documents. It was the language of official reports, crew evaluations, communications with Earth-side management. Supervisors spoke it fluently. Technicians learned it in training but rarely used it conversationally.

Bahay-Tagalog — home Tagalog — was the intimacy language. Pure Filipino with minimal code-switching. It was the language of crew quarters, of meals eaten in the cramped galley, of complaints and jokes and the small moments of connection that made eighteen months in orbit bearable.

Sarah had been documenting the lexical innovations in Technical Tagalog, mapping how new terms emerged and spread through the crew. But what she was beginning to realize was that the three languages weren't just coexisting. They were pulling apart.

---

On her fifteenth day, Sarah interviewed Amihan Cruz in the observation module. Amihan was eight months into her first contract, young enough that her face still showed surprise at the Earth's curve through the viewport.

"How's the adjustment?" Sarah asked in English.

Amihan's shoulders tensed slightly. She always tensed when addressed in English, Sarah had noticed. "*Okay naman*," she said. It's okay.

"Can we talk about the technical vocabulary?"

"*Ah.*" Amihan looked down at her hands. "Yes. The technical vocabulary is... challenging."

The switch to English was deliberate, Sarah knew. Amihan had learned that English signaled formality, competence, the performance of professional adequacy. But her Technical Tagalog was weak, and she knew it.

"What's challenging about it?"

"*May mga salita na hindi ko pa alam.*" There are words I don't know yet. Amihan caught herself, switched back to English. "The terms change very fast. Maya and Jun, they use new words every week. I ask Maya to explain, but then next week there are more new words."

"Do you use English when you're working?"

Amihan's face flushed. "*Minsan*. Sometimes. I know it sounds... *ang formal*." Too formal. "But I don't want to use the wrong Technical Tagalog word and sound stupid."

Sarah made a note. The language fracture was creating a competence hierarchy. Those who could keep up with Technical Tagalog innovations — mostly the longer-serving crew — had operational fluency. Those who couldn't — like Amihan — compensated by overusing Institutional English, which made them sound awkward and out of place.

"What do the others think when you use English?"

"*Hindi ko alam*." I don't know. Amihan looked out the viewport. "Maybe they think I'm trying to sound... *importante*. Important. Like I think I'm better than them. But I'm not. I just don't know the right words yet."

---

The mistranslation incident happened on day twenty-three.

Sarah was in the command deck when the alarm sounded. Array Delta was showing signal degradation. Rafael pulled up the diagnostic readout, his face tight with concentration.

Maya's voice came through the comm from Ring C. "*May problema sa delta array. Ang kwantum na bituin ay may —*" She paused, searching for the term. "*Pagkasira ng tinig.*"

Rafael's hand hovered over the response button. Sarah saw him calculating, translating.

"Repeat that," he said in English. "What kind of signal degradation?"

"*Ang bituin ay —*" Maya stopped. Switched to Technical Tagalog. "*Pagkasira ng tinig. Ang signal ay nawawala nang unti-unti.*"

Rafael typed into the console, pulling up the manual. Sarah could see him scanning for the Institutional English equivalent. The seconds stretched out.

"Is it node decay or entanglement drift?" Rafael asked.

Maya's frustration came through the comm clearly. "*Hindi ko alam kung paano sabihin sa English. Tingnan mo lang ang readout.*"

Rafael pulled up the data stream. His face went pale. "How long has it been degrading?"

"*Sampung minuto.*"

Ten minutes. The array had been failing for ten minutes while they argued about terminology.

Rafael slammed his hand on the emergency protocol button. "All crews, array delta offline. Rerouting traffic to gamma and epsilon. Maya, full shutdown procedure, now."

Sarah watched the signal graphs flatline as Maya cut power to the array. The rerouting took six minutes. Six minutes of dead air across sixty percent of Earth's interplanetary data traffic. Six minutes that would generate an incident report, an investigation, possibly contract penalties.

When it was over, Rafael sat back in his chair and pressed his palms against his eyes.

"What happened?" Sarah asked quietly.

"*Pagkasira ng tinig*," Rafael said. "Voice damage. Signal damage. It's a Technical Tagalog term for when the entanglement coherence starts to degrade but the node itself is still functional. It's not in the manual because the manual is from 2061 and we didn't understand entanglement degradation patterns until 2075."

"So there's no Institutional English term for it."

"There's 'signal degradation,' but that's too broad. It could mean anything. Maya was trying to tell me it was *pagkasira ng tinig* specifically, but I didn't —" He stopped. "I didn't recognize the term fast enough."

Sarah made a note: *Translation delay causing operational failure. Gap between Technical Tagalog precision and Institutional English adequacy creating safety risk.*

---

The next morning, Sarah found Maya in the Ring C galley, eating breakfast alone. She looked like she hadn't slept.

"*Pwede ba akong maupo?*" Sarah asked. Can I sit?

Maya gestured to the bench across from her. Her breakfast was rice and dried fish, the same meal Sarah had seen her eat every morning. Comfort food from Earth-side, probably. Something that tasted like home.

"I wanted to ask about yesterday," Sarah said carefully. "*Ang pagkasira ng tinig.*"

Maya's jaw tightened. "*Alam mo ba kung ano ang nangyari pagkatapos?*" Do you know what happened after?

"Rafael filed the incident report."

"*Oo.*" Yes. "*At sinabi niya sa Earth-side na 'node malfunction.' Hindi 'pagkasira ng tinig.' Hindi nila maintindihan kung gaano kaseryoso.*" And he told Earth-side it was a 'node malfunction.' Not 'pagkasira ng tinig.' They don't understand how serious it is.

Sarah pulled out her recording device. "Can I record this?"

Maya looked at the device, then at Sarah. "*Para sa research mo?*"

"Yes. But also because I think it matters."

Maya shrugged. "*Sige.*" Go ahead.

Sarah pressed record. "Tell me about *pagkasira ng tinig*. When did you start using that term?"

"*Mga tatlong buwan na*." About three months ago. "*Nakita ko ang pattern sa readouts. Ang signal ay hindi nawawala nang bigla. Unti-unti. Parang namamatay ang tinig.*" I saw the pattern in the readouts. The signal doesn't disappear suddenly. Gradually. Like a voice dying.

"And you needed a term for it."

"*Oo. Kailangan namin ng salita para sa bagay na iyon.*" Yes. We needed a word for that thing. "*Kaya ginawa ko ang 'pagkasira ng tinig.' Ginamit ko ito kasama sina Jun at ang iba pang technicians. Naintindihan nila.*" So I made 'pagkasira ng tinig.' I used it with Jun and the other technicians. They understood.

"But Rafael didn't."

Maya's hands clenched around her fork. "*Si Rafael ay nasa Ring B. Siya ay supervisor. Hindi niya naririnig ang mga salitang ginagamit namin sa Ring C.*" Rafael is in Ring B. He's a supervisor. He doesn't hear the words we use in Ring C.

"So when you needed to communicate during the emergency—"

"*Wala siyang ideya kung ano ang sinasabi ko.*" He had no idea what I was saying. Maya pushed her plate away. "*Pagod na pagod na ako sa pagsasalin. Bawat shift, kailangan kong mag-isip: Anong salita ang gagamitin ko? Technical Tagalog ba? English ba? Kung gagamitin ko ang maling salita, hindi nila ako maintindihan. Kung gagamitin ko ang English, tutunog akong —*" She stopped, searching for the word. "*Peke. Fake.*"

Sarah made a note: *Translation labor as constant cognitive load. Language choice as identity performance. Gap between operational vocabulary and institutional vocabulary creating communication failure.*

"What would help?" Sarah asked.

Maya looked at her for a long moment. "*Magtanong ka kay Rafael. Siya ang tulay. Siya ang nakakaalam kung paano ayusin ito.*" Ask Rafael. He's the bridge. He's the one who knows how to fix this.

---

Sarah found Rafael in his cabin that afternoon. He was reviewing maintenance logs, his screen filled with Technical Tagalog terms that he was systematically translating into Institutional English for the monthly report.

"Can we talk about the language problem?" Sarah asked.

Rafael looked up. His eyes were tired. "There's no language problem. The system works."

"Yesterday's incident—"

"Was an operational error. I should have recognized the term faster."

"But you couldn't recognize it because you didn't know it. And you didn't know it because Technical Tagalog is changing faster than you can keep up."

Rafael closed his screen. "Dr. Okonkwo. I've been on this station for four years. I've done two contracts. I've translated thousands of maintenance reports, hundreds of shift handovers, dozens of emergency protocols. The system works. Crews communicate. Arrays stay online. The drift is just... linguistic evolution."

Sarah pulled up her data on her tablet. "I've been mapping the lexical innovation rate. Three months ago, Technical Tagalog was adding an average of four new terms per week. Last month it was six. This month it's nine. The drift is accelerating."

Rafael stared at the graph. "So what? Languages change. That's normal."

"But you're the bridge between the languages. You're the one doing the translation work. And the gap is getting wider."

He rubbed his temples. Sarah had seen him do this before, usually when he'd spent too long translating between Technical Tagalog and Institutional English. The gesture of someone carrying too much weight in their head.

"What do you want me to say?" Rafael asked quietly. "That I'm losing words? That there are Technical Tagalog terms I knew six months ago and now I can't remember the English? That every month it takes me longer to find the right institutional term for a new technical concept?"

"Is that what's happening?"

Rafael looked at her. "Yes," he said. "That's what's happening."

Sarah made a note: *Bridge exhaustion. Translation labor unsustainable. Coordinator awareness of system degradation but no agency to fix it unilaterally.*

"Have you told Earth-side management?"

Rafael laughed, but there was no humor in it. "What would I tell them? That the crews are inventing too many words? That Technical Tagalog is evolving too fast? They'd say: enforce Institutional English as the operational language. Make everyone use the manual terms." He shook his head. "But we can't. The manual is from 2061. It doesn't have terms for half the things we deal with up here. Technical Tagalog exists because we need it."

"So you're trapped."

"We're all trapped," Rafael said. "The crews need Technical Tagalog to do their work. I need Institutional English to communicate with Earth-side. And the gap between them keeps getting wider, and I'm the only one trying to hold them together, and I can't do it anymore."

He said the last part in Filipino. *Hindi ko na kaya.*

---

Sarah spent the next two weeks interviewing crew members across all three shifts. She recorded conversations in the galley, in the maintenance bays, in the observation module. She documented code-switching patterns, lexical innovations, mistranslation incidents. She mapped the semantic drift.

What emerged was a picture of a system under increasing strain.

The older crew members — those on their second or third contracts — had built fluency in Technical Tagalog over years. They could navigate the constant lexical change because they'd been present for the evolution. They knew why *bituin na patay* meant catastrophic failure because they'd been there when Maya coined the term. They knew why *pagkasira ng tinig* was different from simple signal degradation because they'd seen the pattern in the readouts.

The newer crew members — like Amihan — were learning Technical Tagalog as a foreign language, but the language kept changing before they could master it. They overcompensated by using Institutional English, which made them sound formal and awkward and created social distance from their pod-mates.

The supervisors — like Rafael — were exhausted from constant translation labor. They spent hours every week converting Technical Tagalog into Institutional English, knowing that each translation lost precision, knowing that the gap was widening, knowing that eventually someone would get hurt because of a mistranslation.

And Earth-side management, reading the incident reports in Institutional English, had no idea how serious the communication problem was becoming. To them, "node malfunction" and "signal degradation" were adequate terms. They didn't know about *bituin na patay* or *pagkasira ng tinig*. They didn't know that the crews were inventing new vocabulary every week because the manual terms were obsolete.

Sarah compiled her findings into a presentation. She titled it "The Lexicon Problem: Semantic Drift and Communication Failure on Kepler-7."

---

She gave the presentation in the command deck on day forty-two. The entire gamma shift attended — Maya, Jun, Amihan, and eight other technicians from Ring C. Rafael came, along with two other shift supervisors. Even Commander Chen showed up, though she sat in the back and didn't say anything.

Sarah pulled up her first slide: a graph showing lexical innovation rate over the past three years.

"Technical Tagalog is changing faster than it ever has before," she said. "Three years ago, crews were adding about two new terms per week. Now it's nine. The drift is accelerating."

Maya leaned forward in her seat. Jun whispered something to her in Bahay-Tagalog.

Sarah pulled up the next slide: a timeline of mistranslation incidents over the past year.

"Six months ago, there was one mistranslation incident every three months. Now there's one every two weeks. The gap between Technical Tagalog and Institutional English is creating operational failures."

Rafael's face was carefully neutral, but his hands were clenched in his lap.

Sarah pulled up the final slide: a comparison with the previous rotation cycle's communication data.

"This is the interesting part," she said. "I requested archived maintenance logs from the 2086-2087 rotation. That crew had a lower mistranslation incident rate than you do. Their lexical innovation rate was also lower — about four new terms per week instead of nine."

She zoomed in on a note in the archived files. "They had an informal glossary. A shared document where they recorded new Technical Tagalog terms along with their Institutional English equivalents. It was maintained by a technician named Luis Mendoza. He updated it every two weeks."

Maya sat up. "*Bakit hindi nila sinabi sa atin?*" Why didn't they tell us?

"The glossary wasn't official," Sarah said. "It wasn't part of the station protocols. When that crew rotated out, the document stayed in the archives. Your crew didn't know it existed."

Rafael was staring at the screen. "So the drift is changeable."

"Yes," Sarah said. "The drift is changeable."

---

The meeting ran for two hours. Crew members argued in three languages, switching between Technical Tagalog and Bahay-Tagalog and Institutional English depending on who they were addressing and what they were trying to say.

Maya wanted a comprehensive glossary. Something that would reduce the translation labor, make it easier to communicate with supervisors, legitimize the Technical Tagalog terms that crews were already using.

Rafael wanted Earth-side management buy-in. He was tired of translating terms that didn't translate well. He wanted permission to use Technical Tagalog in official reports, or at least to have the glossary recognized as a valid reference document.

Amihan wanted learning support. She was tired of feeling incompetent, tired of asking Maya for translations, tired of sounding awkward when she tried to use Technical Tagalog terms she didn't fully understand.

Commander Chen, speaking for the first time, said: "Earth-side won't approve this. They'll say it's not in the protocols."

"Then we do it anyway," Maya said. "*Ginawa ito ni Luis. Kaya natin din.*" Luis did it. We can too.

Rafael shook his head. "If we create an unofficial glossary and Earth-side finds out, they'll say we're not following procedures. We could lose contract bonuses."

"We're already losing them," Jun said. "Every mistranslation incident costs us bonus points. The array went offline for six minutes last month. That cost us three months of bonuses."

The argument circled for another hour. Sarah sat in the back and took notes, watching the crew negotiate the terms of their own coordination.

Finally, Rafael said: "What if we pilot it? Run it for three months. See if the mistranslation rate goes down. If it works, I'll take the data to Earth-side and make the case for official recognition."

Maya looked at Amihan, then at Jun. "*Okay*," she said. "Three months."

---

Sarah spent the next two weeks documenting Technical Tagalog terms. She recorded Maya and Jun during their shifts, noting every innovation, every coinage, every moment when they invented a new word for a problem that had no existing name. She interviewed technicians across all three shifts, asking them to explain terms in their own words, mapping the semantic networks that made Technical Tagalog coherent to those who spoke it.

She compiled everything into a shared document: fifty terms with Technical Tagalog definitions and Institutional English translations. She included etymology notes, usage examples, and the names of the crew members who'd coined each term.

*Bituin na patay* (dead star): Catastrophic quantum node failure. Characterized by complete loss of entanglement coherence. Coined by Maya Reyes, 2089-03-15.

*Pagkasira ng tinig* (voice damage): Progressive signal degradation. Entanglement coherence decreases gradually over time. Requires node replacement before complete failure. Coined by Maya Reyes, 2089-02-20.

*Hanging na bituin* (hanging star): Quantum node in unstable state. Signal strength fluctuates but doesn't fail completely. Can be stabilized through recalibration. Coined by Jun Santos, 2088-11-03.

She showed the glossary to Maya first. They sat in the Ring C galley, Sarah's tablet between them.

Maya scrolled through the entries slowly. Her finger paused on *bituin na patay*.

"*Ginawa mo ito*," she said quietly. You made this.

"You made it," Sarah said. "I just wrote it down."

Maya looked up. Her eyes were wet. "*Alam mo, minsan naiisip ko na walang silbi ang mga salitang ginagawa ko. Na naglalaho lang sila sa hangin.*" You know, sometimes I think the words I make are useless. That they just disappear into the air.

"They don't disappear," Sarah said. "They're real. They matter."

Maya pressed her palm against the screen, over the entry for *bituin na patay*. "*Salamat*," she said.

---

The glossary went live on day sixty-eight. Sarah posted it to the station's internal network, accessible to all crew members. Rafael added a note at the top: "Pilot program. Three-month evaluation period. Report errors or suggested additions to Dr. Okonkwo."

The first week, Sarah received fifteen suggested additions. Technicians from all three shifts submitted terms they'd been using but hadn't seen documented. *Nag-aalab na bituin* (burning star) for an overheating node. *Tahimik na pagkasira* (quiet damage) for gradual system degradation that didn't trigger alarms. *Pagbagsak ng hanay* (array collapse) for multiple simultaneous failures.

Sarah added them all. The glossary grew to seventy terms, then ninety, then one hundred and twelve.

Amihan started using it as a learning tool. Sarah saw her in the observation module one afternoon, reading through entries, mouthing the Technical Tagalog terms to herself. When Sarah asked how it was helping, Amihan said: "*Hindi na ako nag-iisa sa pag-aaral*." I'm not learning alone anymore.

Rafael started referencing the glossary in his incident reports. Instead of translating *pagkasira ng tinig* as "signal degradation," he wrote: "Signal degradation (Technical Tagalog: *pagkasira ng tinig*; see station glossary for definition)." Earth-side management didn't object. Possibly they didn't notice. Possibly they didn't care as long as the reports were filed on time.

The mistranslation incident rate dropped. Week one: three incidents. Week two: two incidents. Week three: one incident. Week four: zero incidents.

On day ninety-four, Rafael called a meeting.

---

The command deck was crowded. All three shift supervisors attended, along with a representative sample of technicians from each shift. Commander Chen sat in the front row this time.

Rafael pulled up a comparison graph: mistranslation incidents before and after the glossary implementation.

"The data is clear," he said. "The glossary works. Mistranslation incidents are down seventy-three percent. Translation time for incident reports is down forty percent. Crew satisfaction surveys show increased confidence in technical communication."

He looked at Commander Chen. "I want to submit this to Earth-side for official recognition. Make the glossary part of station protocols. Legitimize Technical Tagalog as a valid operational language."

Commander Chen was quiet for a long moment. Then she said: "Earth-side will want to control it. They'll want to standardize the terms. Lock them down. Stop the drift completely."

"That won't work," Maya said. "*Ang drift ay hindi mapipigilan. Kailangan lang natin ng paraan para ma-track ito.*" The drift can't be stopped. We just need a way to track it.

"So we propose a living document," Sarah said. "Updated monthly. New terms added as they emerge. Old terms marked as deprecated if they fall out of use. The glossary evolves with the language."

"Who maintains it?" Commander Chen asked.

Sarah looked at Maya. "The crews. It's their language."

Rafael nodded. "I'll coordinate with Earth-side. Make sure the translations are acceptable for official reports. But the Technical Tagalog side belongs to the crews."

Commander Chen looked at the graph again. "Three months," she said. "We'll run it for three more months. If the mistranslation rate stays down, I'll submit it to Earth-side with a recommendation for adoption."

---

Sarah's contract ended on day one hundred and eighty. She packed her research materials, backed up her recordings, and prepared to transfer Earth-side.

On her last day, Maya found her in the observation module.

"*Aalis ka na*," Maya said. You're leaving.

"Yes."

They stood together at the viewport, watching the Earth turn below them. Sarah had spent six months on Kepler-7, but she still felt like a visitor. She spoke Technical Tagalog adequately now, could follow Bahay-Tagalog conversations without her recording device, but she knew she'd always be slightly outside the language. Always translating in her head.

"*Salamat sa ginawa mo*," Maya said. Thank you for what you did.

"I just documented what was already there."

Maya shook her head. "*Hindi. Binigyan mo ng pangalan ang problema. Pinapakita mo sa amin na kaya nating ayusin ito.*" No. You named the problem. You showed us we could fix it.

Sarah thought about the glossary, now up to one hundred and forty-seven terms. Thought about the monthly update meetings where crews argued about definitions, negotiated translations, decided collectively which terms deserved documentation. Thought about how her research had stopped being observation and become intervention.

"The drift will continue," she said. "The glossary just makes it manageable."

"*Okay lang*," Maya said. It's okay. "*Hindi namin kailangan ng perpekto. Kailangan lang namin ng sapat.*" We don't need perfect. We just need enough.

They stood in silence for a while. Then Maya said: "*Ang research mo — ano ang isusulat mo?*" Your research — what will you write?

Sarah had been thinking about this for weeks. Her original research question had been about creole formation in isolated populations. But what she'd found on Kepler-7 was something different. Not a single creole emerging from language contact, but three languages coexisting in deliberate separation, each serving a distinct social function, the boundaries between them carefully maintained even as they drifted apart.

"I'll write about the glossary," Sarah said. "About collective semantic stabilization. About how crews negotiate coordination when the language keeps changing."

"*Maiintindihan ba nila?*" Will they understand?

"I don't know," Sarah said honestly. "I'll try to make them understand."

Maya smiled. "*Ganyan din ang ginagawa natin araw-araw*." That's what we do every day too.

---

Sarah's paper was published six months later in the *Journal of Linguistic Anthropology*. She titled it "Negotiated Stability: Semantic Drift and Collective Coordination in Orbital Technical Creoles."

The abstract read: "This study examines language evolution in Kepler-7 orbital station, where a three-language system enables operational coordination despite accelerating semantic drift. Data suggests that collective documentation protocols can stabilize drift without eliminating innovation, creating a negotiated equilibrium between linguistic change and communicative function."

The paper was cited forty-three times in the first year. Two other orbital stations implemented similar glossary systems. Earth-side management, reading the incident rate data, quietly updated the protocols to include "station-specific technical vocabulary documentation" as an acceptable practice.

Sarah sometimes wondered if the crews on Kepler-7 knew their glossary had become infrastructure. If they knew that what had started as a pilot program was now standard practice across three orbital stations. If they knew that their negotiated equilibrium — imperfect, fragile, requiring constant maintenance — had become a model for coordination in other isolated technical communities.

She thought about Maya, probably still inventing terms, still documenting them, still teaching new crew members the language that kept the arrays online. She thought about Rafael, probably still translating, but with the glossary as a shared reference now, the burden distributed across the crew instead of falling on him alone. She thought about Amihan, probably fluent in Technical Tagalog by now, probably teaching the next generation of new arrivals.

She thought about the recording device in her palm, the hundreds of hours of conversation it held. The voices of people living inside a constraint, finding ways to coordinate despite it, negotiating their own stability.

The drift was still happening. The glossary was still being updated monthly. The system was still fragile.

But it was working.

That was enough.
