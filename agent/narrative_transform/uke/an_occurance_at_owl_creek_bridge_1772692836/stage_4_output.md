# The Weight You Carry

The drill bit screams against stone and my shoulders lock. Twelve hours down here and the lithium veins glow like diseased teeth in the rock face. I lean into the machine's kick, let the vibration travel up my arms into my chest where it sits next to everything else I carry.

"Miguel, your cart's full." 

Dante's voice behind me. I kill the drill and the silence rushes in, thick with dust and the smell of overheated metal. My hands won't stop shaking. They never do anymore, even in sleep. Rosa mentioned it last week on the video call—said I was twitching, asked if I was okay. I told her I was fine. The house looks good, I said. The walls are really coming together.

"You good?" Dante asks.

"Yeah. Just thinking about home."

He helps me haul the cart to the sorting station. We don't talk much down here—the air's too thin for conversation, even in the Valles Depression where we can work without suits. Every word costs breath you need for the next lift, the next drill, the next twelve hours.

At the station, I watch the lithium chunks tumble onto the conveyor. Each one is money. Each one is my brother's tuition, my mother's medicine, the tin roof that doesn't leak when the monsoons come. I chose this. That's what I tell myself when my lungs burn and my hands shake and the rock dust coats my throat. I chose this.

"Shift change in twenty," Dante says. "You want to grab food?"

"Sure."

We ride the lift up together, our boots leaving dust prints on the metal floor. The hab-modules come into view—concentric rings of prefab boxes stacked three high. Ours are in the outer ring, 7-G-33, where the walls are thin enough to hear Dante's breathing through the partition at night. The middle ring looks better from here—larger modules, actual windows. The central core gleams in the distance, climate-controlled and carpeted. I've never been inside. No reason to go.

In the mess hall, we eat rice and reconstituted vegetables that taste like the packet they came in. Dante pushes his food around.

"You hear about the new quotas?" he asks.

"No. What about them?"

"Up fifteen percent next quarter. Same wages."

I keep eating. The rice is gummy but I'm too tired to care. "We'll make it work."

"That's what you always say."

"Because it's true. We're almost done, Dante. Eight more months and we're out. You'll go home, build that store you keep talking about. I'll finish the house, get Rosa pregnant finally." I smile at that, try to make him smile too. "We just have to finish."

He doesn't smile. "You ever think about what happens if we don't?"

"Don't what?"

"Finish. What if something happens? What if we get hurt, or sick, or—"

"Then we get medical care. It's in the contract."

"Miguel, the contract says a lot of things."

I put down my spoon. "What are you saying?"

"Nothing. Forget it." He stands, dumps his tray. "I'm going to sleep. See you at shift."

I watch him go. Dante's been weird lately—asking questions, talking to other workers during breaks instead of resting. I don't like it. Questions don't help. Questions don't build houses or pay tuition or buy medicine. Work does. Finishing does.

Back in the hab, I strip off my work clothes and stand in the communal shower. The water is rationed—three minutes, lukewarm. I watch the rock dust swirl down the drain and think about the shower in the new house. Rosa sent pictures last week. Real tile, she said. Blue tile, like the ocean. I've never seen the ocean but I imagine it's cleaner than this.

In my bunk, I pull up the video messages on my tablet. Rosa's face fills the screen, pixelated but smiling.

"Miguel, *mahal*, the walls are done! Look—" The camera swings around and I see concrete, solid and real, with electrical conduits already installed. "Your brother helped with the wiring. He's so good at this, Miguel. The engineering program is perfect for him. He says thank you, by the way. He says he'll make you proud."

I will make him proud. I am making him proud. Every shift, every cart of lithium, every time I don't ask questions—that's me making him proud.

"I miss you," Rosa says. Her face is back on screen. "Eight more months feels like forever. But I know you're working hard. I know you're doing this for us. *Mahal kita*. I love you."

I love you too, I mouth at the screen. The message is three days old—that's how long signals take from here to Earth. By the time she sees my response, I'll already be in the shaft for another shift.

I record my reply. "Rosa, the house looks amazing. You and Mama did such a good job with the walls. Tell my brother I'm proud of him. Tell him to study hard, finish the program. Eight more months and I'll be home. We'll have that baby you keep talking about. We'll fill that house with noise and life and everything good. I love you. *Mahal kita*."

I send it and lie back on my bunk. Through the thin wall, I hear Dante's breathing, slow and even. He's already asleep. My hands are still shaking but I fold them on my chest and close my eyes.

Tomorrow is another shift. Another twelve hours in the shaft. Another cart of lithium that becomes another brick in the wall, another month of tuition, another dose of medicine. The weight in my chest—the exhaustion, the debt, the *utang na loob*—it's heavy, but it's also purpose. I can carry it. I have to carry it. That's what you do for family.

---

Three weeks later, I'm in the deep shaft when the drill hits something wrong. The bit catches and the machine bucks hard enough to throw me backward. I land on my tailbone and the pain shoots up my spine.

"*Putang ina*," I hiss.

Dante helps me up. "You okay?"

"Yeah. Just surprised me." I check the drill—the bit is bent, useless. "I need to swap this out."

"I'll finish the vein. You go to equipment."

I limp to the equipment station, my back throbbing with each step. The supervisor on duty is a woman I've seen before but never spoken to. She's middle-aged, Filipino, with a badge that says REYES, L. - OPERATIONS SUPERVISOR.

"Drill bit bent," I tell her in English. "I need a replacement."

She looks at the mangled bit, then at me. "*Ano'ng nangyari?*" she asks. What happened?

The switch to Filipino surprises me. Supervisors usually stick to English—it's protocol, keeps everything official. But her tone is casual, almost concerned.

"Hit something in the rock. Kicked back hard."

"You hurt?"

"Just my back. I'll be fine."

She pulls a new bit from storage and hands it over, but she doesn't let go immediately when I reach for it. Her eyes are on mine, searching.

"You're Miguel Santos, right? Hab 7-G-33?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"How long have you been here?"

"Almost thirty-two months. Eight more to go."

She nods slowly. "That's a long time in the shaft. You must be tired."

"I'm fine."

"I'm sure you are. But *alam mo*, Miguel, I've been here eleven years. I started in the outer ring, just like you. Same bunk setup, same thin walls, same twelve-hour shifts. I know what it's like."

Something in her voice makes me pause. She's still holding the drill bit, and I'm still reaching for it, and the moment stretches between us.

"You made supervisor," I say carefully. "You finished your term."

"I did. And I want you to finish yours too. That's why I'm talking to you." She releases the bit and I take it. "There are people here, Miguel, who care about workers like us. People who remember what it's like in the outer ring. People who want things to be better."

My back throbs. I want to get back to the shaft, back to work, back to not thinking about anything except the next cart, the next lift.

"I appreciate that, ma'am, but I just need to finish my shift."

"Of course. But if you ever want to talk—about conditions, about the quotas, about anything—you can find me. I'm in the middle ring now, Module 4-C-17. We could have coffee. Just talk."

"Thank you, ma'am."

I head back to the shaft, the new drill bit heavy in my hand. Dante's already halfway through the vein when I return.

"Took you long enough," he says.

"Supervisor wanted to talk."

"About what?"

"Nothing. Just checking if I was okay."

I install the new bit and fire up the drill. The vibration travels up my arms, into my chest, and I lean into it. The lithium glows in the rock face and I think about eight more months, about the house with blue tile, about Rosa's face on the screen saying *mahal kita*.

I don't think about Supervisor Reyes or her invitation to talk. There's nothing to talk about. The contract is clear. The work is clear. Eight more months and I'm done.

That's all that matters.

---

But two days later, Dante brings it up.

We're in the mess hall, eating the same rice and reconstituted vegetables. He leans across the table, voice low.

"Miguel, you know that supervisor you talked to? Reyes?"

"Yeah. What about her?"

"She talked to me too. Yesterday, at equipment check."

My stomach tightens. "What did she say?"

"Same thing, probably. That she used to be in the outer ring. That she knows what it's like. That there are people who want things to be better." He pauses. "She said we should talk. Her, me, you, maybe some others."

"Dante, no."

"Why not? What's wrong with talking?"

"Because talking doesn't help. Working helps. Finishing helps."

"The quotas went up again, Miguel. Did you hear? Another ten percent. They're squeezing us and we're just supposed to take it?"

"We signed the contract. We knew the terms."

"The terms keep changing!"

I glance around. The mess hall is half-full, workers hunched over their trays, too tired to pay attention to us. But you never know who's listening.

"Keep your voice down."

"I'm just saying we should listen to what she has to say. Maybe there's a way to make things better without breaking contract. Better conditions, shorter shifts, something."

"There's no 'better,' Dante. There's finishing or not finishing. That's it."

He stares at me. "You're scared."

"I'm smart. There's a difference."

"No, you're scared. You're scared that if you even think about anything except finishing, the whole thing will fall apart. Your brother's tuition, the house, all of it. So you just put your head down and drill and pretend that's the only option."

My hands are shaking. Not from the drill—from anger, from fear, from something I can't name.

"My family is counting on me, Dante. My brother is in school because of me. My mother has medicine because of me. Rosa is building a house because of me. If I don't finish, they lose everything. You understand that? Everything."

"And what do you lose if you do finish? You ever think about that?"

I stand up, my chair scraping loud against the floor. "I'm done eating."

"Miguel—"

"I'll see you at shift."

I leave before he can say anything else. My hands are shaking so hard I have to shove them in my pockets. In the corridor back to the hab, I pass a supervisor—not Reyes, someone else—and I nod, say "Sir" with the right amount of respect, keep walking.

In my bunk, I pull up Rosa's latest message. She's standing in front of the house, the blue tile visible through the bathroom doorway behind her.

"Miguel, it's almost done. Can you believe it? The roof is on, the plumbing works, the electrical is all connected. Your brother did such a good job. We're going to move in next month, even before you get home. I can't wait for you to see it. I can't wait for you to be here."

I replay it three times. The house is real. The tile is real. Rosa's smile is real. That's what matters. Not Dante's questions, not Supervisor Reyes's invitations to talk, not the quotas or the shifts or the vibration in my hands.

Eight more months.

I can do eight more months.

---

But Supervisor Reyes finds me again.

It's during a water break, topside, where the thin atmosphere makes everything feel fragile. She appears next to me at the dispenser, filling her own canteen.

"Miguel," she says in Filipino. "You've been avoiding me."

"No, ma'am. Just working."

"*Alam ko*. I know. But I also know you're thinking about what I said. About talking."

"With respect, ma'am, I'm not interested."

She caps her canteen slowly. "Your friend Dante is."

My chest tightens. "What?"

"He came to see me. We talked. He's smart, Miguel. He sees what's happening here. He sees that the system isn't fair, that the quotas are designed to extract more work for the same pay, that the contract terms keep shifting in TPMC's favor." She pauses. "He gave me names. Other workers who feel the same way. Who want things to change."

The words hit me like the drill bit catching wrong. Dante gave names. Dante went to see her. Dante is organizing.

"He shouldn't have done that."

"Why not? Because you're scared? Because you think keeping your head down is the only way to survive?" Her voice is gentle, almost sad. "Miguel, I was like you once. I thought if I just worked hard enough, if I just finished my term, everything would be fine. And you know what? I did finish. But I finished because I learned to navigate the system, not just endure it. That's what I'm trying to teach you."

"I don't want to learn anything. I just want to finish."

"And you will. But you could finish with something more than just your contract fulfilled. You could finish with conditions improved for everyone who comes after you. Isn't that worth something?"

I think about Rosa, about the house, about my brother's tuition. I think about the weight in my chest and how it's the only thing keeping me upright.

"I need to get back to my shift."

"Think about it, Miguel. That's all I'm asking. Just think about it. And if you change your mind, you know where to find me."

She walks away, and I stand there with my canteen in my hand, my back throbbing, my hands shaking.

Dante is organizing. Dante gave names.

I should talk to him. I should tell him to stop, to be careful, to think about what he's risking. But when I get back to the shaft, he's already deep in the vein, and the drill is too loud for conversation.

That night, I lie in my bunk and listen to him breathe through the wall. I want to knock, to say something, but I don't know what to say. Be careful? Stop? Don't trust her?

I don't know who to trust anymore.

---

Four days later, I break.

The quotas go up again—another twelve percent—and during the shift, I watch Dante's shoulders slump as he reads the notice posted at the equipment station. He doesn't say anything. Just picks up his drill and heads down.

At the end of the shift, I find myself walking toward Module 4-C-17.

The middle ring is different. The corridors are wider, the air smells less like sweat and metal. The modules have actual doors, not just curtains. I stand outside 4-C-17 for a full minute before I knock.

Supervisor Reyes opens the door. She's not in uniform—just regular clothes, a shirt and pants that look comfortable. Behind her, I can see a small living space with a real bed, a table, a window that shows the Valles Depression stretching to the horizon.

"Miguel," she says, and her smile is warm. "I'm glad you came. Come in."

I step inside. The floor is solid, not the thin metal grating of the outer ring. There's a chair at the table and she gestures for me to sit.

"Coffee?" she asks in Filipino.

"Yes. Thank you."

She pours from a real coffee maker—not the instant packets we get in the outer ring—and hands me a mug. The ceramic is warm against my palms.

"I know this is hard for you," she says, sitting across from me. "Coming here. Talking to me. It goes against everything you've told yourself about finishing your term."

"I just want to understand what Dante is doing. What you're doing."

"We're trying to build something, Miguel. A network of workers who can advocate for better conditions. Not a union—that's prohibited by contract—but a group that can present concerns to management collectively. There's power in numbers. Right now, TPMC can change quotas whenever they want because workers are isolated. But if we had enough people, if we could coordinate, we could push back. We could negotiate."

"The contract doesn't allow negotiation."

"The contract doesn't allow strikes. But it doesn't prohibit workers from making suggestions, from presenting concerns through proper channels. That's all we're doing. Working within the system to make it better."

I sip the coffee. It's real, not reconstituted. It tastes like something I remember from before I came here.

"How many people have joined?"

"Enough to make a difference. Dante's been helpful—he's connected us with workers in other sectors, people who feel the same way. We're building something real, Miguel. Something that could actually change things."

"And you're doing this because you care about workers?"

"I'm doing this because I remember what it's like. I remember the outer ring, the twelve-hour shifts, the thin walls. I remember feeling like I had no power, no voice. And then I finished my term and I moved up and I realized—there are ways to work within the system. There are ways to make things better without breaking contract." She leans forward. "I want to help you, Miguel. I want to help all of you. But I need to know who's with us. Who can we trust?"

The question hangs in the air. The coffee is warm in my hands. Through the window, I can see the Valles Depression, the extraction shaft in the distance, the outer ring where Dante is probably sleeping already, his breathing steady through the thin wall.

"Dante gave you names," I say.

"He did. And those workers have been reassigned to different shifts, different sectors, so we can build the network more efficiently. We can't have everyone in the same place—that would be too obvious. But spread out, coordinating through trusted people like Dante, like you if you join us—that's how we build power."

"What do you need from me?"

"Names, Miguel. Other workers who are frustrated, who want things to change. People you trust. People who would join us if they knew it was safe."

I think about the workers in my sector. Carlos, who's been here four years and still has two to go. Jin, who sends every peso home to his parents in Sichuan. Amara, who talks about her daughter's school fees.

I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about my brother in his engineering program. I think about the weight in my chest and how it's been getting heavier, how the eight months feel longer every day.

"I need to think about it," I say.

"Of course. Take your time. But Miguel—" She reaches across the table and touches my hand. Her fingers are warm. "I know you're scared. I know you think this could jeopardize everything you've worked for. But I promise you, this is safe. This is smart. This is how you finish your term with your dignity intact."

I nod. I finish the coffee. I stand up.

"Thank you for the coffee."

"Anytime. You know where to find me."

I walk back to the outer ring with my hands in my pockets. The corridors feel narrower than before. The air smells worse. In my hab, Dante is already asleep, his breathing steady through the wall.

I lie in my bunk and stare at the ceiling. Names. She wants names.

Carlos. Jin. Amara.

Workers who are frustrated. Workers who want things to change.

Workers who trust me.

---

I give her the names three days later.

I tell myself it's the right thing. That Supervisor Reyes is building something real, something that could help everyone. That the reassignments are strategic, not punitive. That this is how you work within the system to make it better.

I give her Carlos's name. Jin's name. Amara's name.

I tell her they're frustrated with the quotas, that they've talked about wanting better conditions, that they would join if they knew it was safe.

She writes everything down. She thanks me. She says I'm doing the right thing, that I'm helping build something important.

She says I've demonstrated leadership potential.

---

The reassignments happen within a week.

Carlos moves to a different sector—early shift instead of late. Jin gets transferred to surface operations. Amara is sent to a different hab-module in the outer ring, far enough away that I never see her anymore.

None of them say goodbye.

At shift change, I see Carlos in the corridor. I raise my hand to wave, to say something, but he looks right through me and keeps walking.

The next day, I find a notice in my hab. I've been reassigned to the middle ring, Module 4-C-22. Supervisor trainee position. Immediate transfer.

I pack my things—the tablet with Rosa's messages, my work clothes, the few personal items I've accumulated. Through the wall, I can hear Dante's breathing. I want to knock, to explain, to say something. But I don't know what to say.

I don't know how to explain what I've done.

In Module 4-C-22, I have a real bed. A solid floor. A window that shows the Valles Depression. The air doesn't smell like sweat and metal.

I have a badge now: SANTOS, M. - SUPERVISOR TRAINEE.

I wear it during training sessions, during equipment checks, during the times I'm supposed to monitor workers from the outer ring. Workers like I was two weeks ago. Workers who don't look at me the same way anymore.

Dante doesn't talk to me. When I see him in the corridors, he turns away. When I try to approach him in the mess hall, he leaves. The breathing through the wall is gone—I'm in a different ring now, with solid walls and silence.

I send messages to Rosa but I don't tell her about the promotion. I don't tell her about the names I gave, about Carlos and Jin and Amara, about the way Dante won't look at me anymore.

I tell her the house looks beautiful. I tell her I'm proud of my brother. I tell her seven more months and I'll be home.

I don't tell her that the weight in my chest has changed. That it's not purpose anymore—it's something else. Something that sits there like a stone, cold and heavy and wrong.

At night, I lie in my real bed with my solid floor and my window view, and I think about the coffee in Supervisor Reyes's module. The way she touched my hand. The way she said *trust*.

I think about the names I gave her.

I think about Dante's breathing through the thin wall, steady and even, and how I'll never hear it again.

---

Six weeks after the promotion, I'm called to the central core.

I've never been inside before. The corridors are carpeted—actual carpet, soft under my boots. The air is cool, climate-controlled. There are plants in pots, real plants with green leaves. Everything smells clean.

A security officer escorts me to a conference room. The door is solid wood, not metal. Inside, there's a long table with chairs that look comfortable. At the head of the table sits a man in a clean uniform, his hands folded on a tablet.

"Miguel Santos?" he asks in English.

"Yes, sir."

"I'm Captain Torres, Contract Compliance Division. Please sit."

I sit. The chair is comfortable. My boots leave dust prints on the carpet and I try to tuck them under the chair, out of sight.

Captain Torres looks at his tablet. "You've been with TPMC for thirty-three months. Seven months remaining on your contract."

"Yes, sir."

"And you were recently promoted to supervisor trainee after demonstrating leadership initiative."

"Yes, sir."

He looks up at me. His eyes are gray, distant. "Mr. Santos, we've been monitoring communications in your sector. We have recordings of conversations between you and former workers—Carlos Mendez, Jin Wei, Amara Hassan. Conversations in which you discussed organizing activities, labor coordination, and potential contract violations."

My stomach drops. My hands go cold.

"Sir, I—I was reporting those conversations to Supervisor Reyes. She told me to identify workers who were organizing. I was helping the company."

"Supervisor Reyes," he says slowly, "is a contract compliance intelligence officer. Her job is to identify and document organizing attempts. Your job, as a worker, was to complete your term without engaging in prohibited activities. By participating in those conversations, by soliciting names, by coordinating with other workers, you violated Section 7.3 of your contract: Prohibited Organizing Activity."

"But she told me—she said I was helping—"

"What Supervisor Reyes told you is irrelevant. The contract is clear. The violation is documented." He taps his tablet. "The tribunal has reviewed your case. The hearing will begin now."

"Hearing? Sir, I don't understand—"

Two more people enter the room. Another security officer. A woman with a supervisor badge who won't meet my eyes. They sit at the table.

Captain Torres reads from his tablet. "Miguel Santos, you are charged with willful breach of contract terms under Section 7.3: Prohibited Organizing Activity. The evidence includes recorded conversations, documented meetings with a compliance officer, and testimony from multiple sources. You have the right to speak on your own behalf. Do you have anything to say?"

My mouth is dry. My hands are shaking. "I thought I was helping. Supervisor Reyes said—"

"The contract waives claims of misunderstanding or coercion. The activities are documented. Do you deny participating in organizing discussions?"

"No, but—"

"Do you deny meeting with Supervisor Reyes to discuss worker coordination?"

"I met with her, yes, but she told me it was safe, she said—"

"Do you deny providing names of other workers for organizing purposes?"

The names. Carlos. Jin. Amara. Dante.

"I gave names, yes."

"Then the facts are established." Captain Torres looks at the other two. They nod. He looks back at me. "The tribunal finds you guilty of willful breach of contract terms, Section 7.3. The sentence is immediate: termination without wages, return transport to Earth on the next supply ship, family liable for signing bonus repayment plus interest. Additionally, you will undergo 72 hours of processing before departure, as outlined in standard debriefing protocols."

The words hit me but I can't process them. Termination. No wages. Family liable. Seventy-two hours.

"Sir, please—my family—the house—my brother's in school—"

"Your family will be contacted regarding repayment terms. The signing bonus plus accumulated interest comes to approximately forty-three thousand dollars. They will have sixty days to begin repayment or the house will be seized as collateral."

"Please, sir, I didn't know—I thought I was helping—"

"The hearing is concluded. Security will escort you to processing."

The security officer stands. His hand is on my arm, pulling me up. The chair is comfortable but I'm not sitting in it anymore. My boots leave dust prints on the carpet as he leads me out.

In the corridor, I try to speak. "I need to call my wife. I need to explain—"

"No communications during processing," the officer says.

"Please, just let me send one message—"

"No communications."

We walk through the carpeted corridors, past the plants in pots, past the clean air and the solid doors. We go down, down, into a section of the central core I've never seen. The corridor narrows. The carpet ends. The walls are bare metal again, like the outer ring, but different. Institutional. Cold.

We stop at a door marked PROCESSING - AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

The officer opens it. Inside is a small room, empty except for a metal chair bolted to the floor. No windows. No plants. The air smells like disinfectant and something else, something I can't name.

"Seventy-two hours," the officer says. "Someone will be with you shortly."

He pushes me inside and closes the door. The lock clicks.

I stand in the middle of the room. The chair is bolted to the floor. The walls are bare. There's a drain in the corner.

I think about Rosa and the house with blue tile. I think about my brother in his engineering program. I think about the weight in my chest and how it's been getting heavier, how I thought it was purpose but maybe it was always something else.

I think about the names I gave. Carlos. Jin. Amara. Dante.

I think about Supervisor Reyes and her coffee and the way she touched my hand and said *trust*.

I think about Captain Torres reading from his tablet, his hands clean, his eyes distant.

I think about seventy-two hours.

I don't know what happens in seventy-two hours. But I've heard the rumors. I've seen the people who come out. They don't talk about it. They don't talk about anything.

The door opens. Someone enters. I don't look up. I'm staring at the drain in the corner, at the way the metal curves down into darkness, and I'm thinking about how I got here, how I made every choice that seemed right at the time, how I thought I was building something and instead I was just giving them what they needed to take everything away.

The person speaks but I don't hear the words. My hands are shaking. Not from the drill—from something else. Something that's been building for thirty-three months, something that's been sitting in my chest like a stone, cold and heavy and wrong.

The door closes again. The lock clicks.

I'm alone in the room with the bolted chair and the drain in the corner.

Seventy-two hours.

I sit down on the floor, my back against the wall, and I wait for whatever comes next.
