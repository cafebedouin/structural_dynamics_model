# The Shaft

## Part I

The shuttle descent takes forty minutes. Miguel counts them against his pulse—one hundred twenty beats per minute at separation, dropping to ninety as Mars grows from rust-colored marble to textured world. His hands don't shake until the landing jets fire.

The contract sits in his pocket, printed on actual paper. TPMC provided it at the Manila office, explained that digital signatures don't hold the same weight in Martian arbitration. The paper is thick, cream-colored, expensive. Miguel has touched it so many times in the past six weeks that the edges have gone soft.

*Three years. Seventy-two thousand dollars. Enough for the surgery, the medications after, the house with blue tile his mother described from a magazine she found at the clinic.*

The shuttle settles with a pneumatic hiss. Through the viewport, the landing pad extends in all directions—gray regolith, gray buildings, gray sky. The sun is a pale coin behind dust.

"First time?" The woman across the aisle is maybe forty, Filipina, watching him with the careful attention of someone who's learned to read strangers quickly.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't call me ma'am. I'm Reyes. You're outer ring?"

Miguel nods.

"Me too. Six years now." She stands, reaches into the overhead compartment with practiced efficiency. "Word of advice—don't spend your first paycheck on the company store. They mark everything up forty percent. Wait until you know someone who can get you supplies from the inner rings."

"Inner rings?"

"You'll see." She hands him a card—physical card, like the contract, like everything here is designed to have weight. "Message me if you need anything. We take care of our own out there."

The card says *Maria Reyes, Shift Supervisor, Extraction Operations.* Miguel puts it in his pocket with the contract.

The processing center smells like recycled air and disinfectant. They take his fingerprints, retinal scan, genetic sample. They give him a tablet with his housing assignment, work schedule, and a map of the settlement. The map shows three concentric circles: outer ring (red), middle ring (yellow), central core (green). His housing is in the outer ring, sector 7, unit 248. His work assignment is outer ring, sector 12, shaft 7.

"You'll want to review the safety protocols before your first shift," the processing clerk says. She's young, maybe twenty-five, and she doesn't look at him while she talks. Her fingers move across her screen with the mechanical precision of someone who's done this thousands of times. "Mandatory orientation is at 0600 tomorrow. Report to the equipment depot in your sector."

"Is there—" Miguel hesitates. "Is there a way to send messages to Earth?"

"Company network. Fifty cents a minute, video is a dollar. There's a terminal in every residential unit." She looks up, finally, and her expression softens slightly. "First month is hardest. After that you stop counting days."

The outer ring is a tunnel. That's Miguel's first thought when the transport drops him at sector 7—that someone has taken a tunnel and bent it into a circle and buried it under Martian regolith. The ceiling is low, maybe seven feet, lined with exposed pipes and cable runs. The walls are bare polymer, scuffed and patched. The air smells like bodies and cooking and something else, something chemical he can't identify.

Unit 248 is a room. Six meters by four. A bed, a desk, a small bathroom behind a partition. The walls are thin enough that he can hear his neighbors—someone coughing in 247, music playing in 249. The floor is soft, some kind of rubberized material that gives slightly under his weight.

Miguel sits on the bed and takes out his tablet. The message interface loads slowly. He types: *Made it safe. Starting work tomorrow. I love you.*

He attaches a photo of himself in the room, trying to smile. The camera makes his face look gray in the fluorescent light.

The cost display updates: $1.50 for the message, $3.00 for the photo.

He sends it anyway.

---

The shaft goes down three kilometers. Miguel learns this during orientation, along with the fact that the outer ring houses 4,000 workers, that the middle ring houses 800 supervisors and technicians, that the central core houses 200 administrators and executives. He learns that the shafts are numbered by sector, that each crew works a twelve-hour shift, that the quota is 400 kilograms of processed ore per person per shift.

"You'll make quota," the orientation supervisor says. He's American, maybe thirty, with the kind of lean build that suggests he's never worked a shaft himself. "Everyone makes quota eventually. The ones who don't get reassigned."

"Reassigned where?" someone asks.

"Surface maintenance. Recycling. Wherever we need people." The supervisor smiles. "Don't worry about it. Just focus on making quota."

The equipment depot issues Miguel a suit, a drill, a collection bag, and a headlamp. The suit is used—he can see the patches on the knees and elbows, the wear marks around the seals. It fits poorly, tight across his shoulders, loose around his waist.

"You'll want to adjust the air mix," the depot worker says. She's older, maybe fifty, with the kind of weathered face that suggests decades in low pressure. "Default setting is too rich. You'll get headaches. Dial it down to eighteen percent, you'll last longer."

"Eighteen percent?"

"Trust me. You want to stay sharp down there, not comfortable."

Shaft 7 is a vertical tunnel with a cage elevator. Miguel descends with five other workers—two Filipino, one Vietnamese, two he can't place by appearance. Nobody talks. The elevator drops for five minutes, the walls sliding past in the headlamp beams, before it stops at level 1.

The tunnel extends in both directions, maybe two meters wide, one meter high. They have to crouch to move. The rock face is marked with spray paint—numbers, arrows, symbols Miguel doesn't recognize.

"New guy gets position six," someone says. "End of the line. Work your way back toward the elevator. Don't cross into another crew's section."

Position six is 200 meters down the tunnel. Miguel crawls the last fifty meters, his knees already aching against the rock floor. The air is thin, cold, dry enough to crack his lips. His headlamp shows the rock face: rust-red, striated, embedded with darker veins of ore.

He starts drilling.

The quota is 400 kilograms. The ore is maybe twenty percent of what he extracts—the rest is waste rock. He has to drill, extract, separate, bag. The drill is heavy, the vibration traveling up his arms into his shoulders. After an hour his hands are shaking. After two hours he can't feel his fingers. After three hours he's moving on autopilot, drill-extract-separate-bag, drill-extract-separate-bag, the rhythm the only thing keeping him conscious.

His tablet chirps: *Quota: 67 kg. Time remaining: 9 hours.*

He keeps drilling.

At hour six he stops to eat. The ration bar tastes like cardboard and protein powder. He drinks water from his suit reservoir, realizes he's already gone through half his supply. The tunnel is silent except for the distant sound of drilling, the hiss of his air recycler, his own breathing.

His tablet chirps: *Quota: 189 kg. Time remaining: 6 hours.*

He's not going to make it.

The thought arrives with perfect clarity. The math is simple: he's averaging 31 kilograms per hour, he needs 400 kilograms total, he has six hours left. Even if he maintains his current pace—which he won't, he's already exhausted—he'll end up at 375 kilograms. Twenty-five short.

He keeps drilling anyway.

At hour ten his hands cramp so badly he has to stop. He sits in the tunnel, his back against the rock, and tries to massage feeling back into his fingers. The headlamp beam shows his collection bags: twelve of them, lined up against the wall. Maybe 300 kilograms total.

His tablet chirps: *Quota: 298 kg. Time remaining: 2 hours.*

"First day?"

Miguel turns. Someone is crawling up the tunnel toward him—one of the other workers from the elevator, the Vietnamese man. He's older than Miguel thought, maybe forty-five, with gray in his hair and deep lines around his eyes.

"Yeah."

"You're not going to make quota."

"I know."

The man nods. He reaches into his collection bag, pulls out two smaller bags, hands them to Miguel. "Put these with yours. That's another forty kilos."

"I can't—"

"Yes you can. Everyone does it first week. After that you're on your own." The man starts crawling back toward the elevator. "Name's Dante. You need anything, I'm position three."

Miguel stares at the bags. The ore inside is already processed, separated, ready to submit. He could refuse, could call Dante back, could insist on making his own quota or failing honestly.

He puts the bags with his collection.

At hour twelve the elevator returns. Miguel loads his bags, watches the counter update: *Quota: 412 kg. Status: COMPLETE.*

The ride up feels longer than the ride down.

---

The message from Rosa is waiting when he gets back to his unit. Her face fills the screen—she's in the kitchen, the one with the cracked tile they've been meaning to replace. Behind her he can see his mother sleeping in the chair, the oxygen tank beside her.

"Miguel, we got your message. I'm so glad you're safe. Your mother wanted to talk but she's sleeping now—the new medication makes her tired. The doctor says it's working though. Her oxygen levels are better."

Rosa looks tired. There are shadows under her eyes that weren't there six weeks ago.

"Your brother finished the foundation. He says the walls will go up next month. I took a picture—" The video cuts to a photo: concrete foundation, rebar sticking up, red dirt all around. "—it's going to be beautiful, Miguel. Blue tile just like she wanted."

The video cuts back to Rosa. She's smiling but it doesn't reach her eyes.

"I miss you. We all miss you. But we're so proud of you. You're doing this for all of us."

The video ends. Cost: $3.00.

Miguel plays it again. And again. He watches his mother sleep, watches Rosa's tired smile, watches the foundation that will become a house. He watches until the cost hits $15.00 and he has to stop.

He lies in bed and listens to his neighbors through the walls. Someone is crying in 247. The music in 249 has changed to something slower, sadder. His hands still shake from the drilling.

He made quota. That's what matters. He made quota and tomorrow he'll make it again and the day after that and the day after that. Three years. One thousand ninety-five days. He can do this.

He has to do this.

---

By the end of the first week, Miguel can make quota without help. His hands have developed calluses, his shoulders have stopped aching, his body has learned the rhythm of drill-extract-separate-bag. He works position six for three weeks before moving up to position five. Dante nods at him in the elevator but doesn't speak. Nobody speaks in the elevator.

The outer ring develops its own logic. Miguel learns which food stalls have the best prices, which message terminals have the fastest connection, which maintenance workers can get you supplies without asking questions. He learns that the coughing in 247 is a man named Carlos, Filipino, two years into his contract. He learns that the music in 249 is a woman named Jin, Korean, six months left before she goes home.

He learns that "reassignment" means you failed quota too many times, that surface maintenance is where they send people who can't work the shafts anymore, that nobody comes back from recycling.

He sends messages to Rosa every week. The cost adds up: $12.00 per message, $156.00 per month, $1,872.00 per year. He tells himself it's worth it. He needs to see her face, needs to see his mother getting better, needs to see the house taking shape. The walls go up. The roof goes on. They install windows.

*It's beautiful,* Rosa says. *Your mother cries every time she looks at it.*

Miguel watches the video three times. Cost: $9.00.

At three months he gets his first paycheck: $6,000.00, minus $2,400.00 for housing, minus $1,200.00 for food, minus $800.00 for equipment maintenance, minus $561.60 for messages home. Net: $1,038.40.

He sends $1,000.00 to Rosa. Keeps $38.40 for himself.

The next message shows his mother in the new house. She's sitting in the kitchen with the blue tile, crying, touching the walls like she can't believe they're real.

*Thank you,* Rosa says. *Thank you, thank you, thank you.*

Miguel plays the video until the cost hits $30.00.

It's worth it.

---

At six months, Dante stops coming to the shaft. Miguel asks around—nobody knows anything, or nobody wants to say. The crew gets a replacement, a younger guy from Indonesia who doesn't speak much English. He works position three now. Miguel is at position four.

At seven months, Carlos in 247 stops coughing. Miguel knocks on his door one evening, gets no answer. The next day there's a new person in 247, a woman from Thailand who plays the same music Jin plays in 249.

At eight months, Miguel makes supervisor. The promotion comes with a message from Maria Reyes: *Good work. Report to middle ring, sector 3, office 12 tomorrow at 0800.*

The middle ring is different. The ceiling is higher, maybe nine feet. The walls are painted, clean, decorated with safety posters and production charts. The floor is solid, doesn't give under his weight. The air smells like coffee instead of bodies.

Reyes's office is small but private. She has a real desk, a real chair, a window that shows the Martian surface. She's wearing clean clothes, not a work suit. Her hair is down.

"Sit," she says. "Coffee?"

"Thank you, ma'am."

"I told you not to call me ma'am." But she's smiling. She pours two cups from a thermal carafe, hands him one. The cup is ceramic, heavy, warm. The coffee is real—Miguel can smell it, can taste it, the first real coffee he's had in eight months.

"You've been doing good work," Reyes says. "Consistent quota, no safety violations, no complaints from your crew. That's rare."

"Thank you."

"How's your family?"

The question catches him off guard. "They're good. My mother is better. They finished the house."

"That's good. That's really good." Reyes sips her coffee. "I came here six years ago. Same situation—family needed money, I needed work. I was in the outer ring for two years before I made supervisor. You did it in eight months."

"I just did my job."

"No. You did more than your job. You helped Dante that first week. You helped the new guy from Indonesia. You keep your section clean, you follow protocols, you don't cause problems." She sets down her cup. "That's why I'm offering you a position."

Miguel waits.

"We need people we can trust in the outer ring. People who understand how things work, who can help us identify problems before they become bigger problems. You'd still work your regular shift, but you'd also report to me once a week. Let me know if anyone is struggling, if anyone is talking about breaking contract, if anyone is doing anything that might affect production."

The coffee has gone cold in Miguel's hand.

"It pays extra," Reyes says. "Five hundred a month. That's six thousand a year, eighteen thousand over three years. That's a lot of money to send home."

"You want me to inform on my crew."

"I want you to help me help them. Look, Miguel—people break out here. They get desperate, they do stupid things, they hurt themselves or others. If we know about problems early, we can intervene. We can get people reassigned before they fail quota, before they end up in recycling. This isn't about punishment. This is about keeping people safe."

Miguel looks at the coffee, at the ceramic cup, at the window showing the Martian surface. He thinks about Carlos, about Dante, about the man from Indonesia who doesn't speak English.

He thinks about his mother in the house with blue tile.

"I need to think about it," he says.

"Of course. Take your time." Reyes stands, walks to the window. "You know what I see when I look out there? I see people who came here because they had no choice. People who are working themselves to death to send money home. People who are trapped in a system that doesn't care about them." She turns back to him. "We can't change the system, Miguel. But we can make it a little less brutal. We can look out for each other. That's all I'm asking."

Miguel sets down the cup. The coffee has left a ring on the desk.

"I'll think about it," he says again.

---

He doesn't sleep that night. He lies in bed and listens to the woman in 247, to Jin's music in 249, to his own breathing. He thinks about Dante, about where he might be now. He thinks about Carlos, about the coughing that stopped.

He thinks about the house with blue tile, about his mother touching the walls, about Rosa's tired smile.

*Five hundred a month. Six thousand a year. Eighteen thousand total.*

He could pay off the debt faster. He could bring Rosa and his brother to Mars, get them better jobs, get them out of the outer ring. He could make supervisor permanently, move to the middle ring, live in a place with solid floors and real coffee.

All he has to do is tell Reyes when someone is struggling.

*This isn't about punishment. This is about keeping people safe.*

But he thinks about Dante, about Carlos, about the word "reassignment" and what it really means.

He gets up, opens his tablet, starts typing a message to Rosa. He wants to ask her what to do, wants to hear her voice, wants her to tell him it's okay or it's not okay or something that will make the decision easier.

He deletes the message before sending it. This isn't her choice to make.

At 0600 he reports to the equipment depot. At 0630 he descends into shaft 7. At 0700 he starts drilling. The rhythm is automatic now: drill-extract-separate-bag. His hands don't shake anymore. His body knows what to do.

At 1200 his tablet chirps: *Report to middle ring, sector 3, office 12 at 1800.*

He makes quota by 1400. He rides the elevator up with his crew, showers, changes, walks to the middle ring. The transition is sharp: soft floor to solid floor, body smell to coffee smell, low ceiling to high ceiling. He feels it in his chest, in his breathing, in the way his footsteps sound different.

Reyes is waiting in her office. She has two cups of coffee ready.

"So?" she says.

Miguel sits down. He picks up the cup. The ceramic is warm against his palms.

"I'll do it," he says.

## Part II

The first report is easy. Miguel tells Reyes about the man from Indonesia, about how he's struggling with the language barrier, about how he might benefit from a translator or a different crew assignment. Reyes nods, takes notes, thanks him for the information.

Two days later the man from Indonesia is gone. Someone new works position three.

The second report is harder. Jin in 249 has stopped playing music. Miguel mentions this to Reyes, says maybe she's getting sick, maybe she needs medical attention. Reyes asks if Jin has been making quota. Miguel says yes, always. Reyes says then there's nothing to worry about.

The music doesn't come back.

The third report is about a man named Tomas, Filipino, who's been talking about breaking contract. Miguel hears this secondhand, from someone who heard it from someone else. He doesn't know if it's true. He tells Reyes anyway.

Tomas disappears.

Miguel stops sleeping well. He lies in bed and counts the people who've vanished: Dante, Carlos, the man from Indonesia, Tomas. He tells himself they were reassigned, that they're working somewhere else, that they're fine. He tells himself Reyes was right—this is about keeping people safe.

He doesn't believe it.

But he keeps reporting. Once a week he goes to Reyes's office, drinks her coffee, tells her about his crew. She pays him five hundred dollars a month. He sends it all to Rosa.

The house gets furniture. His mother gets a garden. His brother starts technical school.

*We're so proud of you,* Rosa says in her messages. *You're changing our lives.*

Miguel watches the videos. Cost: $12.00 per message, $48.00 per month, $576.00 per year. He can afford it now.

At ten months, Reyes calls him to her office outside his regular reporting time.

"We have a problem," she says. No coffee this time. "There's been talk in the outer ring. People organizing, talking about collective action, work slowdowns. We need to know who's involved."

"I haven't heard anything."

"You're not listening hard enough." Reyes leans forward. "Miguel, this is serious. If people start organizing, if they start refusing to work, the whole system breaks down. We can't let that happen."

"What do you want me to do?"

"I want you to find out who's talking. I want names."

Miguel's hands start shaking. He puts them in his lap where Reyes can't see.

"And if I can't find any names?"

"Then you're not trying hard enough." Reyes stands, walks to the window. "Look, I know this is hard. I know you don't want to think of yourself as an informant. But that's not what this is. This is about protecting everyone. If a few people start a work slowdown, TPMC will crack down on the whole outer ring. People will lose their contracts, lose their pay, lose everything they've worked for. Is that what you want?"

"No."

"Then help me stop it before it starts. Give me names."

Miguel goes back to his unit. He lies in bed and listens to his neighbors. The woman in 247 is crying again. Jin's room in 249 is silent.

He thinks about Dante, about Carlos, about Tomas. He thinks about what "reassignment" really means, about where people go when they disappear.

He thinks about his mother in the garden, about Rosa's smile, about his brother in technical school.

He gets up, walks through the outer ring, listens. He goes to the food stalls, the message terminals, the equipment depot. He listens to conversations, watches for patterns, pays attention to who talks to whom.

He finds three names: Carlos (different Carlos, not the one from 247), Amara, Jin (different Jin, not the one from 249). They're meeting in sector 9, talking about quota reduction, about collective bargaining, about forcing TPMC to negotiate.

Miguel writes down the names.

He goes to Reyes's office three days later. She has coffee waiting. He sits down, picks up the cup, feels the warmth against his palms.

"I have names," he says.

Reyes smiles. "I knew you would."

Miguel gives her the names. She writes them down, thanks him, pays him an extra thousand dollars.

"This is good work, Miguel. Really good work. You're helping a lot of people."

Miguel goes back to his unit. He lies in bed and doesn't sleep. He thinks about Carlos, Amara, Jin. He thinks about what will happen to them, about where they'll go, about whether they'll come back.

He thinks about his mother touching the blue tile, about Rosa crying with gratitude, about the house that wouldn't exist without his money.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

---

Carlos, Amara, and Jin disappear within a week. New people move into their units. The outer ring is quiet for a month, then two months. Nobody talks about organizing. Nobody talks about work slowdowns. Everyone makes quota.

Miguel gets promoted to shift supervisor. The pay is better—$8,000 per month instead of $6,000. He moves to the middle ring, to a unit with solid floors and a private bathroom and a window. He can afford to message Rosa every day now.

The house gets a second story. His mother's garden grows. His brother graduates from technical school, gets a job, starts sending money home too.

*We don't need your money anymore,* Rosa says. *You can start saving for yourself.*

But Miguel keeps sending it. He doesn't know what else to do with it.

At fourteen months, Reyes calls him to her office.

"We need to talk about your contract," she says.

Miguel's chest tightens. "Is something wrong?"

"No. Nothing's wrong. In fact, everything is very right." Reyes pulls up a file on her tablet. "You've been an exemplary employee, Miguel. Consistent production, good leadership, reliable reporting. TPMC wants to offer you a contract extension."

"Extension?"

"Another three years. Same pay, same benefits, but with a promotion to operations manager. You'd oversee multiple shafts, coordinate between crews, report directly to central core administration. It's a significant step up."

Miguel stares at the tablet. The contract is already drafted, ready for signature.

"I have one year left on my current contract."

"I know. But we're offering you the extension now, with a signing bonus of twenty thousand dollars. You could send that home, pay off any remaining debt, set your family up for years." Reyes slides the tablet across the desk. "This is a good opportunity, Miguel. Most people never get offered something like this."

Miguel looks at the contract. The terms are clear: three more years, $8,000 per month, operations manager position. The signing bonus is real.

He thinks about going home in one year. He thinks about seeing Rosa, about seeing his mother in the house with blue tile, about sleeping in a bed that doesn't vibrate with his neighbors' breathing.

He thinks about staying for three more years. He thinks about the money, about what it could do, about how much more he could send home.

He thinks about Carlos, Amara, Jin. About Dante, about the first Carlos, about Tomas. About all the people who've disappeared, about where they went, about what happened to them.

"Can I think about it?" he says.

"Of course. Take a week. But Miguel—" Reyes leans forward. "—opportunities like this don't come around often. Don't let it pass you by."

Miguel goes back to his unit in the middle ring. He sits by the window and looks out at the Martian surface. The sun is setting, turning the dust red-gold. It's beautiful in a way that makes his chest ache.

He opens his tablet, starts typing a message to Rosa. He wants to ask her what to do, wants to hear her voice, wants her to tell him to come home or to stay or something that will make the decision easier.

He deletes the message. This isn't her choice to make.

He thinks about the past fourteen months. He thinks about the shaft, about the drilling, about the rhythm of drill-extract-separate-bag. He thinks about the reports to Reyes, about the names he gave, about the people who disappeared.

He thinks about the house with blue tile, about his mother's garden, about his brother's education. He thinks about all the things his money bought, about all the things it made possible.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

He signs the contract.

---

The signing bonus arrives the next day. Miguel sends all twenty thousand to Rosa. Her response comes six hours later—a video of his mother crying, of his brother hugging her, of the house with blue tile and the garden and the second story and everything his money built.

*Thank you,* Rosa says. *Thank you, thank you, thank you. You've given us everything.*

Miguel watches the video. Cost: $3.00. He watches it again. And again. He watches until the cost hits $30.00 and he has to stop.

It's worth it.

He starts his new position the following week. Operations manager means coordinating five shafts, managing fifteen supervisors, reporting to central core administration. It means meetings in the central core, in rooms with real windows and real furniture and air that doesn't smell like anything at all.

The central core is different from the middle ring, which is different from the outer ring. The differences are small but absolute: the quality of light, the texture of air, the sound of footsteps on different floors. Miguel feels them in his body, in his breathing, in the way his chest tightens when he transitions between rings.

He meets the administrators. They're polite, professional, efficient. They talk about production targets, efficiency metrics, cost optimization. They never talk about the people in the outer ring, about the shafts, about what happens in the 72-hour processing.

Miguel doesn't ask.

At sixteen months, one of his supervisors reports a problem: a crew in shaft 3 is consistently underperforming, missing quota by 10-15% per shift. The supervisor wants to know what to do.

Miguel reviews the data. The crew has six people. Five are making quota. One isn't—a woman named Luz, Filipina, eight months into her contract.

Miguel could recommend reassignment. That's the standard protocol. That's what Reyes would do.

Instead he goes to shaft 3. He descends into the tunnel, crawls to position six where Luz is working. She's small, maybe fifty kilograms, struggling with a drill that's too heavy for her frame.

"You need a lighter drill," Miguel says.

Luz looks up, startled. "Sir?"

"The drill. It's too heavy for you. You're wasting energy fighting the weight instead of drilling. Come with me."

He takes her to the equipment depot, gets her a lighter drill, shows her how to adjust the air mix, how to pace herself, how to work smarter instead of harder.

"Thank you, sir," Luz says.

"Don't call me sir. Just make quota."

She does. The next week her production is up 20%. The week after that she's at 105% of quota.

Miguel doesn't report this to Reyes. He doesn't report it to anyone. He just notes it in his log: *Equipment adjustment, problem resolved.*

He starts doing this more often. When a crew underperforms, he goes to the shaft, watches them work, identifies the problem. Usually it's equipment, or technique, or air mix. Sometimes it's injury, or illness, or exhaustion. He fixes what he can fix. He doesn't report what he can't fix.

At eighteen months, Reyes calls him to her office.

"Your numbers are good," she says. "Really good. Production is up across all your shafts, safety violations are down, reassignments are down. Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

"Thank you."

"But I need to talk to you about something else." Reyes pulls up a file. "We've had reports of organizing activity again. Different people this time, but same pattern—talking about collective action, work slowdowns, contract negotiation. We need names."

Miguel's hands start shaking. He puts them in his lap.

"I haven't heard anything."

"You're not in the outer ring anymore. You need to talk to your supervisors, have them talk to their crews. Someone knows something. I need you to find out who."

Miguel goes back to his office in the central core. He sits at his desk and looks at the production reports, at the names of his supervisors, at the names of their crews. He thinks about Carlos, Amara, Jin. He thinks about what happened to them, about where they went, about whether they're still alive.

He thinks about Luz, about the lighter drill, about how she's making quota now.

He thinks about his mother in the garden, about Rosa's smile, about his brother's job.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

But this time, something is different. This time, he knows what the choice means. He knows what will happen if he gives Reyes names. He knows what "reassignment" means, what "processing" means, what happens to people who disappear.

He knows, and he has to choose anyway.

He spends three days listening. He talks to his supervisors, asks careful questions, pays attention to what they don't say. He finds the names: three people in shaft 7, two in shaft 9, one in shaft 12. They're meeting in sector 11, talking about quota reduction, about collective bargaining, about forcing TPMC to negotiate.

Miguel writes down the names.

He goes to Reyes's office. She has coffee waiting. He sits down, picks up the cup, feels the warmth against his palms.

"I have names," he says.

Reyes smiles. "I knew you would."

Miguel gives her the names. She writes them down, thanks him, pays him an extra two thousand dollars.

"This is good work, Miguel. Really good work. You're helping a lot of people."

Miguel goes back to his office. He sits at his desk and doesn't move. He thinks about the six people whose names he just gave, about what will happen to them, about where they'll go.

He thinks about his mother, about Rosa, about his brother. He thinks about the house with blue tile, about the garden, about everything his money bought.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

And this time, he knows it will always be true. This is what he is now. This is what he's become. Not a victim, not a perpetrator, but something in between—someone who makes choices under constraint, someone who is complicit in his own exploitation and the exploitation of others.

Someone who will keep making these choices, over and over, for three more years.

Someone who will go home eventually, who will see Rosa and his mother and his brother, who will sleep in the house with blue tile.

Someone who will never be able to forget the names he gave, the people who disappeared, the price of everything he bought.

The six people disappear within a week. New people move into their units. The outer ring is quiet.

Miguel keeps working. He coordinates shafts, manages supervisors, reports to administration. He fixes equipment problems, adjusts air mixes, helps people make quota. He sends money home every month.

At twenty months, he gets another call from Reyes.

"We need to talk," she says.

Miguel goes to her office. No coffee this time.

"There's been an incident," Reyes says. "One of the people you identified—the one from shaft 12—he tried to escape during processing. He's in medical now, but he's talking. He's saying you set him up, that you targeted him specifically, that you're working for TPMC to suppress organizing."

Miguel's chest tightens. "That's not—"

"I know it's not true. But it doesn't matter. He's talking, and people are listening. We need to contain this before it spreads."

"How?"

"You need to go through processing."

The words don't make sense at first. Miguel stares at Reyes, trying to understand.

"Processing?"

"It's standard protocol when an informant is compromised. We process you, document your cooperation, demonstrate that you were acting in good faith. It takes 72 hours. After that, you're cleared and you can go back to work."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're in breach of contract. We terminate your employment, you forfeit all remaining pay, and you're liable for the cost of your transport to Mars plus penalties. That's about two hundred thousand dollars." Reyes leans forward. "Miguel, this is just a formality. You haven't done anything wrong. We just need to document that, officially. Three days, then it's over."

Miguel thinks about the house with blue tile, about his mother's garden, about his brother's job. He thinks about two hundred thousand dollars in debt, about what that would do to his family.

He thinks about the man from shaft 12, about what "escape during processing" means, about why he's in medical now.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

"When?" he says.

"Now."

## Part III

The processing center is in the central core, in a section Miguel has never seen before. The walls are white, the floors are white, the lights are white. Everything is clean in a way that makes his skin crawl.

They take his tablet, his ID, his clothes. They give him a gray jumpsuit and soft shoes and nothing else. They lead him to a room with a table, two chairs, and a camera.

"Sit," the technician says. She's young, maybe twenty-five, with the same mechanical efficiency as the processing clerk from his first day. "Someone will be with you shortly."

Miguel sits. The chair is comfortable, padded, designed for long sessions. He stares at the camera, at the red light that indicates it's recording.

He waits.

The door opens. A man enters—middle-aged, gray hair, wearing a suit that looks expensive even by central core standards. He sits across from Miguel, opens a tablet, smiles.

"Miguel Santos. Operations manager, shaft coordination, eighteen months employed. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"My name is Dr. Chen. I'm here to conduct your processing evaluation. This is a standard procedure, nothing to be concerned about. We're just going to review your employment history, your reporting activities, and your understanding of TPMC policies. Should take about three days. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Good. Let's begin." Dr. Chen taps his tablet. "You were recruited as an informant by Supervisor Maria Reyes approximately ten months ago. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"And you've provided regular reports on crew activities, organizing attempts, and potential contract violations. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"How many names have you provided to Supervisor Reyes?"

Miguel thinks about Carlos, Amara, Jin. About the six people from shafts 7, 9, and 12. About all the others whose names he's forgotten.

"I don't know. Maybe twenty."

"Twenty-three, according to our records. Do you know what happened to those people?"

"They were reassigned."

"Is that what you believe, or what you were told?"

Miguel doesn't answer.

Dr. Chen taps his tablet again. A video appears on the wall screen—footage from a shaft, showing a man being restrained by security, being dragged to the elevator, screaming. The audio is muted but Miguel can see the man's mouth forming words: *Please, please, please.*

"That's Carlos Mendoza," Dr. Chen says. "You identified him as an organizing risk eight months ago. Do you remember him?"

"Yes."

"Do you know where he is now?"

"No."

"He's in recycling. That's where we send people who can't work the shafts anymore, who are too injured or too traumatized or too dangerous. They process waste, sort materials, work in conditions that make the outer ring look comfortable. Most people last about six months before they break completely. Carlos has been there for eight months. He's still alive, technically."

Dr. Chen changes the video. Another shaft, another person being dragged away. Then another. Then another. Miguel recognizes some of them. Most he doesn't.

"These are the people you identified," Dr. Chen says. "Twenty-three people. Eleven in recycling, seven in medical, three terminated, two deceased. That's the result of your reporting. Do you understand?"

Miguel's hands are shaking. He puts them in his lap.

"I was told they would be reassigned. I was told this was about keeping people safe."

"And you believed that?"

"I wanted to believe it."

"But you knew better."

Miguel doesn't answer.

Dr. Chen leans forward. "Miguel, I'm not here to punish you. I'm here to help you understand what you've been doing, what you've been part of. TPMC operates an extraction system. We extract ore from Mars, we extract labor from workers, we extract compliance through debt and obligation. You've been an instrument of that extraction. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Do you feel guilty about it?"

Miguel thinks about his mother in the garden, about Rosa's smile, about his brother's job. He thinks about the house with blue tile, about everything his money bought.

"Yes."

"Do you think you had a choice?"

Miguel thinks about the contract, about the debt, about his mother's oxygen tank. He thinks about the moment he signed, about the moment he agreed to inform, about the moment he gave Reyes the first names.

"I don't know."

"That's the right answer." Dr. Chen closes his tablet. "You did have a choice, Miguel. You chose to come to Mars. You chose to accept Reyes's offer. You chose to give her names. Those were real choices, made by you, with full knowledge of the consequences. But you also had no choice. Your mother needed surgery, your family needed money, the system gave you exactly one path forward. Both things are true. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Good. Because that's what we need you to understand. You're not a victim. You're not a villain. You're a participant in a system that requires your participation. You made choices, and those choices had consequences, and you're responsible for those consequences. But you're also constrained by circumstances beyond your control, and those constraints shaped your choices. Both things are true. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then let's talk about what happens next."

---

The processing continues for three days. Dr. Chen shows Miguel more videos, more records, more evidence of what his reporting has done. He shows Miguel the contracts, the clauses, the fine print that makes everything legal. He shows Miguel the production reports, the efficiency metrics, the cost-benefit analyses that justify the system.

He shows Miguel himself—recordings of his meetings with Reyes, transcripts of his reports, analysis of his decision-making patterns. He shows Miguel how he was recruited, how he was manipulated, how he was shaped into an instrument of extraction.

And then he shows Miguel how he chose it all anyway.

"You're not a victim," Dr. Chen says again and again. "You made choices. You're responsible for those choices. But you're also constrained. Both things are true."

By the third day, Miguel understands. He understands what he is, what he's done, what he's become. He understands that he chose this and had no choice. He understands that he's guilty and also trapped. He understands that both things are true and will always be true.

On the third day, Dr. Chen gives him a new contract.

"This is a revised employment agreement," Dr. Chen says. "It acknowledges your role as an informant, documents your cooperation with TPMC security operations, and indemnifies you against any legal liability for your reporting activities. It also extends your employment for another five years, with a promotion to security operations manager and a significant pay increase. Sign it, and you can go back to work. Refuse, and you're in breach of your original contract. You'll owe TPMC approximately two hundred thousand dollars, plus penalties."

Miguel reads the contract. The terms are clear. Five more years. Security operations manager. $15,000 per month. Complete legal immunity for his informant activities.

He thinks about his mother, about Rosa, about his brother. He thinks about the house with blue tile, about the garden, about everything his money bought.

He thinks about Carlos in recycling, about the eleven people in medical, about the three terminated, about the two deceased.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

He signs the contract.

---

Miguel returns to work the next day. His new office is in the central core, two floors above his old office. The view is better. The air is cleaner. The coffee is real.

He meets his new team: six security operators, all former informants, all processed, all carrying the same knowledge he carries. They don't talk about it. They just do their jobs.

Miguel's job is to coordinate informant networks across all three rings. He recruits new informants, manages their reporting, processes their information. He identifies organizing risks, tracks potential violations, recommends interventions. He's good at it. He's always been good at it.

At twenty-four months, he gets a message from Rosa. The video shows his mother in the garden, surrounded by flowers, smiling. Behind her, Miguel can see the house with blue tile, the second story, the solar panels on the roof.

*She's so happy,* Rosa says. *We're all so happy. You did this, Miguel. You gave us everything.*

Miguel watches the video. Cost: $3.00. He can afford it now. He can afford anything now.

He doesn't watch it again.

At twenty-six months, one of his informants reports organizing activity in shaft 7. Miguel reviews the information, identifies the names, passes them to processing. Six people disappear within a week.

He doesn't think about where they go. He doesn't think about Carlos, about Amara, about Jin. He doesn't think about the twenty-three people from his first year, or the forty-seven from his second year, or the sixty-two so far this year.

He just does his job.

At thirty months, Reyes retires. She's going back to Earth, back to the Philippines, back to her family. She stops by Miguel's office to say goodbye.

"You've done well," she says. "Better than I expected. You're going to go far here."

"Thank you."

"Can I give you some advice?" She sits down, even though Miguel hasn't invited her. "Don't think about it too much. Don't count the names, don't track the numbers, don't try to calculate the cost. Just do your job, send money home, and remember why you're here. That's the only way to survive."

"Is that what you did?"

"For six years." Reyes stands. "I'm going home to a house I bought with this money. I'm going to see my daughter graduate from university. I'm going to die in my own bed, in my own country, surrounded by family. That's what this bought me. Was it worth it? I don't know. But I chose it, and I'd choose it again."

She leaves. Miguel never sees her again.

At thirty-six months, Miguel's original contract expires. He's been on Mars for three years. He's sent home $432,000. His mother is healthy. His brother has a good job. The house with blue tile is paid off, expanded, beautiful.

He could go home now. The new contract doesn't start for another month. He could take his savings, buy a ticket, leave Mars forever.

Instead he signs a lease on an apartment in the central core. He signs up for another five years. He keeps working.

At forty-two months, he gets promoted to director of security operations. He manages fifty informants, coordinates with central administration, reports directly to the executive board. His salary is $25,000 per month. He sends most of it home.

Rosa stops asking when he's coming back.

At forty-eight months, he recruits a new informant—a young man from the outer ring, Filipino, twenty-three years old, eight months into his contract. The man's name is Daniel. He has a mother who needs surgery, a family who needs money, a house they want to build.

Miguel takes Daniel to his office in the central core. He pours two cups of coffee—real coffee, expensive coffee, the kind that costs more than Daniel makes in a week. He hands Daniel a cup.

"You've been doing good work," Miguel says. "Consistent quota, no safety violations, no complaints from your crew. That's rare."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't call me sir." Miguel sips his coffee. "I came here six years ago. Same situation—family needed money, I needed work. I was in the outer ring for eight months before I made supervisor. You could do it faster."

Daniel waits.

"We need people we can trust in the outer ring," Miguel says. "People who understand how things work, who can help us identify problems before they become bigger problems. You'd still work your regular shift, but you'd also report to me once a week. Let me know if anyone is struggling, if anyone is talking about breaking contract, if anyone is doing anything that might affect production."

The coffee has gone cold in Daniel's hand.

"You want me to inform on my crew," Daniel says.

"I want you to help me help them," Miguel says. "Look, Daniel—people break out here. They get desperate, they do stupid things, they hurt themselves or others. If we know about problems early, we can intervene. We can get people reassigned before they fail quota, before they end up in recycling. This isn't about punishment. This is about keeping people safe."

Miguel watches Daniel's face. He sees the calculation, the conflict, the moment when Daniel thinks about his mother, about his family, about the house they want to build.

"It pays extra," Miguel says. "Five hundred a month. That's six thousand a year. That's a lot of money to send home."

Daniel looks at the coffee, at the ceramic cup, at the window showing the Martian surface.

"I need to think about it," Daniel says.

"Of course. Take your time." Miguel stands, walks to the window. "You know what I see when I look out there? I see people who came here because they had no choice. People who are working themselves to death to send money home. People who are trapped in a system that doesn't care about them." He turns back to Daniel. "We can't change the system. But we can make it a little less brutal. We can look out for each other. That's all I'm asking."

Daniel sets down the cup. The coffee has left a ring on the desk.

"I'll think about it," Daniel says.

Miguel watches him leave. He knows Daniel will come back. He knows Daniel will say yes. He knows Daniel will give him names, will watch people disappear, will sign the contracts, will get promoted, will send money home.

He knows Daniel will become what Miguel is now.

He knows this because he knows the system. He knows how it works, how it extracts, how it reproduces itself through its victims. He knows that Daniel has a choice and has no choice. He knows both things are true.

He sits at his desk and looks out at the Martian surface. The sun is setting, turning the dust red-gold. It's beautiful in a way that makes his chest ache.

He opens his tablet, checks his messages. There's one from Rosa—a video of his mother in the garden, surrounded by flowers, smiling.

*She asks about you every day,* Rosa says. *When are you coming home?*

Miguel closes the message without responding. He doesn't know how to answer. He doesn't know if he's ever going home. He doesn't know if there's anything left to go home to, or if he's become something that can't go home, something that belongs here now, in this system, doing this work.

He thinks about Daniel, about the choice he'll make, about the person he'll become.

He thinks: *I chose this.*

He thinks: *I had no choice.*

Both are true.

And both will always be true, for him and for Daniel and for everyone who comes after, for as long as the system needs them, for as long as they choose to participate, for as long as they have no choice.

Miguel closes his tablet. He finishes his coffee. He goes back to work.
