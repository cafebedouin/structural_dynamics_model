# The Grain

The sensor settles against the formation face and I wait. My hands know this part — the weight of the rig against stone that's older than oxygen, the way the contact pad finds purchase in the metamorphic banding. The timer starts. The resonance needs time to stabilize. Time to crouch in a drift that's barely high enough to kneel in, helmet scraping the ceiling every time I shift my weight.

The cold comes up through my boots. Always does, this deep. The Creighton Deep Extension, and the thermal management only brings the temperature to barely above freezing. My thermals are good — I saved for them — but a full shift in this cold and it finds you anyway. Through the synthetic weave, through the air gaps, into your joints. Your knees go stiff first. Then your fingers, even inside the gloves.

I check the sensor readout. Resonance building. Clean so far. This is Archean formation, ancient beyond measure, and when it's good it's *good* — the kind of reading that certifies at top rate if the SSA doesn't reject it. If.

The formation face in front of me is beautiful. I'm not supposed to notice that — I'm supposed to place the sensor and wait and upload and move to the next placement — but I notice anyway. The banding is so clear here: dark layers and light layers, the record of pressure and heat reorganizing the crystal structure across geologic time. The dark bands are smoother. Higher metamorphic grade. More pressure. The stone remembers.

I run my bare hand along the face, glove off for just a moment. The texture tells you things the sensor can't. The dark bands are glassy-smooth, almost waxy under your fingertips. The light bands have more grain, a slight catch against your skin. You can feel where the pressure was highest, where the crystal structure locked tight. The stone is cold enough that touching it bare-handed stings, but I need to feel it. Need to know what I'm reading.

My helmet lamp flickers. Battery running low. I've got more placements after this, and if the lamp dies before I surface I'll have to navigate by touch. I've done it before. The shaft has a rhythm — the cage stops, the drift branches, the formation changes texture under your hands. But I'd rather not.

The sensor hums. Not a sound you hear — a vibration you feel through the contact pad, through your gloves, into your palms. When it's right, when the placement *sings*, the hum is steady. No flutter. No skip. Just the stone speaking and the sensor listening.

This placement sings.

The hum holds steady. I close my eyes and feel it — not just in my hands but in my chest, a resonance that travels through the contact pad, through the rig frame, into my sternum. When a placement sings like this, you know it before the readout confirms it. Your body knows.

I wait. The timer runs. When it finishes I tap the upload sequence on the sensor screen. The reading compresses, encrypts, travels up the fiber-optic tether to the surface station. The confirmation blinks green.

Placement submitted for certification.

I pull the sensor, pack it carefully, and move to the next face.

This placement fights.

I press the contact pad against the formation and wait. Nothing. No hum. I adjust the angle, try again. The resonance starts, flutters, drops out. I shift the pad slightly left. The hum comes back, holds briefly, dies.

The formation face looks clean. Same Archean banding, same metamorphic grade. But something is wrong. The sensor can't find the grain.

I try a different spot on the face, press harder. The hum starts, wavers. Not clean. Not stable. I check the readout: frequency drift, signal degradation. I wait longer, hoping it will stabilize.

It doesn't.

I know this won't certify. But I place it anyway because the shift quota is minimum placements and if I don't make quota I don't get the shaft access refund. The refund matters. It's the difference between breaking even and going into debt.

I upload the reading and move on.

---

The canteen is crowded when I surface. End of first shift, and everyone's here. The smell hits me before I'm through the door — fry oil and protein bars and bodies that have been underground all day. I find a seat at the long table near the back.

Chika's already there, eating rice and stew. She looks up when I sit.

"How many?" she asks.

"Half a dozen placements. Most good, maybe a couple borderline. The last placement I know they'll reject."

"Why?"

"Couldn't get stable resonance. The formation face looked clean but the sensor couldn't find it." I pull out my ration card, slide it across the counter to Bayo. He swipes it and hands back a bowl of stew and a hunk of bread. The card balance drops.

Chika makes a sound in her throat. "You placed it anyway?"

"Needed the quota."

"They'll reject it."

"I know."

She's right. They will. But you place it anyway because the mathematics of the shift are unforgiving. Quota minus rejections equals certified placements. Certified placements times rate equals gross. Gross minus fees, minus rent, minus food equals what's left. And what's left is usually not much.

I eat. The stew is thin but it's hot and my hands are still cold from the deep. The heat of the bowl feels good against my palms. Chika finishes her rice and leans back.

"You check your notices yet?"

"Not yet."

"I got rejections this morning. Multiple. Some of them I *know* were clean — stable, good banding, no contamination. 'Insufficient clarity.' That's all it said."

I've heard this before. We all have. The SSA rejection notices don't explain. They just state: *Insufficient clarity. Non-standard lithology. Contaminated by modern drilling.* You're supposed to know what it means. But we don't.

"You dispute it?" I ask.

"What's the point? Dispute costs credits and they never reverse."

She's right about that too. I disputed once — a reading I *knew* was good, stable, deep Archean, clean signal — and they sent back the same notice with an additional line: *Rejection upheld. No further correspondence.* Credits gone.

I finish the stew and check the terminal near the door. My credit balance has dropped since yesterday. Rent is due soon. Food for the week will take most of what's left. The margin is thin.

The screen blinks. New message. I open it.

*Rejection Notice*
*Placement ID: CD-EXT-447-A*
*Reason: Insufficient clarity*
*Certification fee: (non-refundable)*

That was days ago. Proterozoic formation, clean placement, stable reading. I checked the log multiple times before I uploaded. The resonance was *clean*.

I close the message and walk out.

---

The prefab is cold when I get back. I turn on the heater — it'll take time to bring the temperature up to something livable — and sit on the bed. Small space. Bed, table, chair, sink. The bathroom is shared, down the hall, multiple units to a bathroom.

I pull out my sensor rig and check the calibration. The readout is green. Calibration is good. I run a diagnostic on the contact pad. No faults. I check the resonance log from my last rejected placement. Stable signal, frequency within standard parameters.

I don't understand.

I open the SSA guidelines on my tablet. *Certification Requirements for Stratigraphic Readings, Section on Signal Clarity.* The language is dense — "resonance stability within standard deviation," "frequency band compliance," "lithological consistency" — but the parameters are clear. My placement met every requirement.

I could dispute it. But Chika's right — they never reverse.

I put the tablet down and lie back on the bed. The heater hums. My body aches — knees, back, shoulders. A full shift crouched in drifts. Tomorrow I'll do it again.

I close my eyes and see the formation face from this morning. The Archean banding, dark and light, the stone holding time beyond comprehension. The sensor settled so clean. The hum was steady, that resonance I felt in my chest. The stone was speaking.

And the SSA said: *Insufficient clarity.*

I don't understand.

---

Days later I'm back in the deep. Placement in the middle of my shift. My knees are already screaming and I've got more to go.

This placement is fighting.

I've had the sensor on the formation face for a while and the resonance won't stabilize. The hum skips, flutters, drops out. I adjust the contact pad angle. Nothing. I move it slightly, try again. The hum comes back, holds briefly, drops out.

The formation face looks clean. Proterozoic, metamorphic banding, no obvious contamination. But something is wrong. The sensor can't find the grain.

I pull off my glove and touch the face bare-handed. The texture is different here. The banding looks the same as formations that sing, but under my fingers there's a subtle difference. The dark bands have a roughness the sensor can't detect. Not contamination — something in the crystal structure itself. The pressure gradient was different here. The metamorphic grade is slightly lower.

I don't have language for what I'm feeling. The SSA guidelines don't describe it. But my hands know it.

I try the sensor again. More time passes. The hum holds, drops out. I'm wasting time. I've got more placements and limited hours left in the shift and if I don't make quota I lose the access refund.

I pull the sensor, move to the next face.

This placement sings.

The contact pad settles and the hum is immediate, steady, clean. I don't understand why. The formation looks the same — same lithology, same age, same metamorphic grade. But this face sings and the last face fought.

I touch this face bare-handed too. The texture is different. Smoother. The dark bands are glassy under my fingertips. The crystal structure locked tighter here. More pressure. Higher grade.

The sensor found what my hands already knew.

I wait the full duration, upload the reading, move on.

---

End of shift. I surface and check my messages.

Multiple placements submitted. Rejections already.

*Placement ID: CD-EXT-512-C*
*Reason: Insufficient clarity*

*Placement ID: CD-EXT-513-A*
*Reason: Non-standard lithology*

I open the logs. The first was the placement that fought — unstable, I knew it wouldn't certify. But the second was *clean*. Stable, deep Proterozoic, no contamination. I checked it multiple times before I uploaded. That was the placement that sang.

*Non-standard lithology.*

I don't know what that means.

I walk to the canteen. Chika's there, and Jin. Jin is older, and he's been reading formations for years. He owns his equipment — no rental fees — and he gets consulting work sometimes, off-world firms that want readings verified before purchase. He knows more than the rest of us.

I sit down next to him.

"Jin, what's 'non-standard lithology'?"

He looks up from his rice. "Where'd you get that?"

"Rejection notice. Proterozoic formation, clean signal, stable reading. The placement sang. I felt it. But they rejected it anyway."

He frowns. "Proterozoic should certify. What was the sulfide concentration?"

"I don't know. The sensor doesn't measure that."

"Exactly." He puts down his fork. "The standard measures sulfide concentration. If your formation doesn't have enough sulfide — even if the signal is clean, even if the reading is good — it gets rejected."

"Why?"

"Because the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction. High sulfide means high nickel. Low sulfide means waste rock. The SSA still uses the same calibration."

I stare at him. "But we're not mining nickel. We're reading formations."

"I know."

"So why—"

"Because changing the standard would invalidate decades of certified readings. Every database, every archive, every off-world construction plan built on SSA data would need recalibration. The SSA isn't going to do that."

I sit back. My hands are shaking. I don't know if it's anger or cold or exhaustion.

"So what do we do?"

Jin shrugs. "You learn which formations certify and which don't. You avoid the low-sulfide faces. You place your sensors where the standard wants them, not where the stone wants them."

"That's not reading formations. That's—"

"That's how you make quota." He picks up his fork. "You want to read formations for love, go work for a university. You want to eat, you follow the standard."

I sit there for a long time after he goes back to his meal. The canteen noise continues around me — the clatter of trays, the hum of conversation, the scrape of chairs. But all I can think about is the formation face from this morning. The way it sang. The way my hands knew it was good before the sensor confirmed it. The way the resonance traveled through the contact pad into my chest.

The stone was speaking. I was listening. And the SSA said it wasn't worth hearing.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo opened the rejection notice and added it to her database.

*Reader: Lena Okeke. Formation: Proterozoic, Creighton Deep Extension. Rejection reason: Non-standard lithology. Signal: stable, frequency within parameters.*

Another entry.

She saved the file and closed the laptop. Her office was small — a converted bedroom in her house on the Sudbury rim — but the window looked out over the Basin. From here she could see the crater floor, the flooded shafts, the ruins of the old smelters. Geologic time laid bare, and the SSA certified less than a third of it.

She had worked for the SSA for years. Certification officer, regional division, responsible for reviewing applications and maintaining the calibration database. She had been good at it. She had believed in it.

Then she started noticing the pattern.

Archean formations — the oldest, the most geologically significant — had the highest rejection rate. More than half. The rejection reason was always the same: *Non-standard lithology.* But when she checked the resonance logs, the signals were clean. Stable. Within parameters.

She asked her supervisor about it. He said the standard was calibrated for nickel-bearing formations. Archean formations had different sulfide structures. Lower concentrations. The standard couldn't certify them accurately.

She asked why they didn't recalibrate the standard.

He said it would cost too much. The entire system would need updating. Every sensor, every database, every buyer agreement. The SSA's budget didn't allow for it.

She asked what happened to the rejected readings.

He said they were discarded.

Geologic time discarded because the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction generations ago.

She quit months later.

That was years ago. Since then she had been building her private database: every rejected reading she could verify as scientifically valid. Every formation the SSA had discarded. Every piece of planetary memory the standard had missed.

She opened her email and composed a message to Dr. Sarah Chen, Planetary Geology Department, University of Toronto.

*Dr. Chen,*

*I am writing to follow up on my previous correspondence regarding alternative stratigraphic certification standards. I have now documented rejected SSA readings that meet all signal quality parameters but fail due to lithological bias in the baseline calibration.*

*These readings represent significant planetary formation data, particularly from Archean-era formations, which are underrepresented in current databases due to their low sulfide concentrations.*

*I have attached a summary analysis and sample data. I believe this material could form the basis for an alternative certification system optimized for planetary geology rather than extraction economics.*

*I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this further.*

*Sincerely,*
*Dr. Amara Okonkwo*

She hit send and closed the laptop.

Dr. Chen would not respond. She knew this. Dr. Chen had not responded to the previous emails. Nobody bought uncertified readings. The SSA system was entrenched. The market ran on certified data, and certified data meant SSA data, and SSA data meant the old baseline.

But she kept sending the emails anyway.

She stood and walked to the window. The sun was setting over the Basin, and the light turned the crater floor gold. Somewhere down there, deep underground, strat-readers were placing sensors against formation faces. Some placements would sing. Some would fight. Some would certify. Most would be rejected.

And the stone would keep holding its memory, whether anyone listened or not.

---

Weeks pass. My rejection rate climbs. More than half my placements now. The mathematics stop working. Quota minus rejections leaves too few certified placements. The gross drops. After fees, after rent, after food, there's nothing left. Then less than nothing. I owe the credit cooperative.

I'm sitting in the canteen with Chika and other readers — Obi and Ngozi. We're all in the same situation. High rejection rates, low net income, debt accumulating. Obi borrowed to fix his sensor rig. Ngozi borrowed to cover medical after she fell in a drift and broke her wrist.

"We should go to the SSA office," Chika says. "All of us. Demand they explain the rejection criteria."

Obi shakes his head. "They won't tell us. You know they won't."

"Then we refuse to work until they do."

Ngozi's laugh is sharp. "And then what? We don't work, we don't eat."

"So we organize," Chika says. Her voice is rising. "Every reader in the Basin. We shut down every shaft until they negotiate."

"With what leverage?" Obi asks. "The SSA has the certification monopoly. Off-world buyers won't touch uncertified data."

"There has to be—"

"There isn't." Ngozi cuts her off. Her voice is flat. "I tried organizing at my last site. You know what happened? Got fired. Blacklisted from the shaft access system. Couldn't work anywhere in the Basin for half a year." She looks at Chika. "You know what half a year without work means? I was sleeping in the old smelter ruins. Eating whatever I could scavenge. Nearly starved before they let me back in."

The table goes quiet.

Chika's hands are fists on the table. "But if we all—"

"We won't all," Ngozi says. "There's always someone who needs the work more than they need the principle. Always someone who'll cross the line. And the SSA knows it. They'll wait us out. They've got reserves. We don't."

"So we just accept it?" Chika's voice cracks. "We just keep working for rejections we don't understand? We just let them discard our work because the standard was calibrated for something that doesn't exist anymore?"

"Yes," Ngozi says. "That's exactly what we do. Because the alternative is starving."

"There has to be another way—"

"There isn't." Obi's voice is quiet but it cuts through. "I've been reading for most of my working life. I've seen people try to organize. I've seen them blacklisted. I've seen them leave the Basin with nothing. The SSA doesn't negotiate. They don't have to."

Chika looks at me. "Lena. You've been quiet. What do you think?"

I don't know what I think. I think about the formation face from last week, the way it sang. I think about my hands knowing the texture before the sensor confirmed it. I think about the resonance traveling through the contact pad into my chest. I think about the SSA rejection notice: *Non-standard lithology.*

"I think," I say slowly, "that we're reading something real. The formations are real. The memory in the stone is real. And the standard is asking the wrong question."

"That doesn't help us," Obi says.

"I know." My voice is steadier now. "I know it doesn't help. But I think we should name it anyway. The SSA isn't making mistakes. They're not confused. They're measuring something different than what we're reading. And they know it."

"So what do we do?" Chika asks again.

I look at her. At Obi. At Ngozi. We're all exhausted. We're all in debt. We're all going to go back down into the deep tomorrow and place sensors and hope they certify and know most of them won't.

"We keep reading," I say. "We learn which formations certify and which don't. We follow the standard because we have to. But we remember what we're hearing when the placements sing. We remember that the stone is speaking even when the SSA won't listen."

"That's not enough," Chika says.

"I know," I say. "But it's what we have."

Ngozi stands up. "I need to get back. Early shift tomorrow."

Obi follows. Then Chika. I sit alone at the table for a while, looking at the empty bowls, the crumbs of bread, the ring of condensation where Chika's cup was.

We could organize. We could refuse to work. We could demand the SSA explain their rejections, recalibrate their standard, acknowledge that the formations we're reading hold knowledge the market doesn't value.

And we would lose. We would be blacklisted. We would sleep in smelter ruins and scavenge for food and wait for the SSA to let us back in.

Because there's always someone who needs the work more than they need the principle. Always someone who'll cross the line.

And the SSA knows it.

I stand up and walk out into the cold.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo descended into the Creighton Deep Extension with a verification contract from an off-world construction firm. They wanted specific readings confirmed before purchase.

She knew the placement she was verifying. It was in her database. Lena Okeke's reading. Rejected for non-standard lithology.

The cage descended. Pressure equalization stops every few levels — the lurch in your stomach, the pop in your ears, the way your chest tightens briefly before the pressure stabilizes. She'd felt it many times when she was a working reader. Muscle memory.

At depth the cage stopped and she stepped out into the drift. Her helmet lamp cut through the dark. The air was cold and wet and tasted like metal. She walked carefully — the floor was uneven, the ceiling low. Her shoulders brushed the walls.

She found the formation face where the placement had been made. The contact pad marks were still visible — small depressions in the stone where the sensor had pressed, the faint circular outline where the pad's edge had sat.

She pulled off her glove and touched the face bare-handed. The texture was what she expected: smooth dark bands, slightly rougher light bands. High metamorphic grade. Good banding. She ran her fingers along the contact pad marks, feeling where the sensor had been.

She placed her own sensor. The contact pad settled into the depressions Lena's sensor had made. She waited.

The resonance started immediately. Clean. Steady. That hum you feel in your palms, in your chest. The placement sang.

She checked the readout. Frequency within parameters. Signal stability excellent. She let it run the full duration, watching the resonance hold steady.

Then she checked the lithology scan. Proterozoic metamorphic. Sulfide concentration: below threshold.

Low sulfide. Below the baseline requirement.

But the signal was perfect.

She uploaded the reading to her private database, not to the SSA system. Then she moved to the next verification point.

Hours later she surfaced and walked to the SSA regional office. Marcus Chen was at his desk, reviewing applications on a triple-monitor setup. He looked up when she entered.

"Dr. Okonkwo. What can I do for you?"

She sat down across from him. They had worked together for years before she quit. He had been her supervisor. She knew his tells — the way he leaned back when he was about to refuse something, the way his jaw tightened when he was annoyed.

"I'm verifying readings for an off-world client. Multiple placements. All were rejected by SSA certification."

"And?"

"And all should have been certified. Clean signals, stable resonance, frequency within parameters."

Marcus leaned back. There it was. "Dr. Okonkwo, we've had this conversation before. The standard is the standard. If a reading doesn't meet the lithological criteria—"

"The lithological criteria are arbitrary."

"They're based on the Geological Survey baseline—"

"Which was calibrated for nickel extraction, not planetary formation data." Her voice stayed level. Professional. "Marcus, you know this. You know the Archean formations don't certify because they have low sulfide concentrations. You know the standard is biased."

"The standard is *functional*. It allows buyers to compare readings, prevents fraud, maintains database consistency—"

"It also discards most Archean formation data. It discards Proterozoic formations with stable signals and clean resonance because they don't have enough sulfide. It's rejecting valid geological knowledge because the baseline is asking the wrong question."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. His jaw tightened. "What do you want me to do, Amara? I don't set the standard. The SSA board sets the standard. And the board isn't going to change it because changing it would cost millions and invalidate decades of data."

"So we just keep discarding the oldest formations on the planet?"

"We certify what the standard allows us to certify."

"And the readers? The ones placing sensors in the deep, learning to feel the difference between formations that sing and formations that fight, building knowledge the standard can't recognize? What happens to them?"

Marcus's expression didn't change. "They learn to follow the standard or they find other work."

Amara stood. "I have rejected readings in my database, Marcus. Many of them. All scientifically valid. All discarded because the standard is asking the wrong question."

"Then publish your database. Submit it to a journal. Get an alternative certification system approved by the Planetary Geology Council."

"Nobody buys uncertified readings."

"Then I don't know what to tell you." Marcus turned back to his monitors. "The system works, Amara. Maybe it's not perfect, but it works. And it pays my salary and it feeds my family and it keeps the market stable. That's enough."

Amara walked out.

She stood in the parking lot for a while, looking out over the Basin. The sun was low. The crater floor was in shadow. Somewhere down there, deep underground, readers were placing sensors against formation faces. Learning which formations certified and which didn't. Learning to follow the standard instead of following the stone.

And the knowledge they were building — the feel of formations that sang, the texture of high-grade metamorphic banding, the resonance that traveled through the contact pad into your chest — that knowledge had no market value. The SSA wouldn't certify it. The buyers wouldn't purchase it. The standard couldn't recognize it.

But it was still real.

She got in her car and drove home.

I'm in the deep when my sensor dies.

Late in my shift. I've been down here for hours and my body is done — knees, back, shoulders, everything aches. More placements to go before I can surface.

I press the sensor against the formation face and wait. Nothing. No hum. No vibration. The screen is black.

I pull it back, check the power supply. Dead. The battery indicator shows zero.

I carry a spare battery. Always. I pull it from my pack, swap it in, reboot the sensor. The screen flickers, shows the startup sequence, goes black again.

Hardware failure.

I sit back against the drift wall and close my eyes. A new sensor rig costs more than I have. More than I can borrow. The credit cooperative already holds my debt. I already owe them from last winter when I couldn't make quota for multiple weeks and the rent came due anyway.

I could ask Chika. She has savings. Not much, but some. But she's already lent money to Obi and Ngozi and she's not getting it back. I can't ask her for more.

I could go to Jin. He has equipment. Maybe he'd rent me a rig until I can afford my own. But rental fees are high and they'd eat into my gross and I'm already barely breaking even.

Or I could go to the SSA office and ask Marcus Chen why half my placements get rejected when the resonance logs show clean signals. I could ask him to explain "non-standard lithology" in language that means something. I could ask him why the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction when we're reading planetary formation data.

But I already know what he'll say.

*The system works. The standard is the standard. If you want to read formations for love, go work for a university.*

I stand up, pack the dead sensor, and start the long walk back to the cage. My helmet lamp flickers. The battery is dying too. I walk faster, my hand on the drift wall to guide me. The texture changes under my fingers — rough here, smooth there, the banding visible even in the dark through touch alone.

The stone is still speaking. I just can't afford to listen anymore.

The SSA regional office is on the crater rim, a low concrete building with triple-glazed windows that look out over the Basin. I've never been inside. I stand in the lobby for a long time before I work up the nerve to approach the desk.

The receptionist looks up. "Can I help you?"

"I need to speak to someone about certification rejections."

"Do you have an appointment?"

"No. But I need to speak to Marcus Chen. It's about specific placements that were rejected even though they met all the signal requirements."

"Mr. Chen doesn't meet with readers without appointments."

"I've submitted dispute forms. They were all denied. I just need someone to explain why placements with clean signals are being rejected for 'non-standard lithology.' I've read the guidelines. I don't understand what I'm doing wrong."

The receptionist's expression doesn't change. "If you'd like to schedule an appointment—"

"How long is the wait?"

She checks her screen. "Currently about a month and a half."

A month and a half. I can't work for a month and a half. My sensor is broken and I can't afford to replace it and I need to understand why my readings keep getting rejected so I can—

So I can what? Learn to follow the standard? Learn which formations certify and which don't? Learn to place my sensors where the standard wants them instead of where the stone wants them?

"I can't wait that long," I say. "I need to work. My sensor is broken and I need to understand what I'm doing wrong so I can—"

"Ma'am." Her voice hardens. "If you continue to disrupt the office, I'll have to call security."

I stand there. The words won't come. I can feel other people in the lobby staring. A man in a suit. A woman with a tablet. They're looking at me like I'm the problem. Like I'm the disruption.

"I just need to understand," I say quietly.

"Please leave," the receptionist says.

I turn and walk out.

I stand in the parking lot for a long time. The sun is setting over the Basin. The crater floor is gold in the last light. Somewhere down there, deep underground, readers are placing sensors against formation faces. Some placements are singing. Some are fighting. Some will certify. Most won't.

And I can't afford to be part of that anymore.

I walk to the bus stop and wait for the transport back to the prefabs.

I open my email and compose a message to the SSA regional office.

*To whom it may concern,*

*I am writing to request clarification on the rejection of multiple placements. All readings met signal quality requirements (stable resonance, frequency within standard parameters) but were rejected for "insufficient clarity" or "non-standard lithology."*

But I need to have tried everything. I need to be able to say I asked. I need to know that when I stop trying, it's because there's nothing left to try, not because I gave up too soon.

And the stone will keep holding its memory, whether anyone listens or not.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo sat in her office and stared at her database. Another rejected reading added. Another valid signal discarded by the SSA system. Lena Okeke's name appeared multiple times now. Multiple rejections. All scientifically valid. All discarded.

She could keep doing this. Keep documenting. Keep sending emails to academics who never responded. Keep building an archive that nobody would ever use.

Or she could do something different.

She opened her email. Not to Dr. Chen this time. Not to the university. Not to anyone who could change the system, because nobody could change the system. The SSA board wouldn't recalibrate. The market wouldn't buy uncertified readings. The standard would keep discarding Archean formations and Proterozoic formations and anything else with low sulfide concentration.

But the knowledge didn't have to be completely lost.

She searched the SSA rejection database for recent entries from the Creighton Deep Extension. Found Lena Okeke's contact information in the reader registry.
