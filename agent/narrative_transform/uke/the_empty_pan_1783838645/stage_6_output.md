# STRATEGY BRIEF

**IMPLIED AUTHOR:**
A sensibility that notices texture before theory — the way cold finds you through good thermals, the difference between formations that sing and formations that fight, the specific weight of a sensor rig against Archean stone. This author trusts physical knowledge (hands knowing the rig's weight, the hum you feel through gloves) over abstract knowledge, and treats bureaucratic language ("non-standard lithology") as opacity to be felt rather than decoded. The register lives in the body: crouching in drifts, knees screaming, the lurch in your stomach during pressure equalization. When meaning arrives, it arrives through sensation first — the stone holding memory whether anyone listens, not because it's poetic but because that's what the contact pad teaches you.

**AUDIENCE:**
Readers who recognize that technical systems encode political choices, and who can hold two truths simultaneously: that individual competence doesn't overcome structural constraint, and that competence still matters. This audience doesn't need the SSA explained as metaphor (they'll see extraction logic, credentialism, the gap between use-value and exchange-value) but they need the stone to be *actually stone* — geologically specific, physically present, not just standing in for authentic labor. They can tolerate an ending that doesn't resolve the system because they understand transmission-outside-markets as a real form of survival, not consolation.

**SCOPE:**
Target range: 8,000–10,000 words. The current 4,360-word draft has the right structural beats but needs expansion in three places: (1) the sensory grammar of reading formations (what makes a placement sing vs. fight needs more physical vocabulary before the SSA rejections land as loss), (2) the canteen arguments (the organizing debate in Section 5 is doing heavy systemic work in compressed space — it needs room to breathe so the failure to organize registers as chosen impossibility rather than narrative necessity), (3) Dr. Okonkwo's verification descent (Section 6 is too thin — her hands-on confirmation that Lena's rejected readings were valid needs the same physical attention Lena's placements get, so the alternative knowledge-practice feels embodied, not just ideological). This is a short story, not a novella — the scope should stay inside the Creighton Deep Extension and the immediate SSA system, with off-world construction firms and Planetary Geology Council as background texture, not plot.

**CREATIVE DEEPENING:**
The story's latent strength is the difference between *reading the stone* and *reading for the standard* — two different forms of attention that produce different knowledge. Right now that difference is stated (Jin's "you place your sensors where the standard wants them, not where the stone wants them") but not fully dramatized. The deepening available: show Lena learning to read *against* the standard before she knows that's what she's doing. Early placements where she notices something (a formation face that should sing but doesn't, or one that sings in a register the sensor can't capture) and dismisses it because it's not in the guidelines. Then the SSA rejections force her to revisit those moments — not as "I was right all along" but as "I was hearing something I didn't have language for." The story should end with her beginning to build that language, not having already built it. Dr. Okonkwo's offer is the offer of a grammar, not a solution.

**FLINCH POINTS:**

1. **Section 5, the organizing debate**: The draft has Ngozi shut down the possibility of collective action with her blacklisting story, and the table goes quiet, and that's the end of it. What the draft is doing: converting structural constraint into individual trauma (Ngozi's half-year sleeping in smelter ruins) so the failure to organize feels like wisdom rather than defeat. What honesty requires: let someone push back. Not to win the argument, but to make visible that the choice to not organize is still a *choice*, even when it's the only survivable choice. The draft needs the pressure of "but if we all—" to last longer, to force the characters to articulate why they won't, so the reader feels the cost of that won't.

2. **Section 8, Lena at the SSA office**: The receptionist threatens to call security and Lena leaves. What the draft is doing: treating institutional refusal as a wall Lena bounces off, which makes her subsequent email (the polite request for clarification) read as dignity rather than what it might actually be — the last move available to someone who's already lost. What honesty requires: let Lena know, in the moment, that the email won't work. Let her send it anyway, not because she believes it will work, but because she needs to have tried everything before she can stop trying. The draft is protecting her from that knowledge; the story needs to let her have it.

3. **Section 9, Dr. Okonkwo's email to Lena**: The draft frames this as small-scale transmission, knowledge finding a route outside the market. What the draft is doing: offering teaching-as-resolution, which converts the SSA's structural failure into an opportunity for mentorship. What honesty requires: Dr. Okonkwo should know this doesn't fix anything. The email should carry that knowledge. Not despair — she's still sending it, she still believes transmission matters — but clarity about what it can't do. The draft's version ("It wouldn't change the system. Not today. Maybe not ever.") is close, but it arrives as narrator commentary rather than something Dr. Okonkwo is feeling in her hands as she writes. Put that knowledge in the email itself, in what she offers and what she doesn't offer.

**PATTERN CHECK:**
Concern. The story defaults to quiet resistance (Lena keeps placing sensors, Dr. Okonkwo keeps documenting, the knowledge transmits outside the market) and treats that as the honest ending. It might be — this material might genuinely demand that register. But the rejection-accumulation pattern (the counting rhythm the review flagged) suggests the draft is using numerical escalation to create inevitability, which is the pipeline's way of avoiding dramatized breaking points. The strategy directs the craft passes: convert at least one rejection from count to crisis. Not "rejection rate over half" but a specific placement — one Lena knows is good, one she's checked multiple times, one that sings so clearly she can't dismiss it — and have that specific rejection be what breaks her sensor or her credit line or her ability to keep believing the system is correctable. Let the story earn its quiet ending by first paying the cost of a loud breaking point.
