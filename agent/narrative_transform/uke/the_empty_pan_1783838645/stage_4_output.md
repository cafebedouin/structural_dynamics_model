# The Grain

## 1.

The sensor settles against the formation face and I wait. My hands know this part — the weight of the rig against stone that's older than oxygen, the way the contact pad finds purchase in the metamorphic banding. Forty minutes until the resonance stabilizes. Forty minutes crouched in a drift that's barely high enough to kneel in, helmet scraping the ceiling every time I shift my weight.

The cold comes up through my boots. Always does, this deep. Four point two kilometers down, the Creighton Deep Extension, and the thermal management only brings it up to eight degrees. My thermals are good — I saved three months for them — but eight hours in eight degrees and the cold finds you anyway.

I check the sensor readout. Resonance building. Clean so far. This is Archean formation, two point seven billion years old, and when it's good it's *good* — the kind of reading that certifies at 180 credits if the SSA doesn't reject it. If.

The formation face in front of me is beautiful. I'm not supposed to notice that — I'm supposed to place the sensor and wait and upload and move to the next placement — but I notice anyway. The banding is so clear here: dark layers and light layers, the record of pressure and heat reorganizing the crystal structure over a billion years. The dark bands are smoother. Higher metamorphic grade. More pressure. The stone remembers.

My helmet lamp flickers. Battery at forty percent. I've got four more placements after this one, and if the lamp dies before I surface I'll have to navigate by touch. I've done it before. The shaft has a rhythm — the cage stops, the drift branches, the formation changes texture under your hands. But I'd rather not.

The sensor hums. Not a sound you hear — a vibration you feel through the contact pad, through your gloves, into your palms. When it's right, when the placement *sings*, the hum is steady. No flutter. No skip. Just the stone speaking and the sensor listening.

This one sings.

I wait. Thirty-five minutes now. Five more and I can upload.

My knees ache. The drift floor is rough — blasted rock, never smoothed — and kneeling on it for forty minutes means you stand up slow. Last week I tried sitting, back against the wall, sensor braced between my boots, but the angle was wrong and the resonance never stabilized. You have to hold it. Your body has to be part of the circuit.

The hum stays steady.

Forty minutes. I tap the upload sequence on the sensor screen. The reading compresses, encrypts, travels up the fiber-optic tether to the surface station. Ten seconds. Twenty. The confirmation blinks green.

Placement CR-4521. Submitted for certification.

I stand up slow, wait for the blood to come back into my legs, and move to the next face.

---

The canteen is crowded when I surface. Sixteen hundred hours, end of first shift, and everyone's here. The smell hits me before I'm through the door — fry oil and protein bars and bodies that have been underground for eight hours. I find a seat at the long table near the back.

Chika's already there, eating rice and stew. She looks up when I sit.

"How many?" she asks.

"Six placements. Three good, two maybe, one I know they'll reject."

"Why?"

"Formation face was contaminated. Modern drilling, maybe ten years old. The sulfide bands were cut through." I pull out my ration card, slide it across the counter to Bayo. He swipes it and hands back a bowl of stew and a hunk of bread. Twenty credits.

Chika makes a sound in her throat. "You placed it anyway?"

"I placed it anyway. Sensor settled. Resonance was clean. Maybe they don't notice."

"They always notice."

She's right. They always notice. But you place it anyway because the shift quota is six placements minimum and if you don't make quota you don't get the shaft access refund. Eight credits. It matters.

I eat. The stew is thin but it's hot and my hands are still cold from the deep. Chika finishes her rice and leans back.

"You check your notices yet?"

"Not yet."

"I got three rejections this morning. Three. One of them I *know* was clean — forty-two minutes stable, Proterozoic banding, no contamination. 'Insufficient clarity.' That's all it said."

I've heard this before. We all have. The SSA rejection notices don't explain. They just state: *Insufficient clarity. Non-standard lithology. Contaminated by modern drilling.* You're supposed to know what it means. But we don't.

"You dispute it?" I ask.

"What's the point? Dispute costs five credits and they never reverse."

She's right about that too. I disputed one last month — a reading I *knew* was good, forty minutes stable, deep Archean, clean signal — and they sent back the same notice with one additional line: *Rejection upheld. No further correspondence.* Five credits gone.

I finish the stew and check the terminal near the door. My credit balance: 340. Rent is due in three days: 180. Food for the week: 120. That leaves 40 for everything else.

The screen blinks. New message. I open it.

*Rejection Notice: Placement CR-4477*
*Reason: Insufficient clarity*
*Certification fee: 18 credits (non-refundable)*

CR-4477. That was two days ago. Proterozoic formation, clean placement, twenty-eight minutes stable. I checked the log three times before I uploaded. The resonance was *clean*.

I close the message and walk out.

---

## 2.

The prefab is cold when I get back. I turn on the heater — it'll take an hour to bring the temperature up to fifteen degrees — and sit on the bed. Twelve square meters. Bed, table, chair, sink. The bathroom is shared, down the hall, four units to one bathroom.

I pull out my sensor rig and check the calibration. The readout is green. Calibration is good. I run a diagnostic on the contact pad. No faults. I check the resonance log from CR-4477. Twenty-eight minutes, stable signal, frequency within standard parameters.

I don't understand.

I open the SSA guidelines on my tablet. *Certification Requirements for Stratigraphic Readings, Section 4.2: Signal Clarity.* The language is dense — "resonance stability within standard deviation," "frequency band compliance," "lithological consistency" — but the numbers are clear. CR-4477 met every requirement.

I could dispute it. Five credits. But Chika's right — they never reverse.

I put the tablet down and lie back on the bed. The heater hums. My body aches — knees, back, shoulders. Eight hours crouched in drifts. Tomorrow I'll do it again.

I close my eyes and see the formation face from this morning. The Archean banding, dark and light, the stone holding two point seven billion years. The sensor settled so clean. The hum was steady. The stone was speaking.

And the SSA said: *Insufficient clarity.*

I don't understand.

---

## 3.

Three days later I'm back in the deep. Placement seven of ten. My knees are already screaming and I've got three more to go.

This one is fighting.

I've had the sensor on the formation face for twenty minutes and the resonance won't stabilize. The hum skips, flutters, drops out. I adjust the contact pad angle. Nothing. I move it three centimeters left, try again. The hum comes back, holds for ten seconds, drops out.

The formation face looks clean. Proterozoic, metamorphic banding, no obvious contamination. But something is wrong. The sensor can't find the grain.

I try again. Thirty minutes now. The hum holds for fifteen seconds, drops out. I'm wasting time. I've got three more placements and two hours left in the shift and if I don't make quota I lose the access refund.

I pull the sensor, move to the next face.

This one sings.

The contact pad settles and the hum is immediate, steady, clean. I don't understand why. The formation looks the same — same lithology, same age, same metamorphic grade. But this face sings and the last one fought.

I wait forty minutes, upload the reading, move on.

---

End of shift. I surface at sixteen hundred and check my messages.

Five placements submitted. Two rejections already.

*Placement CR-4598: Insufficient clarity*
*Placement CR-4603: Non-standard lithology*

I open the logs. CR-4598 was the one that fought — thirty minutes unstable, I knew it wouldn't certify. But CR-4603 was *clean*. Forty-one minutes stable, deep Proterozoic, no contamination. I checked it twice before I uploaded.

*Non-standard lithology.*

I don't know what that means.

I walk to the canteen. Chika's there, and Jin. Jin is older, maybe forty, and he's been reading formations for nine years. He owns his equipment — no rental fees — and he gets consulting work sometimes, off-world firms that want readings verified before purchase. He knows more than the rest of us.

I sit down next to him.

"Jin, what's 'non-standard lithology'?"

He looks up from his rice. "Where'd you get that?"

"Rejection notice. CR-4603. Proterozoic formation, clean signal, forty-one minutes stable."

He frowns. "Proterozoic should certify. What was the sulfide concentration?"

"I don't know. The sensor doesn't measure that."

"Exactly." He puts down his fork. "The standard measures sulfide concentration. If your formation doesn't have enough sulfide — even if the signal is clean, even if the reading is good — it gets rejected."

"Why?"

"Because the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction. High sulfide means high nickel. Low sulfide means waste rock. The SSA still uses the same calibration."

I stare at him. "But we're not mining nickel. We're reading formations."

"I know."

"So why—"

"Because changing the standard would invalidate eighteen years of certified readings. Every database, every archive, every off-world construction plan built on SSA data would need recalibration. The SSA isn't going to do that."

I sit back. My hands are shaking. I don't know if it's anger or cold or exhaustion.

"So what do we do?"

Jin shrugs. "You learn which formations certify and which don't. You avoid the low-sulfide faces. You place your sensors where the standard wants them, not where the stone wants them."

"That's not reading formations. That's—"

"That's how you make quota." He picks up his fork. "You want to read formations for love, go work for a university. You want to eat, you follow the standard."

## 4.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo opened the rejection notice and added it to her database.

*Placement CR-4603. Reader: Lena Okeke. Formation: Proterozoic, Creighton Deep Extension. Rejection reason: Non-standard lithology. Signal: 41 minutes stable, frequency within parameters.*

Entry 1,847.

She saved the file and closed the laptop. Her office was small — a converted bedroom in her house on the Sudbury rim — but the window looked out over the Basin. From here she could see the crater floor, the flooded shafts, the ruins of the old smelters. Two point seven billion years of planetary memory, and the SSA certified less than thirty percent of it.

She had worked for the SSA for six years. Certification officer, regional division, responsible for reviewing applications and maintaining the calibration database. She had been good at it. She had believed in it.

Then she started noticing the pattern.

Archean formations — the oldest, the most geologically significant — had the highest rejection rate. Sixty-two percent. The rejection reason was always the same: *Non-standard lithology.* But when she checked the resonance logs, the signals were clean. Stable. Within parameters.

She asked her supervisor about it. He said the standard was calibrated for nickel-bearing formations. Archean formations had different sulfide structures. Lower concentrations. The standard couldn't certify them accurately.

She asked why they didn't recalibrate the standard.

He said it would cost too much. The entire system would need updating. Every sensor, every database, every buyer agreement. The SSA's budget didn't allow for it.

She asked what happened to the rejected readings.

He said they were discarded.

Two point seven billion years of planetary memory, discarded because the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction in 1883.

She quit three months later.

That was three years ago. Since then she had been building her private database: every rejected reading she could verify as scientifically valid. Every formation the SSA had discarded. Every piece of planetary memory the standard had missed.

1,847 readings.

She opened her email and composed a message to Dr. Sarah Chen, Planetary Geology Department, University of Toronto.

*Dr. Chen,*

*I am writing to follow up on my previous correspondence regarding alternative stratigraphic certification standards. I have now documented 1,847 rejected SSA readings that meet all signal quality parameters but fail due to lithological bias in the 1883 baseline calibration.*

*These readings represent significant planetary formation data, particularly from Archean-era formations (2.5-4.0 Ga), which are underrepresented in current databases due to their low sulfide concentrations.*

*I have attached a summary analysis and sample data. I believe this material could form the basis for an alternative certification system optimized for planetary geology rather than extraction economics.*

*I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this further.*

*Sincerely,*
*Dr. Amara Okonkwo*

She hit send and closed the laptop.

Dr. Chen would not respond. She knew this. Dr. Chen had not responded to the previous four emails. No one bought uncertified readings. The SSA system was entrenched. The market ran on certified data, and certified data meant SSA data, and SSA data meant the 1883 baseline.

But she kept sending the emails anyway.

She stood and walked to the window. The sun was setting over the Basin, and the light turned the crater floor gold. Somewhere down there, four kilometers deep, strat-readers were placing sensors against formation faces. Some placements would sing. Some would fight. Some would certify. Most would be rejected.

And the stone would keep holding its memory, whether anyone listened or not.

---

## 5.

I'm three weeks into the worst month I've ever had. Rejection rate: fifty-three percent. Last week I netted 340 credits. Rent is 180. Food is 120. I have 40 credits left and I owe the credit cooperative 800.

I'm sitting in the canteen with Chika and two other readers, Obi and Ngozi. We're all in the same situation. High rejection rates, low net income, debt accumulating. Obi borrowed 600 credits last month to fix his sensor rig. Ngozi borrowed 400 to cover medical after she fell in a drift and broke her wrist.

"We should go to the SSA office," Chika says. "All of us. Demand they explain the rejection criteria."

Obi shakes his head. "They won't tell us. You know they won't."

"Then we refuse to work until they do."

"And then what?" Ngozi's voice is sharp. "We don't work, we don't eat. The SSA doesn't care if we strike. There's fifty readers in Creighton alone. They'll just hire new ones."

Chika leans back, frustrated. "So we do nothing?"

"We do what Jin said. We learn which formations certify and we avoid the ones that don't."

"That's not reading formations," I say quietly. "That's following a map someone else drew."

They all look at me.

"The stone is *there*," I continue. "The memory is *there*. The Archean formations, the Proterozoic, all of it. And we're not reading it. We're reading the parts the SSA wants us to read."

"So?" Obi's voice is tired. "The SSA pays for the parts they want. We're not geologists. We're workers."

"But the stone—"

"The stone doesn't pay rent."

I stop. He's right. The stone doesn't pay rent. The stone doesn't care if I net 60 credits or 180. The stone has been here for two point seven billion years and it will be here long after I'm gone.

But I care.

I care because when I place the sensor and the resonance stabilizes and the hum is clean, I'm touching something that's older than oxygen. I'm listening to the planet's memory. And when the SSA rejects it — when they say *insufficient clarity* or *non-standard lithology* — they're not rejecting my work. They're rejecting the stone's voice.

And I don't know how to stop caring about that.

## 6.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo descended into the Creighton Deep Extension with a verification contract from an off-world construction firm. They wanted three specific readings confirmed before purchase: CR-4521, CR-4598, CR-4603.

She knew CR-4603. It was in her database. Lena Okeke's reading. Rejected for non-standard lithology.

The cage descended for forty minutes. Three pressure equalization stops. At 2.8 kilometers the cable tension shifted — she felt it in her stomach, the same lurch she'd felt a thousand times when she was a working reader. Muscle memory.

At 4.2 kilometers the cage stopped and she stepped out into the drift. Her helmet lamp cut through the dark. The air was cold and wet and tasted like metal.

She found the formation face where CR-4603 had been placed. The contact pad marks were still visible — four small depressions in the stone where the sensor had pressed.

She placed her own sensor. Waited.

The resonance stabilized in eighteen minutes. Clean signal. Frequency within parameters. She checked the lithology: Proterozoic metamorphic, sulfide concentration 0.8%.

Low sulfide. Below the 1883 baseline threshold of 1.5%.

But the signal was perfect.

She uploaded the reading and moved to the next verification point.

---

Three hours later she surfaced and walked to the SSA regional office. Marcus Chen was at his desk, reviewing applications on a triple-monitor setup. He looked up when she entered.

"Dr. Okonkwo. What can I do for you?"

She sat down across from him. They had worked together for four years before she quit. He had been her supervisor.

"I'm verifying readings for an off-world client. CR-4521, CR-4598, CR-4603. All three were rejected by SSA certification."

"And?"

"And all three should have been certified. Clean signals, stable resonance, frequency within parameters."

Marcus leaned back. "Dr. Okonkwo, we've had this conversation before. The standard is the standard. If a reading doesn't meet the lithological criteria—"

"The lithological criteria are arbitrary."

"They're based on the 1883 Geological Survey baseline—"

"Which was calibrated for nickel extraction, not planetary formation data." Her voice stayed level. Professional. "Marcus, you know this. You know the Archean formations don't certify because they have low sulfide concentrations. You know the standard is biased."

"The standard is *functional*. It allows buyers to compare readings, prevents fraud, maintains database consistency—"

"It also discards sixty-two percent of Archean formation data."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "What do you want me to do, Amara? I don't set the standard. The SSA board sets the standard. And the board isn't going to change it because changing it would cost millions and invalidate eighteen years of data."

"So we just keep discarding the oldest formations on the planet?"

"We certify what the standard allows us to certify."

Amara stood. "I have 1,847 rejected readings in my database, Marcus. All scientifically valid. All discarded because the standard is asking the wrong question."

"Then publish your database. Submit it to a journal. Get an alternative certification system approved by the Planetary Geology Council."

"No one buys uncertified readings."

"Then I don't know what to tell you." Marcus turned back to his monitors. "The system works, Amara. Maybe it's not perfect, but it works. And it pays my salary and it feeds my family and it keeps the market stable. That's enough."

Amara walked out.

## 7.

I'm in the deep when my sensor dies.

Placement nine of ten. I've been down here for seven hours and my body is done — knees, back, shoulders, everything aches. One more placement and I can surface.

I press the sensor against the formation face and wait. Nothing. No hum. No vibration. The screen is black.

I pull it back, check the power supply. Dead. The battery indicator shows zero.

I carry a spare battery. Always. I pull it from my pack, swap it in, reboot the sensor. The screen flickers, shows the startup sequence, goes black again.

Hardware failure.

I sit back against the drift wall and close my eyes. A new sensor rig costs 1,200 credits. I have 40 credits in my account. The credit cooperative will lend me 800, maybe, if I can prove I'll pay it back. But I already owe them 800 from last winter.

I could ask Chika. She has savings. Not much, but some. But she's already lent money to Obi and Ngozi and she's not getting it back.

I could go to Jin. He has equipment. Maybe he'd rent me a rig until I can afford my own.

Or I could go to the SSA office and ask Marcus Chen why fifty-three percent of my placements get rejected when the resonance logs show clean signals. I could ask him to explain "non-standard lithology." I could ask him why the standard was calibrated for nickel extraction when we're reading planetary formation data.

But I already know what he'll say.

*The system works. The standard is the standard. If you want to read formations for love, go work for a university.*

I stand up, pack the dead sensor, and start the long walk back to the cage.

---

## 8.

The SSA regional office is on the crater rim, a low concrete building with triple-glazed windows that look out over the Basin. I've never been inside. I stand in the lobby for five minutes before I work up the nerve to approach the desk.

The receptionist looks up. "Can I help you?"

"I need to speak to someone about certification rejections."

"Do you have an appointment?"

"No."

"Then you'll need to submit a dispute form. There's a terminal over there." She points to a kiosk near the door.

"I've submitted three dispute forms. They were all denied."

"Then I'm sorry, but there's nothing else we can do."

"I just need five minutes. I just need someone to explain—"

"Ma'am, if you don't have an appointment, I can't help you."

I stand there for another ten seconds. Then I turn and walk out.

---

That night I sit in my prefab and open my tablet. I pull up the SSA guidelines, the certification requirements, the lithological criteria. I read them three times. I still don't understand.

I open my email and compose a message to the SSA regional office.

*To whom it may concern,*

*I am writing to request clarification on the rejection of placements CR-4477, CR-4598, and CR-4603. All three readings met signal quality requirements (stable resonance, frequency within standard parameters) but were rejected for "insufficient clarity" or "non-standard lithology."*

*I have reviewed the SSA guidelines and I do not understand why these readings failed certification. I am requesting access to the specific rejection criteria so that I can improve my placement technique.*

*Sincerely,*
*Lena Okeke*

I hit send and close the tablet.

I know they won't respond. Or if they do, it will be a form letter: *The standard is the standard. Rejection criteria are proprietary. No further correspondence.*

But I send it anyway.

I lie back on the bed and close my eyes. Tomorrow I'll borrow a sensor rig from Jin. I'll descend into the deep. I'll place sensors against formation faces and wait for resonance and upload readings and hope they certify.

And the stone will keep holding its memory, whether anyone listens or not.

## 9.

Dr. Amara Okonkwo opened her laptop and checked her email. No response from Dr. Chen. No response from the University of Toronto. No response from the Planetary Geology Council.

She opened her database and added entry 1,848.

*Placement CR-4612. Reader: Lena Okeke. Formation: Archean, Creighton Deep Extension. Rejection reason: Non-standard lithology. Signal: 39 minutes stable, frequency within parameters.*

She saved the file and stared at the number. 1,848.

How many readings would it take? How much evidence would she need before someone listened?

She didn't know.

But she kept adding them anyway.

Because somewhere in the deep, four kilometers down, Lena Okeke was placing sensors against stone that was older than oxygen. And the stone was speaking. And the SSA was saying: *Non-standard lithology.*

And maybe — maybe — if Amara kept documenting, kept building the database, kept sending emails that no one answered — maybe someday someone would listen.

Maybe someday the system would change.

But not today.

She closed the laptop and walked to the window. The sun was setting over the Basin. The crater floor glowed gold in the last light.

And the stone kept holding its memory, whether anyone listened or not.
