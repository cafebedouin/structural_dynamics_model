# THE PUMP-ROOM HOUR

## I.

The Rittmeister's lady was, one understood, much occupied this season with her correspondence. Nobody asked with whom.

Klára knew the schedule the way she knew the shape of her own hands, without ever having to look at it directly. Seven o'clock, the waters. Eleven, the promenade beneath the colonnade's iron lace, her arm through Anton's, her face arranged to the particular warmth that greeted the Baroness Fischer's cool nod and the young Hoffmanns' eager one, calibrated separately, correctly, every time. One o'clock, calling hours, the parlor of whoever's turn it was to receive. Eight, dinner, seated by rank, which meant seated beside Anton, always, twelve years running.

None of this required her attention. It required only her body, which had learned it the way a hand learns a hymn on the keys — the fingers go without the mind's help once the mind has stopped mattering to the outcome.

What required her attention was nine o'clock.

She could feel it coming from as far away as eight-forty, a kind of lightening under the sternum, not unlike hunger, not unlike relief. At the pump-room the light through the glass roof went a particular color toward evening — a blue with iron in it, the color of the mineral water itself held up to a lamp — and she had learned to read that blue the way other women read a clock face, though she never looked at a clock to confirm it. She did not need confirming. Her body kept the hour better than any clock in the establishment, better than the pump-room's own, which the attendants set each morning by the physician's watch and which ran, everyone agreed, three minutes fast.

At nine, in her own room, with the door shut and Anton settled in the smoking room below with his brandy and Colonel Bittner's tedious opinions on the Balkan question, she crossed to the writing-desk, opened the lower drawer, and touched the latch of the small rosewood box inside it.

It was not locked. It had never needed to be locked. Nobody in twelve years had thought to look inside a box that sat, unremarkably, among stockings and ribbon and a broken fan she kept meaning to mend.

The latch was brass, gone a little green at the hinge, cold always, even in August, even after a day when the whole town had sweated through its linen at the eleven o'clock promenade. She touched it with two fingers. She did not open the box. She had not opened the box in years — there was no longer any need; she knew what was inside without needing to see it, the way one knows the shape of a scar under a sleeve.

She held the cold of it for the length of one held breath and let it go.

That was the whole of it. There had never been more to it than that, and this was the thing she had never once tried to explain to anyone, not because it was complicated but because saying it aloud would have made it sound like more than it was, or like less — either way, a different thing than the true one.

Nine o'clock. The blue light. The cold latch. Twelve years, and before that, seven years of not yet being married, in which the discipline had already been old.

She did not think his name. She had stopped needing to.

---

The Rittmeister Anton Vesely considered himself, on the whole, a fortunate man, and saw no reason his friends should think otherwise.

He had said as much to Oberst Řehák only that morning, over the waters — a glass each, drunk standing, grimacing companionably at the bitterness, the two of them observing the eleven o'clock crowd assemble beneath the colonnade with the proprietary pleasure of men who had watched the same crowd assemble every August for a decade.

"My wife wants for nothing," he had said, not boastfully — he disliked boastfulness in a man, thought it common — but as a simple accounting, the kind a man might give of his stables or his standing with his bank. "She has her house, her name, her position. Twelve years, and I don't believe I've heard her complain of anything beyond the dressmaker's prices."

Řehák had laughed at that, the dressmaker being a subject of general grievance among the husbands that season, and the conversation had moved on to the question of whether young Hoffmann's engagement to the Petrov girl would go forward given the uncle's gambling debts, which was a much more interesting question, being one with an outcome still in doubt.

Anton had not thought of the remark again. There was no reason he should have. It was true, as far as he knew it to be true, and a man does not turn a true thing over and over looking for a false bottom in it. Klára managed the house without complaint, dressed well within what he provided, was universally allowed to be a handsome and gracious woman for her years, and had never once, in twelve years, given him cause to suppose she wished for anything he had not already given her.

He did privately calculate, that same morning, standing at the pump-room rail with his glass, that he was now eleven hundred gulden in arrears to his tailor and something worse than that to Reiner's apothecary for the various tinctures the physician had prescribed him against his gout, and that the coming winter would require an economy in Klára's household accounts that he had not yet found the words to raise with her. This calculation ran alongside the remark to Řehák in his mind, in the same quarter-hour, without either thought troubling the other. A man's ledgers were a man's own business. His wife's contentment was a separate account, and it stood, as far as he had ever had reason to audit it, comfortably in credit.

He did not think to ask her whether the account was hers to keep or his to check. The question did not arise, because asking it would have required first supposing there was a question — and nothing in twelve years had ever suggested to him that there was.

He finished his water, grimaced once more for form's sake, and went to find his hat. The published schedule, posted daily on the board outside the Colonnade in a hand so practiced it might have been printed, showed calling hours beginning at one. He liked to be early. It was, he had always felt, a mark of a man who knew his own standing that he need never hurry to occupy it.

## II.

The Season's difficulties, when they came that year, came in the ordinary way — a seating.

At the dinner given by the Baroness Fischer for her nephew's name-day, the Rittmeister Vesely found himself placed not at the Baroness's right hand, where twelve years of precedent had put him, but three places down, below the young district judge who had arrived only that season and whose father, it developed, had lately been made a councillor in Prague.

Anton said nothing at the table. A man did not remark on his own seating; to do so was to confess that the seating mattered, which was worse than the seating itself. He ate his soup, complimented the Baroness's cook, and laughed at the judge's mild joke about the water-cure with every appearance of a man entirely at ease.

But he did not sleep well that night, and the next morning at the pump-room he found himself standing a half-minute longer than usual before Dr. Reiner's little knot of morning callers, waiting to be noticed, waiting for the physician's nod that would tell the room — for the room was always watching Reiner's nods, that was the whole mechanism of it — that the Rittmeister Vesely remained a man of consequence whatever some Prague councillor's son might suppose.

Reiner gave him the nod eventually. Reiner was scrupulous about eventually giving everyone the nod, sooner or later, in an order he alone determined and never explained, and which everyone therefore studied like scripture. He believed himself, sincerely, to run the fairest waiting-room in the kingdom — a man's turn came according to the state of his health and the press of the morning, nothing more, he would have said, if anyone had been impertinent enough to ask him to say it. He did not notice that the district judge's father's new appointment had reached him by breakfast, nor that this was why the judge was seen that morning before the Rittmeister, nor that a dozen other such adjustments moved through his waiting-room every week, invisible to him as adjustments, visible to him only as the natural sorting of deserving cases.

Anton got his nod. He felt the fatigue of the last two days settle into his feet, which ached from an unusual quantity of standing correctly, and into his throat, worn from a fortnight of animated, careful talk. He attributed this fatigue, in the vague way a man attributes a headache to weather, to the general cost of maintaining a household and a name through a difficult season, and did not think to ask what it was costing anyone else. It did not occur to him that there was anyone else's cost to ask about. His wife had not been at the Baroness's dinner — a slight headache, she'd said, and stayed home — and if it crossed his mind at all that a headache on the night of that particular seating might have spared her a difficult evening, it did not cross far enough to become a thought he kept.

He noticed, on his way out through the colonnade, Frau Nemcová at the entrance to her pension-house across the square, receiving two elderly sisters from Brno who had just arrived by the morning post-coach, both trunks and both women fussing over a lost hatbox. Frau Nemcová's face wore, throughout the fuss, an expression of perfect, unhurried welcome — patient with the hatbox, solicitous of the journey's fatigues, already halfway to charming, by some alchemy of tone alone, two women who had arrived cross and travel-worn into two women who would tell their friends at home, come autumn, what a very superior establishment it had been.

Anton thought, passing, that the woman had a genuinely good nature, and that it was fortunate for her guests she did; some of these pension-house keepers were sour as vinegar, in his experience, and no one could blame a traveler for wishing to be received pleasantly. He did not wonder what it cost her to produce that welcome at the fortieth arrival of a season that had brought her, so far, perhaps two-thirds of the bookings she required to see her through to spring. He had never had to be liked in order to eat, and a man does not easily imagine a hunger he has never had reason to feel.

---

Klára had gone to the pension-house herself that same week, on an errand concerning a length of trimming Frau Nemcová's seamstress-cousin had promised to alter for her, and while she waited in the little front office for the alteration to be fetched, she watched Frau Nemcová at the booking desk receive a query from a gentleman about rooms for the following week.

There were, in fact, no good rooms left for the following week; Klára had gathered as much from an earlier remark. But Frau Nemcová's face, when the gentleman asked, went through no visible calculation at all — it arrived at warmth a half-beat before the question had even finished being asked, as though warmth were simply the room's temperature and had nothing to do with the man's question, and she said something about "seeing what might be managed" in a tone that promised nothing and reassured everything, and the gentleman went away pleased, having secured no actual commitment whatsoever.

It was a fine piece of work. Klára recognized it as such the way one musician recognizes another's fingering — not because she could have named the technique, but because she had a technique of her own, differently made, that ran on a similar principle: the face arrives before the feeling does, and no one downstream of the face can tell the difference, and there are mornings when you cannot tell the difference yourself anymore either.

Frau Nemcová caught her eye across the little room, once, briefly, while the gentleman was still gathering his hat and stick. Neither woman smiled at the other in that moment — the ordinary smile was, just then, otherwise engaged — and something passed between them that was not quite a communication, having no content that either could have stated, and was for that reason more accurate than most communications Klára could remember having. Then the gentleman said his good-day, and Frau Nemcová's warmth turned to him again, entire, undiminished, priced at whatever thirty more mornings of it would cost her before the baths closed and the town emptied and she could, if she chose, stop smiling until May.

The trimming was fetched. Klára paid what was owed and went home to dress for the one o'clock calls.

That night, at nine, the light in her room went the particular blue it went, and she crossed to the drawer and touched the cold brass, and this had nothing to do with Frau Nemcová at all, and everything else in her day had.

## III.

The Season's last fortnight ran, as it always did, a little faster than the ones before it — the same hours on the schedule, the same eleven o'clock promenade and eight o'clock dinner, but something in the light already thinning toward September so that everyone hurried through the fixed points as though hurrying might make there be more of them left.

Anton found himself, in that fortnight, unusually restless. The gout was better; Reiner had adjusted the waters and pronounced him improved, which pleased him disproportionately, as though the physician's good opinion of his liver were itself a kind of standing. The matter of the seating at the Baroness's had resolved itself in the ordinary way — a return dinner of his own, carefully hosted, at which the young judge was seated correctly below him, restored the visible order of things, and Anton felt the fatigue of the last weeks begin, at last, to lift.

It was on one of the last evenings, a Tuesday, unseasonably cool, that he came up to Klára's room later than his custom — Bittner's Balkan opinions having run long over the brandy — and found her door not quite latched, a thread of candlelight along its edge.

He had not meant to observe anything. He had meant only to say goodnight, the two of them having kept largely separate rooms these twelve years by the ordinary arrangement of people married a long time and comfortable in it. But the door gave a little under his hand, and there she was at the writing-desk with the lower drawer open, and her two fingers resting on something small and brass that he had genuinely, in twelve years, never once seen.

"Klára," he said. Not sharply. He was not a jealous man; jealousy required a suspicion he had never had grounds to form, and he did not form one now, exactly — what he felt was closer to the mild confusion of finding a room in his own house he had not known was there.

She did not startle. That surprised him more than the box itself, afterward, thinking back on it — that she simply lifted her hand from the latch and turned to him with the same face, more or less, that she wore at the eleven o'clock promenade, except that something in it had not yet finished arriving, the way Frau Nemcová's warmth always arrived a half-beat ahead of the need for it. Klára's face, just then, was still catching up to its own performance.

"It is nothing," she said, which was true, and which he did not believe, not because he suspected a lie but because a man does not credit that a locked-looking silence over an unremarkable box could contain, truly, nothing.

He might have let it go. He had let a great many things go, over twelve years, out of a settled confidence that there was nothing under them worth the trouble of lifting. But he was tired in a way that made him, for once, less patient with a mystery than curious about it, and he said, "You've never shown me that box."

"No," she agreed. "I haven't."

"Is there a reason?"

And here — she told him afterward she could not entirely account for it, except that the hour was wrong, it was not yet nine, the light through the window was still the ordinary gray-gold of an evening candle and not the particular blue, and something in her that kept the hour so faithfully every other night of the year simply did not rise to guard the door in time — here she said, in a voice gone suddenly plain, stripped of every hedge the last twelve years had taught her:

"There was a man. Before you. He died before we married — an accident, at the garrison in Temesvár, before I ever knew you existed. I have kept something for him every night since. Not a letter. Not a photograph. There is nothing in the box you would call evidence of anything. I touch the latch at a fixed hour and that is the whole of it, and I have never told you, or anyone, because the moment I did, it would stop being what it is."

She heard herself say it and felt the thing already changing under her hands, even as she spoke — felt the twelve years' discipline turn, mid-sentence, from a private fact into an account of a private fact, which was not the same object at all, however similar the words.

Anton stood in the doorway for a long moment.

He did not ask his name. That, at least, she would remember afterward as a mercy of a kind, whether or not he meant it as one.

## IV.

The Rittmeister Anton Vesely walked, the next several mornings, to the eleven o'clock promenade at his accustomed hour, greeted the Baroness Fischer with his accustomed bow, drank his water, discussed with Řehák the now nearly certain collapse of the Hoffmann engagement, and did all of this with no outward alteration whatsoever, which was itself the most alarming thing about him, though no one in the town was positioned to notice it, since no one in the town had known there was anything to alter from.

He found that he thought of the box more than he thought he should, given how little there had been to see in it — he had not, in the end, asked to see inside; something in her stillness that evening had made the asking feel like it would cost more than he had any right to spend, a rare calculation for him, and he did not examine too closely why he had made it.

Twelve years he had held, without ever once stating it to himself in words, that his wife's contentment was a settled account, audited by nothing more than the absence of complaint, and that this was not merely convenient but *right* — that a man who provided a house, a name, a table, a place in the promenade's ordering, had thereby purchased a kind of clear title to the whole of what his wife was. He did not think it now in those words either. He was not a man given to examining the floor beneath a house he had lived in comfortably for twelve years. But he found that he could no longer quite avoid noticing that there had been a floor, and that something had always been going on beneath it, at a fixed hour, unaudited, entire, and that this had cost her nothing to keep from him and would apparently cost her something, now, to have had it taken by his simply having been standing in a doorway at the wrong time.

He did not know what to do with this. There was no entry for it in any ledger he had ever kept.

On the second-to-last evening of the Season, he came up early, deliberately, and found her door properly shut. He did not open it. He stood outside it for a moment — the light through the crack beneath it was, though he could not have named the color, going a particular blue — and then he went down again to the smoking room, where Bittner was holding forth on troop movements nobody present had the authority to confirm or deny, and he took a brandy, and he found, to his own mild surprise, that he had nothing to say about the Balkans that night at all.

He thought — and this thought arrived complete, in a shape he recognized, before he had time to stop it arriving — that it was, in its way, a rather touching thing, a wife who had carried such a fidelity so quietly for so long; that a man might, if he chose, tell this — carefully, without names, as *an instance of the deep feeling one finds beneath the most ordinary domestic surfaces* — to Reiner, next season, over the waters, in the way one offers an observation about the human heart that reflects, in the telling, rather well on the teller's own perceptiveness in having been married to such depths without ever previously suspecting them.

He did not decide to say this to anyone. He only noticed that the sentence had assembled itself, ready, somewhere in him, the way a bow assembled itself in him before the Baroness Fischer without his ever having decided, that morning or any morning, to bow.

He sat with his brandy a long while, past the hour Bittner gave up expecting an audience and went to bed, past the hour the pump-room clock — three minutes fast, always — would have shown, had he been there to read it, striking the fixed point that governed nothing in his own house at all.

He did not go up. He did not go looking for the box, or for her. He simply sat, in a silence that might, a stranger looking in might have said, have been the first real quiet the man had sat in for twelve years — or might, with equal justice, have been nothing more than a story taking its time to finish forming, waiting only for next August, and Reiner's waiting-room, and the right unhurried moment at which to be well told.

The baths closed nine days later. The Colonnade's board came down, the schedule with it. Frau Nemcová totaled her season in a ledger no one else was shown and began, that same week, quietly repainting the pension-house shutters against the spring.

Klára touched the latch that last night in town as she had touched it every night for longer than her marriage had existed, and felt, under her fingers, only cold brass — no colder, no warmer, no different in any way she could have named — and understood, without being able to say when the understanding had entered her, that whatever she was doing now, faithfully, at the fixed hour, in the particular blue light, was no longer the thing it had been. It had become, irreversibly, a thing she did *because* she once had. The difference was total and admitted no instrument that could have measured it, and no one — not Anton, not Reiner, not the Baroness's whole attentive town — had ever had, or would ever have, the right accounting to say what it had been worth before that, or what, exactly, had gone.
