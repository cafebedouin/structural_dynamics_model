# The Two Hundred and Eleventh Spring

## I. The Height of the Mark

The soil-reader came to the Vask terrace on the third morning after thaw, chalk in a tin box, assistant carrying the ladder. Odrun stood in his doorway with Sennet beside him and watched the man measure the wet ground with a bare foot.

He walked the width of the terrace. Then he climbed the ladder, and made his mark.

Odrun looked at the door the way he looked at the sky before a hard rain — not reading it, just knowing what it meant in his chest before his mind caught up. The new chalk line sat a hand's width above last year's, which had been chalk once and was now a groove, scratched permanent by his own knife, the way every year's mark got scratched in once the chalk washed off. The door was a ladder of small cuts going down to knee-height, to a mark his father had made when Odrun himself was no taller than that mark.

This year's line came to the middle of his chest.

He did not do the sum. There was no sum he'd ever been taught — not one his grandfather's iron toll-scale, sitting unused in its oilcloth in the granary rafters, could have told him even set on the step in front of him. He did the height. He had done the height every spring of his life. This year the height was higher, and his shoulders already knew what that meant for the back half of winter.

"Why's it higher," Sennet said. She'd grown some herself since autumn and stood measuring the mark against her own crown, a game she'd made out of a thing that was not a game.

"Ground gives what the ground gives," Odrun said. "And takes back its own after. Don't quarrel with the river."

She frowned at the door like it had done something to her personally.

"Marasz Ilendrë says the river doesn't do the taking. She says the reader does."

"Marasz Ilendrë talks too much for a woman who was sent back to the fields for it." He said this without heat. Everyone said it without heat. "Go on. Get the seed-chest open before I have to shame you into it."

She went, but slow, throwing one more look at the chalk line.

Odrun stood a moment longer in the doorway. He had walked the Line every spring of his life beside his neighbors, singing the words that made it holy — *we do not ask the river its reasons, we ask only that it hold to what it has always held* — and he had never once thought to ask the same question of the chalk. The Line was old as the founding, sung into the ground by the Seven Hands themselves. The chalk was just this year's weather, written on a door.

He did not know there was a cellar under a granary down the lane where a different kind of writing had been kept longer than anyone now living could account for. He would not have known what to do with it if he had.

He went to the west channel, because the locks did not care what a man's door said, and worked until his back forgot to complain.

## II. Chalk Is Not Cheap

Marasz's hands were grey to the wrist by midafternoon, not with silt but with chalk-dust. She'd spent the morning helping old Tolbrit Sennett re-mark her door after the reader left — Tolbrit's eyes had gone bad enough that she could not trust her own knife to follow the line true, and a badly scratched mark was its own kind of shame.

The chalk came upriver in sealed paper twists, rationed by the Council, good white chalk being dear and imported, not a thing the Delta made. Marasz had noticed, some years back, doing sums for a cousin who traded upriver, that a twist of the reader's chalk cost about what a silt-hand family spent on salt for a month. She noticed this quietly, and then could never quite stop noticing it again each spring when the tin box came out.

She washed her hands at the trough after, working the grey out of her knuckles.

That evening was lock-duty. Silt-hand households notched their names onto the tally-stick the moment they arrived, a household's days owed against the season's quota before planting could properly begin. Marasz's own family owed most of a week's worth this spring. She knew this without asking; she had helped notch the stick herself the last several years, trusted with the knife because her hand was steadier than the warden-clerk's.

The holding-families sent representatives too, as the song said they must — *let the hands of the ground and the hands of the ledger labor together, for the river does not know one from the other* — and this spring the Marrow household's representative was the daughter, Thessaly, finishing her apprentice-year, working the sluice-gate beside Marasz with her sleeves rolled and her back genuinely bent to the work.

She was good at it. Marasz gave her that. Thessaly hauled the gate-chain, laughed when the spray caught her across the face, did not once complain that the mud got into her boots.

What Thessaly did not do, when her own day was done, was notch anything onto that stick. The holding-families' cut of the Wardens' Rite was paid in grain from stores that had never once gone under a flood, because the holding-terraces sat higher up the delta fan, closer to the old original ground the Seven Hands had claimed first. A silt-hand day at the locks was a day not spent on the silt-hand's own furrows. A holding-family day at the locks cost the household nothing it would otherwise have used.

Marasz could have said all this to Thessaly's face. She had, once, carefully, to the reader who'd taken her on as a girl, before he sent her back for asking the same question about older ledgers. She did not say it now. There was no lever the saying would move that she could see.

So she hauled the chain, and let the water take the weight when it would, and said instead, because Thessaly had asked whether the Vask terrace's yield looked thin this year:

"Thin enough."

"That's a shame," Thessaly said, and meant it. "Odrun works harder than anyone in this district."

"He does," Marasz said, and left it there.

That night, alone, she went down under the granary two lots from her own, to the cellar door that stuck in damp weather and had to be lifted on its hinge before it would give.

The ledger was where it had always been, wrapped in oiled sacking, handwriting gone brown and cramped with age. She did not read it for anything new. She read it the way some women said the walking-words — not for information, but because the shape of the reading was the only place her private knowing was allowed to be a public act, even an audience of one.

*Seven households present at the Drawing. Land held at flood-hour to remain with the holder, and pass to the holder's blood, and not return to the common lot as was the old law, this being agreed by the Seven for the sake of the living, this spring, and no other reason given nor needed.*

She had turned that phrase over so many times it had gone smooth as a river-stone in her mind. People were drowned and starving and someone had to decide, fast, who held what. She was not sure hate was even the shape of the thing she carried.

What she carried had no good name. It sat in her like water sits in low ground. It only looked like forever from where she stood.

She had felt it first at fourteen, in this cellar, the day the old reader's apprentice-master had shown her the ledger meaning to teach her caution — *this is why you do not ask the Council about the old books, girl, some doors are shut for the district's own good* — and instead it had taught her that the ground under her whole life had a date on it.

She wrapped the ledger back in its sacking and went home to sleep a few hours before the fields wanted her again.

## III. What a Warden Chooses Not to Carry

Years before Thessaly was born, when her mother was newly seated, an old woman named Ferrin Dask had come to the warden-house door at an hour past decent, carrying a book wrapped in sacking under her shawl.

Thessaly's mother let her in. She would remember, for the rest of her life, the smell of wet wool and cellar-damp, and the sound the sacking made unwrapping on the table.

"You're new-seated," Ferrin Dask said. "You'll want to know what you're seated on."

She read some of it — not all; she stopped herself before she'd read enough to be unable to un-know it, though she never examined too closely how she'd known where that line was before she reached it. *No other reason given nor needed.* Her own grandmother's hand was not in it, but her grandmother's grandmother's might have been.

She sat with the book open in the empty warden-house and felt something she did not have a word for. Closer to the feeling of putting a hand flat on a granary wall and finding it warm on a cold night, and knowing, without wanting to know, that something was burning on the other side she could not see.

She closed the book. She did not read the rest.

"Why do you keep this," she asked Ferrin Dask, "if there's nothing to be done with it."

"Because someone should," the old woman said, "and it isn't going to be the Council."

Thessaly's mother never went back to the cellar. She arranged her rounds so they never took her past that granary at an hour when she might be tempted, and when Ferrin Dask died, and no one came asking after the book, she let herself believe it might simply be gone now.

She raised her daughter to be exactly the kind of warden she believed herself to be: rigorous, kind, thorough in every visible ledger. She gave her the ceremonial key with her own hands, and felt something close to relief.

## IV. The Ceremonial Key

Thessaly's rite-completion fell three days after lock-duty. She stood at the warden-house steps in the linen coat, her mother beside her in the deeper blue of a seated warden, and together they said the words that closed the rite — *I have walked what was walked before me, I have held what was held before me, I take up the song and do not change its shape* — and when it was done her mother took the ceremonial key from around her own neck and put the cord over Thessaly's instead.

It was a small iron key, opening a cabinet in the Council-house that held nothing more dangerous than the current season's accounting-books and the Council's own seal. Thessaly had seen it hang at her mother's throat her whole life. She had not expected the weight of it, once it hung at her own.

"That's the whole of it?" she asked, half-laughing.

"That's the whole of it you need," her mother said, and there was something in the way she said *need* — a small hitch, gone before Thessaly could properly hear it.

Thessaly did not think of the key as power. She thought of it the way she thought of her own hands, calloused honestly: something she had earned, through apprentice mud and cold water, alongside silt-hand daughters who worked the same chain beside her.

She liked Marasz Ilendrë, insofar as she thought about her at all — sharp, quiet, did the tally-stick notching more correctly than the warden-clerk himself. She had never once had it suggested to her that the grain her family sent instead of a labor-day was anything but a different, equally honest coin.

Her mother did not tell her, that day or any day after, about the other ledger. Thessaly went home with the key at her throat and slept well.

## V. The Accounting

The spring accounting was held in the Council-house's long room, holding-families on the long benches, silt-hands standing behind, a geography no one had written down and everyone understood.

Odrun stood near the back with his hat in both hands. The soil-reader read the season's figures household by household, flat, unhurried, too familiar to need feeling in it.

"The Vask terrace," the reader said, when it came round. "Toll assessed this spring exceeds the terrace's full season yield, by the reader's own measure and the Council's own rule, by more than the terrace has ever yet failed to close."

There was no cruelty in how he said it.

Odrun had known this was coming for two winters. He had felt it the way he felt weather, in the ache of an old break in his wrist before rain. But there was a difference between feeling a thing coming and hearing it said in a room with the whole district listening.

*Going under the toll.* He had heard the phrase said of other families and moved on to his own furrows. He had never thought it would come to sit in his own chest, cold and complete. His jaw had gone tight enough to ache. His hands, still holding the hat, had started, very slightly, to shake, and he pressed them flat against the brim and could not entirely stop it.

He found Marasz already looking back at him. He crossed the floor to her, before he'd decided to.

"Check it," he said, low, his voice not steady. "The reader's figure. You know his sums better than the reader knows them himself."

She looked at the figures a long moment — the toll rate applied, the same rate applied to every terrace this district over, no different for Vask than for anyone.

"It's correct," she said. "By the rules. Every figure follows from the last one."

He nodded, once, the way a man nods at bad weather he'd already dressed for, though his throat had gone tight in a way no nod could carry.

She wanted, in that moment, to say the other thing — that correct was not the same word as fair, that the rate had a history, a choice made once by seven households in a spring of dead bodies and hard bread.

What came out, because his face was already closing around the thing he'd come to hear, was: "It's correct, Odrun."

He heard the only word he had room to hear. He thanked her, and went to stand near the front, his hat gripped tight enough that the brim had begun to crease under his thumbs — a mark that would still be in the felt come summer.

---

Thessaly sat on the long bench in her mother's old seat, in the blue coat, and listened. The Vask figures were sound; she checked them herself, quietly, against her own understanding of the rate.

Odrun Vask worked as honestly as anyone in the district. That the terrace could not close its toll was a hard thing — the river gave unevenly, and a family that had held on through hard years sometimes simply could not hold on through the next.

The Council's business, when a terrace went under the toll, was set out plainly in the season-books: the forfeited land returned first to the Council's own price-register, offered at the price the reader's figures established, available to any holding-family in the district before it passed to open bidding beyond it. The Marrow terrace bordered the Vask terrace along its whole northern edge. It made every kind of practical sense — better the land go to a family who already knew its drainage.

That evening she went to the Council-house alone, let herself in with the key still strange and light at her throat, and drafted the request to open formal inquiry into the Marrow household's acquisition of the forfeited Vask terrace at Council-set price.

## VI. What the Question Could Not Hold

Odrun did not sleep that night. Sennet had gone to bed without asking anything, which frightened him more than her usual questions would have.

Marasz came to his door past the hour when honest people knocked, and he let her in because she was Marasz. She sat at his kitchen table in the low light of the banked fire, and for a while neither of them spoke in the ritual cadence at all — this was field-speech hour, the hour the day's formal words gave way to the plain ones.

"I want to tell you something," she said, "and I don't know how to say it so it comes out as anything but noise."

He waited.

"I found a book once," she said, "under a granary, when I was a girl. It has the names of the Seven Hands in it, and what they did the spring of the flood, in their own hand. It says why the land stopped going back to the common lot. It made sense, that spring. I want you to know that. It made sense."

Odrun turned his cup slowly on the table, not drinking from it. "What's that to me tonight."

"I don't know," she said, and the words came out unsteady in a way they hadn't in fifteen years of carrying them. "I've carried this feeling since I was fourteen, like the ground under everything is thinner than it looks. It's not the toll. It's everything under the toll. I don't have a word for it."

"Marasz." His voice was not unkind. It was simply full, already, of the only thing it had room for. He set the cup down, and his fingers stayed curled around its shape a moment after he'd meant to let go. "By autumn Sennet and I have no plot, no roof I can call ours, nowhere for her to stand and be measured against a door that isn't someone else's door. That's the whole of what's in front of me tonight. I don't have room in me for a book under a granary."

She looked at him a moment, and something in her face closed — not in anger, but in recognition, as if she'd known before she came that this was exactly what the room would say back, and had come anyway because the alternative was carrying it alone one more night.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't suppose you do."

"I'll come by before the reader's next visit," she said, rising. "See what can be carried over from my own store. It won't close it. I want you to know I know it won't close it."

"I know you know," he said.

After she'd gone, he sat a while longer, and did not go to bed, and did not go to the granary rafters where the toll-scale hung wrapped in its oilcloth — though he thought about taking it down, setting its cold iron arms on the step. He did not do it. He sat with his hands flat on the table instead and let the night go on without him doing anything to it at all.

---

## VII. The Fair Price

The paperwork moved through the Council-house's slow channels, past the outgoing warden's final signature, into the season's formal record, where a plot in one family's keeping longer than living memory reached back became, on an unremarkable morning, available for acquisition at a price the Council's own reckoning called fair.

Thessaly walked the boundary line herself the week the offer was lodged, noting the channel-work with a practiced eye. Odrun Vask kept his locks better than half the district. Partway along the northern edge she came to a low granary that marked the corner of a neighboring lot — not the Vask terrace, just a roofline she passed on the footpath — and noticed its foundation stones sat lower and older than any near her own family's holding, packed dark with moss that only grows where stone has stood undisturbed a long time.

She stood a moment looking at it. Then she walked on, back to the boundary and the drainage and the family who'd worked it.

The soil-reader came again the next spring, chalk and ladder, and made the season's mark on the Vask granary door — Marrow granary door, now, though no one below the Line had a word yet for how strange that would sound said aloud, and so no one said it aloud.

Sennet stood in the yard that morning and measured the new mark against her own crown out of old habit, before she remembered there was no game left in it worth playing. The scratched marks going down the door stayed where they were, cut into old wood that would stand another two hundred years whoever held it.

Below the granary, two lots over, the cellar door stayed shut through the whole of that spring, its hinge still stiff with the same damp, the sacking inside gone no browner than it had been the year before.
