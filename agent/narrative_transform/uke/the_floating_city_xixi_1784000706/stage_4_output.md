# The Two Hundred and Eleventh Spring

## I. The Height of the Mark

The soil-reader came to the Vask terrace on the third morning after thaw, as he came every year, with his rationed chalk in a tin box and his assistant carrying the ladder, and Odrun stood in his doorway with Sennet beside him and watched the man measure the wet ground with a bare foot, the way his father had described it being measured, the way it had always been measured.

The soil-reader did not speak until he had walked the whole width of the terrace twice. Then he climbed the ladder, and made his mark.

Odrun looked at the door the way he looked at the sky before a hard rain — not reading it, exactly, just knowing what it meant in his chest before his mind caught up. The new chalk line sat a hand's width above last year's, which had been chalk once and was now a groove, scratched permanent by his own knife the autumn after, the way every year's mark got scratched in once the chalk washed off, so the door was a ladder of small cuts going down to knee-height, to a mark his father had made when Odrun himself was no taller than that mark.

This year's line came to the middle of his chest.

He did not do the sum. There was no sum to do, not one he had ever been taught, not one his grandfather's iron toll-scale — sitting unused in its oilcloth in the granary rafters, forbidden to him for checking anything, kept only because it was his grandfather's and a man does not throw out his grandfather's tools — could have told him even if he'd taken it down and set it on the step. He did the height. He had done the height every spring of his life. This year the height was higher, and his shoulders already knew what that meant for the back half of winter, the way they knew the ache of the west channel before he'd walked there.

"Why's it higher," Sennet said. She had grown four fingers herself since autumn and stood measuring the mark against her own crown, the way she always did, half-serious, a game she'd made herself out of a thing that was not a game.

"Ground gives what the ground gives," Odrun said. "And takes back its own after. Don't quarrel with the river."

She frowned at the door like it had done something to her personally, which he supposed, from where she stood, it had.

"Marasz Ilendrë says the river doesn't do the taking. She says the reader does."

"Marasz Ilendrë talks too much for a woman who was sent back to the fields for it." He said this without heat. Everyone said it without heat; it was simply a fact about Marasz, the way the height of the mark was a fact about the door. "Go on. Get the seed-chest open before I have to shame you into it."

She went, but slow, throwing one more look at the chalk line as if it might climb back down if she watched it hard enough.

Odrun stood a moment longer in the doorway. He had walked the Line every spring of his life beside his neighbors, singing the words that made it holy — *we do not ask the river its reasons, we ask only that it hold to what it has always held* — and he had never once thought to ask the same question of the chalk. The Line was the Line; it was old as the founding, older than memory, sung into the ground by the Seven Hands themselves. The chalk was just this year's weather, written on a door.

He did not know there was a cellar under the granary two lots over where a different kind of writing had been kept for two hundred and eleven years. He would not have known what to do with it if he had.

He went to the west channel, because the locks did not care what a man's door said, and worked until his back forgot to complain, which took most of the day.

## II. Chalk Is Not Cheap

Marasz's hands were grey to the wrist by midafternoon, not with silt but with chalk-dust, because she had spent the morning helping old Tolbrit Sennett re-mark her door after the reader left, since Tolbrit's eyes had gone bad enough that she could not trust her own knife to follow the reader's line true, and a badly scratched mark was its own kind of shame, a sign to the whole lock-district that a household's hands had started failing before its debts had.

The chalk itself came upriver in sealed paper twists, four to a season, rationed by the Council because — so the wardens said — good white chalk was dear, imported, not a thing the Delta made. Marasz had noticed, some years back, doing sums for a cousin who traded upriver, that a twist of the reader's chalk cost about what a silt-hand family spent on salt for a month. She had noticed this the way she noticed most things: quietly, and then never quite able to stop noticing it again each spring when the tin box came out.

She washed her hands at the trough after, working the grey out of the creases of her knuckles, and thought, not for the first time, that a mark meant to look like plain truth cost somebody a month of salt to make.

That evening was lock-duty. Half the district turned out — silt-hand households first, notching their names onto the tally-stick the moment they arrived, because the stick counted labor-days and a household's days had to add to the season's quota before planting could properly begin, quota or no. Marasz's own family owed nine days this spring. She knew this without asking; she had helped notch the stick herself the last four years, trusted with the knife because her hand was steadier than the warden-clerk's.

The holding-families sent representatives too, as they always had, as the song said they must — *let the hands of the ground and the hands of the ledger labor together, for the river does not know one from the other* — and this spring the Marrow household's representative was the daughter, Thessaly, finishing her apprentice-year, working the sluice-gate beside Marasz herself with her sleeves rolled and her back genuinely bent to the work.

She was good at it. Marasz gave her that without resentment, the way she'd give a good ox its due for pulling straight. Thessaly hauled the gate-chain with the rest of them, laughed when the spray caught her across the face, did not once complain that the mud got into her boots the way silt-hand daughters' boots were always full of mud by the second day of thaw.

What Thessaly did not do, because there was no reason for her to, was notch anything onto the tally-stick when her own day was done. The holding-families' cut of the Wardens' Rite was paid in grain from their stores, assessed once a season by the Council's own reckoning — grain they had regardless, kept dry and safe in stone granaries that had never once gone under a flood because the holding-terraces sat higher up the delta fan, closer to the old original ground the Seven Hands had claimed first. A silt-hand day spent at the locks was a day not spent on the silt-hand's own furrows, a debt against the only thing a silt-hand family had to spend, which was time before the river came up. A holding-family day spent at the locks was, as far as the tally-stick was concerned, simply generosity, freely given, gladly repaid in grain that cost the household nothing it would otherwise have used.

Marasz could have said all this to Thessaly's face, in the clean unhurried arithmetic she'd built for it over fifteen years of watching the stick get notched. She had, in fact, said versions of it — carefully, years ago, to the reader who'd taken her on as a girl, before he sent her back for asking the same question about older ledgers. She did not say it now. There was no use in it that she could see, no lever the saying would move, and she had learned the particular ache of saying a true thing into a room built not to hear it.

So she hauled the chain, and let the water take the weight when it would, and said instead, because Thessaly had asked, mildly, whether the Vask terrace's yield looked thin this year:

"Thin enough."

"That's a shame," Thessaly said, and meant it, Marasz could tell she meant it, which was somehow the worst part. "Odrun works harder than any three men in this district."

"He does," Marasz said, and left it there, because the sentence she wanted to build after it — *and it will not be enough, and you will not be the one it isn't enough for* — had no good foothold in an evening of shared labor and honest sweat and a girl who was, by every measure she'd ever been given, kind.

That night, alone, she went down under the granary two lots from her own, past her uncle's grain-store, to the cellar door that stuck in damp weather and had to be lifted on its hinge before it would give.

The ledger was where it had always been, wrapped in oiled sacking against the wet, older than the song that made the Line holy, in handwriting gone brown and cramped with age. She did not read it that night for anything new. She knew what it said. She read it the way some women said the walking-words, not for information but because the shape of the reading itself was the only place her private knowing was allowed to be a public act, even an audience of one.

*Seven households present at the Drawing. Land held at flood-hour to remain with the holder, and pass to the holder's blood, and not return to the common lot as was the old law, this being agreed by the Seven for the sake of the living, this spring, and no other reason given nor needed.*

*No other reason given nor needed.* She had turned that phrase over so many times in fifteen years it had gone smooth as a river-stone in her mind. It had made sense, that spring. She did not doubt that. People were drowned and starving and someone had to decide, fast, who held what, or nothing would have been planted at all. She did not hate the Seven Hands for it. She was not sure hate was even the shape of the thing she carried.

What she carried had no good name. It sat in her like water sits in low ground — not moving, not draining, just there, cold, present every time she put her foot down. She had felt it first at fourteen, in this very cellar, the day the old reader's apprentice-master had shown her the ledger meaning to teach her caution — *this is why you do not ask the Council about the old books, girl, some doors are shut for the district's own good* — and instead of teaching her caution it had taught her that the ground under her whole life had a date on it. Two hundred and eleven years was not forever. It only looked like forever from where she stood.

She wrapped the ledger back in its sacking, chalk-grey dust still faint on her fingers though she'd washed twice, and went home to sleep four hours before the fields wanted her again.

## III. The Ceremonial Key

Thessaly's rite-completion fell three days after lock-duty, on the same morning the last of the winter ice broke loose from the Ashmother's slower bends and went downriver in grey rafts that her mother said, every year, without fail, looked like the Delta shedding its old skin.

She stood at the warden-house steps in the clean linen coat all apprentices wore for their final walking, her mother beside her in the deeper blue of a seated warden, and together they said the words that closed the rite — *I have walked what was walked before me, I have held what was held before me, I take up the song and do not change its shape* — and when it was done her mother took the ceremonial key from around her own neck and put the cord over Thessaly's head instead.

It was a small iron key, ornamental more than functional, opening a cabinet in the Council-house that held nothing more dangerous than the current season's accounting-books and the Council's own seal. Thessaly had seen it hang at her mother's throat her whole life. She had not expected the weight of it, once it hung at her own.

"That's the whole of it?" she asked, half-laughing, because the ceremony had felt so large and the object felt so small.

"That's the whole of it you need," her mother said, and there was something in the way she said *need* — a small hitch, gone before Thessaly could properly hear it as anything — that she filed away without examining, the way a person files away a stumble in someone else's voice when the day is otherwise good and there is cake waiting and half the district has come to see her seated.

Her mother had been warden eleven years, and her mother's mother before that, back and back to a Marrow who had stood among the Seven Hands themselves and put a mark on the ground that the whole Delta still sang about without irony every spring. Thessaly did not think of this as power, exactly, sitting there in the sun with the small key warm against her collarbone. She thought of it the way she thought of her own hands, competent at the sluice-chain, calloused honestly: something she had, that she had also earned, through six years of apprentice mud and cold water and genuinely aching shoulders, alongside silt-hand daughters who worked the same chain beside her.

She liked Marasz Ilendrë, insofar as she thought about her at all — sharp, quiet, did more of the tally-stick notching correctly than the warden-clerk himself, which Thessaly had noted approvingly more than once. She liked Odrun Vask's steadiness, his daughter's quick tongue, the whole practical, weathered dignity of the households whose fields her family's fields bordered. She believed, the way she believed the sun rose, that the Wardens' Cut was fair, because she had paid it herself in genuine labor every spring of her apprenticeship, and no one had ever once suggested to her that the grain her family sent instead of days was anything but a different, equally honest coin.

Her mother did not tell her, that day or any day after, about the other ledger — the one two lots from Marasz Ilendrë's own door, in a cellar that stuck on its hinge. She had decided this, privately, years before Thessaly was born, in the particular way a person decides a thing so early and so completely that it stops feeling like a decision: *a warden who does not carry the old grief judges the current season clean.* She believed this the way she believed most of what she'd been taught to believe, which was thoroughly, and with love for the daughter she was protecting from a weight she herself had never managed to set down.

Thessaly went home that evening with the key at her throat and her mother's blue coat folded for her in the chest at the foot of her bed, ready for the first accounting she would attend as a seated warden rather than an apprentice, and slept well, because there was nothing yet in her that had learned to sleep otherwise.

## IV. The Accounting

The spring accounting was held in the Council-house's long room, where the tally-sticks from every lock-district were laid on the table alongside the reader's season-books, and every holding-family and every silt-hand household with business before the Council stood or sat according to old custom — holding-families on the long benches, silt-hands standing behind, a geography no one had written down and everyone understood.

Odrun stood near the back with his hat in both hands, the way he always stood at accountings, waiting for his own name and dreading it in the same breath, the way he dreaded and waited for the first frost each year, knowing it was coming and unable to make it come faster or slower by wanting.

The soil-reader read the season's figures household by household, in the flat, unhurried voice of a man reciting something too familiar to need feeling in it. Grain owed. Grain given. Toll assessed against what the ground had returned, against what the ground was known to be able to return, against the compounding weight of what had not been paid in full the autumn before, and the autumn before that.

"The Vask terrace," the reader said, when it came round, and Odrun felt his own name arrive in his chest before it arrived in his ears, the way the mark's height had arrived in his shoulders before his mind found words for it. "Toll assessed this spring exceeds the terrace's full season yield, by the reader's own measure and the Council's own rule, by more than the terrace has ever yet failed to close."

There was no cruelty in how he said it. That was almost the worst of it — there was no one in the room to be angry at, no face to set his jaw against. The reader had only read what the ground and the years had put in front of him.

Odrun had known this was coming for two winters. He had felt it the way he felt weather, in the ache of an old break in his wrist before rain. But there was a difference, it turned out, between feeling a thing coming and hearing it said in a room with the whole district listening, in the particular dead-level voice the Council kept for facts that could not be argued with because no one had built them to be argued with.

*Going under the toll.* He had heard the phrase said of other families, over the years — the Hollarns, two lock-districts up, gone the winter Sennet was small; old Petrek Ashvale before that. He had said, the way everyone said it, that it was a hard thing, and moved on to his own furrows. He had never once thought the phrase would come to sit in his own chest, cold and complete, the way it sat there now.

He looked for Marasz among the standing silt-hands and found her already looking back at him, her face doing the careful, held-still thing it did when she was thinking harder than she wanted anyone to see. He crossed the floor to her, before he'd decided to, hat still in both hands.

"Check it," he said, low. "The reader's figure. You know his sums better than the reader knows them himself. Check it for me."

She did not need the ledger for this — the reader's season-book was open on the table, plain enough, and she had watched him tally it twice already without meaning to, the way she could not stop herself doing sums in a room full of numbers. She looked at the figures a long moment, at the compounding carried-forward from two autumns back, at the toll rate the reader had applied, which was the same rate applied to every terrace this district over, no different for Vask than for anyone.

"It's correct," she said. "By the rules. Every figure follows from the last one, clean as a sum should."

He nodded, once, the way a man nods at bad weather he'd already dressed for.

She wanted, in that moment, to say the other thing — that correct was not the same word as fair, that the rate itself had a history, that the reason the Vask terrace's toll compounded the way it did traced back past any season either of them had lived through, to a choice made once by seven households in a spring of dead bodies and hard bread, a choice that had never once been asked to justify itself again in two hundred years because it had been sung instead of argued. She wanted to say all of it.

What came out, because his face was already closing around the thing he'd come to hear, because there was no time in this room for the whole shape of what she knew, was: "It's correct, Odrun."

He heard the only word he had room to hear. He thanked her, and went to stand near the front, to hear the rest of what the Council would say about what came next for a terrace that could not close its own debt.

---

Thessaly sat on the long bench in her mother's old seat, in the blue coat, and listened to the reader's account with the particular clean satisfaction of a thing done properly. The Vask figures were sound. The reader had followed the season's rule to the letter; she checked this herself, quietly, against her own understanding of the rate, because she meant to be a warden who checked things, not one who simply nodded at what she was handed.

It troubled her, in the plain, uncomplicated way that a hard thing troubles a decent person. Odrun Vask worked as honestly as anyone in the district. His daughter was a bright, quick girl who'd have made a good warden's apprentice herself in another life. That the terrace could not close its toll this spring was a genuine misfortune, the kind the Council's own histories were full of — the river gave unevenly, some springs harder than others, and a family that had held on through four hard years sometimes simply could not hold on through the fifth.

The Council's business, when a terrace went under the toll, was set out plainly enough in the season-books her mother had taught her from childhood: the forfeited land returned first to the Council's own price-register, offered at the fair price the reader's figures established, available to any holding-family in the district before it passed to open bidding beyond it. The Marrow terrace bordered the Vask terrace along its whole northern edge. It had done so for two hundred years. It made every kind of practical sense — better the land go to a family who already knew its drainage, who would keep the channel-work sound, who would treat the Vask silt the way it deserved to be treated, than to some stranger-holder from three districts over who might let the terrace run to weed out of ignorance.

She thought, watching Odrun stand very still near the front of the room with his hat in both hands, that this was, in its own hard way, the system working as it should — measuring true, assessing fair, and catching a family gently as it fell, in the hands of neighbors who would care for the ground rather than strangers who would not.

She did not think of it as an acquisition. She thought of it as continuity. She had been taught, her whole life, that there was no cleaner word than *fair* for what the Council did, and nothing in the room that morning gave her cause to reach for another one.

That evening she went to the Council-house alone, let herself in with the small iron key still strange and light at her throat, and drafted, in her own careful hand, the request to open formal inquiry into the Marrow household's acquisition of the forfeited Vask terrace at Council-set price — a piece of ordinary paperwork, filed the way she'd file any Council form, without haste and without dread, because nothing in front of her asked her to feel either.

## V. What the Question Could Not Hold

Odrun did not sleep that night, or slept the shallow, listening kind of sleep a man sleeps when he already knows the exact shape of the thing that will wake him. Sennet had gone to bed without asking anything, which frightened him more than her usual questions would have — she had heard something, at the accounting or after, the way children heard things adults thought they'd kept from them, and had decided, on her own, not to ask.

Marasz came to his door past the hour when honest people knocked, and he let her in because she was Marasz, and because some part of him had been waiting, without admitting it, for someone to come and say something that might hold weight against what the day had put in his chest.

She sat at his kitchen table, in the low light of the banked fire, and for a while neither of them spoke in the ritual cadence at all — this was field-speech hour, the hour the day's formal words gave way to the plain ones, and she used it the way she always used it, as the register in which a true thing might finally be sayable.

"I want to tell you something," she said, "and I don't know how to say it so it comes out as anything but noise."

He waited. He had learned, over a hard day, that waiting was sometimes the only labor left to do.

"I found a book once," she said, "under Tolvard's granary, when I was a girl. Older than the walking-song. It has the names of the Seven Hands in it, and what they did the spring of the flood, in their own hand, not the song's hand. It says why the land stopped going back to the common lot. It says it plain — they needed to know, fast, who held what, so no one starved arguing over boundaries while the water was still up. It made sense. I want you to know that. It made sense, that spring."

Odrun turned his cup slowly on the table, not drinking from it. "What's that to me tonight."

"I don't know," she said, and for the first time in fifteen years of carrying it, the words came out unsteady, reaching for a shape they had never quite found. "I've carried it since I was fourteen, this — feeling, like the ground under everything is thinner than it looks, like I could put my foot down anywhere in this whole Delta and feel it give a little, because none of it was ever as old as it says it is. It's not the toll. It's not even the Cut. It's — everything under the toll. Everything under the Cut. I don't have a word for it. I've never had a word for it. I thought maybe if I said it to you—"

"Marasz." His voice was not unkind. It was simply full, already, of the only thing it had room for. "The terrace can't close its toll. It's under. By autumn Sennet and I have no plot. That's the whole of what's in front of me tonight. I don't have room in me for a book under a granary."

She looked at him a moment, and something in her face closed — not in anger, he saw, but in a kind of recognition, as if she had known, before she came, that this was exactly what the room would say back to her, and had come anyway because the alternative was carrying it alone one more night.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't suppose you do."

She did not try again to say the rest of it. The vertigo she'd carried since fourteen sat back down inside her, unnamed, unmet, exactly where it had always sat, and the question that mattered in that kitchen that night was not what the ground under the whole Delta was made of, but whether a man and his daughter would have a roof over them come the first frost. The larger question receded the way deep water recedes under a bridge you're too busy crossing to look down at. It did not go away. It simply waited, the way it had waited fifteen years already, for a night with room enough to hold it, which this was not.

"I'll come by before the reader's next visit," she said, rising. "See what can be carried over from my own store. It won't close it. I want you to know I know it won't close it."

"I know you know," he said, and that was as close as either of them came, that night, to saying anything true about the size of what they were both standing in.

## VI. The Fair Price

The paperwork moved the way ordinary paperwork moved — through the Council-house's slow, proper channels, past the outgoing warden's final signature confirming the figures, into the season's formal record, where a plot two hundred and eleven years in one family's keeping became, on a specific unremarkable morning, available for acquisition at a price the Council's own reckoning called fair.

Thessaly walked the boundary line herself, the week the offer was formally lodged, the way she'd walk any new addition to her family's holding, noting the channel-work with a practiced, approving eye — Odrun Vask kept his locks better than half the district, she thought, which was one more reason it made sense for the terrace to pass to hands that would appreciate what he'd built rather than let it run down through neglect.

She did not go looking for a cellar under a granary two lots away. There was no channel in her that ran toward it; nothing in her whole clean, competent, honestly-earned life had ever given her cause to doubt that the ground she stood on went all the way down to something solid. She felt, walking the boundary in the thin early light, something close to tenderness — for the terrace, for its careful drainage, for the family who'd worked it and would, she was already deciding, be offered fair terms to stay on as hired hands if they wished it, which seemed to her the kindest thing the Council could do, and which she meant, entirely.

---

The soil-reader came again the next spring, as he always did, with his rationed chalk and his ladder, and made the season's mark on the Vask granary door — Marrow granary door, now, though no one below the Line had a word yet for how strange that would sound said aloud, and so no one said it aloud.

Sennet stood in the yard that morning, taller by another season's growth, and measured the new mark against her own crown out of old habit before she remembered there was no game left in it worth playing. The scratched marks going down the door — her grandfather's, her father's, going back to a height her father had once reached only to his own father's waist — stayed where they were, cut into old wood that would stand another two hundred years whoever held the door.

Below the granary, two lots over, a cellar door that stuck on its hinge in damp weather stayed shut through the whole of that spring, the way it had stayed shut through two hundred and eleven springs before it, holding what it held, waiting, the way ground waits, for someone with reason enough, and room enough, to lift it.
