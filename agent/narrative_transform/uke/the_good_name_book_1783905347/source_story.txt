# The Good-Name Book

The book lives in a walnut case with a lock the damp ruined years ago, and nobody steals it, because stealing it would be like stealing the river: the water is not the thing, the falling is, and the book would go on falling without its pages. I keep it. I have kept it for the house of Verentz and Sons for twenty-two years, and I can tell you what every keeper before me could have told you and none of them wrote down: the book does not lend money. The book decides what a name costs.

It works like this. A woman wants coin against her harvest, or credit at the chandler's, or a winter's fuel before the winter. She does not go to the chandler. She goes to the house, and the house forwards the coin and takes its part, and the part is set by her standing — a mark against her name, one to twelve, that I raise when her debts clear through our door and lower when they clear late, or short, or not at all. A twelve borrows at almost nothing. A three pays a third of everything she raises for the raising of it. Below a three the house does not lend, and a name the house will not lend to is a name the chandler will not chalk and the seed-merchant will not wait for, because they have all learned to ask the house before they trust, the way you'd ask a ferryman about the ice.

Nobody decreed this. That is the part visitors cannot be made to understand. There is no law that a person must be in the book. There is only the fact that everyone who might trust you consults it first, so that being outside it is not freedom, it is fog.

Isa Wendl lived outside it for forty years, two doors up from the house itself, and kept half our street alive, and the book never heard her name. When the Aldermann's wife shut the mill accounts in the bad year, it was Isa's flour that went over the back walls in the dark. When Brasch the roofer smashed his hand, Isa's two sons sat his ladders for a season and no coin moved anywhere. I knew, everyone knew, what her word was worth: everything, and in no amount. You could not have written it in the book, and I want to be exact about why, because I have heard clever men get it wrong. It is not that her standing was high and we failed to record it. The book reads debts that pass through the house's door. What Isa kept never passed any door; it went hand to hand, and the keeping of it was the whole of what it was. A faith like that is not a figure the book misses. It is not a figure. Ask what it converts to and you have already stopped talking about it.

Her son crushed his leg under the ice-cart the winter I turned fifty. The surgeon in Thale wanted more than hands can carry, and hands were all she had — hands owed her half the street's winters, and she would not call a single one of them in, because calling them in would have made them debts, and she had spent forty years keeping them from being that. So she came through our door instead, and stood in front of my desk with her hat off, and asked to be written in.

I wrote her at the entering mark. A four. Everyone unwritten enters at a four; the book gives no credit for the years it was not reading. She paid a four's part on the surgeon's sum — steep, near a third — and she paid it without a word, and I raised her a point each quarter as the payments cleared, exactly as the rules of the house instruct, and here is where the story goes wherever it is going, because I did nothing wrong to her, and I wrote nothing false, and I watched the book eat her street like lye.

It read her true. That was the harm. Once her name stood in the book, what her hands did began to be visible from inside it. The neighbors who owed her their winters knew her mark, knew she carried a written debt now, and their gratitude — which had been the kind of thing you repay by being ready, for years, for nothing — turned quietly into the kind of thing you settle. Coin came to her door from people who had never once paid her in coin, because nobody wants to owe a hand-debt to a written name; it feels like standing surety for the weather. Brasch's wife squared their account to the copper and did not send the autumn cabbages after. The flour stopped going over walls, hers or anyone's; you cannot pass flour over a wall to a woman whose mark you know, it has a value now, it goes on a slate somewhere even when no slate is in the room. Within two years the street's whole web of being-owed-and-not-minding had pulled itself in through our door and lay in my columns, priced, current, and dead. Her mark stood at nine by then. It has since gone higher. She is, the book says, one of the best names on the street, and the street is poorer to live on than it has been in my lifetime, and every column in my keeping is correct.

I know what a listener wants me to have done. Shaved her figure, forged her a twelve, kept one page mercifully wrong. As if the trouble were the number and a kinder number would have spared her. Understand this much or you have wasted an evening: a twelve would have eaten the street faster. The harm was never in where the mark stood. It was in there being a mark, where before there had been a woman whose word did not convert.

The house is pleased with me. Old Verentz's grandson runs it now, and he has seen what I have seen, though he reads it off the other side of the ledger: Isa's page, he says, showed us a street the book had never reached. All that faith moving in the dark with no part taken of it. He has had a new entering-book bound, red boards, my name stamped on it as keeper, and this spring he is sending me up the street, door to door, to offer every unwritten name the favor the house did her.

I have not refused. I am the keeper; keeping is what I know; and I tell myself, on the stairs, in the doorways, with the red book under my arm, that a true figure honestly kept is no lie. It is not a lie. There was just a thing the street had that was only itself while nobody could read it, and I am the reading, come up the stairs, taking off my hat.
