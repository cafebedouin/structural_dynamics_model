# The Damp

The stove had been lit since matins and still her hands would not warm.

Marfa Osipovna held them close to the black iron, turned them front and back like a woman drying wet mittens, though the day was dry and the fire good. Foma Silovich kept a well-stocked woodpile; this she knew because he told her so, and because the house did not creak with cold the way her father's house had creaked, out on the drier ground where the wind came in under the door in winter and no one thought anything of it. Here the door was tight. Here the roof did not weep. It was, by every measure she had been given to measure a house by, a good house, and she was ashamed, standing at the stove with her hands out like a beggar's, that her own blood had grown too thin to hold the warmth such a house provided.

She had been a poor keeper of blood before this. Her mother used to say so — *Marfushka runs cold, always did, even at the breast* — and so when the ache came into her wrists that winter, and the grey crept up under her nails like dusk coming early, she understood it the way she understood everything about her body: as a fault of her own thinness, not as a message from anywhere outside herself.

She did not write to her mother. To write would mean saying she was unwell, and to say she was unwell in her husband's house, to kin outside her husband's house, was to say the house did not tend her — and the house *did* tend her, this she would have sworn on the Gospel, this was not a thing she doubted any more than she doubted the ground held when she walked on it. Foma Silovich was not an unkind man. He did not raise his hand. He brought in fish and marsh-fowl and, twice that autumn, a length of red wool that he'd traded a fox-pelt for, specifically, he said, because red suited her coloring — though by the time the wool came she had stopped believing anything suited her coloring, since her coloring had gone the grey of standing water.

She kept the threshold. Her mother had kept it before her, and her mother's mother. When old Xenia from two steadings over came asking after her — this was in the second year, before the worst of it — Marfa Osipovna had gone out to the yard herself, rather than let the old woman past the gate, and had stood in the cold answering her questions with her arms folded into her shawl so the shaking wouldn't show, and had said, *I am well, Foma Silovich keeps a warm house,* and had believed every word of it as she said it, the way one believes the sky is up.

Her hands ached worst in the evening, after she'd carried the water. She had begun, without deciding to, resting the buckets partway to the door, twice, sometimes three times, and hating herself for the weakness of it, glancing back toward the house as though the house itself might be watching her fail at the one thing a wife was for.

---

The znakharka Praskovya Terentievna had delivered forty-one children into the marsh air, buried perhaps a third of the mothers who bore them and rather more of the children, and she had, in fifteen years of doctoring by hand and eye and memory, met the damp in every one of its moods. She knew it as a slow damp and a quick damp, a damp that came with the first frost and a damp that came with the thaw-mud, a damp that took the old first and, more cruelly, a damp that took the young mothers, the ones worn down already by childbed, faster than it took anyone.

She had been called to Foma Silovich's house near the Feast of the Intercession, on account of the wife's cough, and had found a young woman she'd delivered no children for — the marriage was two years old and empty yet of that particular grief, which Praskovya had noted and set aside without comment, since barren years happened and were no one's business but God's — sitting very straight at the table with her hands folded into her sleeves.

She unwrapped the hands herself, since Marfa Osipovna seemed reluctant, and looked a long moment at the nail-beds.

She had seen that grey. She could have told you, standing in that kitchen, the names of six women in fifteen years whose nails had gone that particular color, the blue-grey like a duck's egg left too long in the shell, and she could have told you which of them lived and which did not, and roughly how the season had gone for each, wet or dry, and what herbs she'd given, and in what order.

What she could not tell you — what nagged her, later, walking home along the dike with her basket on her arm — was why none of the six fit.

She gave nettle and the bitter marsh-root that brought the water off the lungs, and told Foma Silovich, who stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and his face arranged into the correct shape of a worried husband, that his wife should rest and take broth and be kept from drafts, and he nodded and thanked her formally and paid her in dried fish, and she left thinking, the way she thought after most such visits, that she had done what could be done and the rest was in other hands than hers.

It was on the walk home, with the light going long and orange over the reeds, that she said aloud, to no one, the way she sometimes talked to herself on long walks, "Some fevers come from the pot, not the marsh." Her own teacher had said it to her once, an old woman half a century dead now, meaning it the way old women meant such things — a warning against bad cooking, spoiled grain, the general moral slovenliness that let a house go to ruin. Praskovya had laughed at it even then, young as she was, thinking it the kind of thing said by women who hadn't yet learned to tell one fever from another by better means. She laughed at it again now, alone on the dike, a little embarrassed at herself, and put it out of her mind, and did not write it anywhere, because there was nowhere in her practice such a thought had a place to be written.

---

By the time the first hard frost came, Marfa Osipovna had stopped resting the water-buckets partway; she simply could not lift a full one anymore, and Foma Silovich, without being asked, without complaint, took over the carrying himself, morning and evening, and told anyone who inquired that his wife was under the damp and being nursed as well as any wife in the marsh, and no one doubted it, because it was true.

He was, in this season, seen by the settlement as a man bearing a hardship with patience. His uncle Prov Andreyevich, who sat *sobor* and had married the two of them with his own blessing, remarked to another elder that Foma Silovich was managing his household with proper order despite the affliction God had sent it, and that a lesser man might have sent the wife back to her people rather than nurse her through — which, Prov Andreyevich noted approvingly, would have been within his rights, the dowry-contract binding her to the house but not requiring him to keep a wife who could no longer keep it. That he kept her, tended her, spoke of her with the correct grave tenderness at council meetings — this was, to every man in that room, evidence of a well-run house and nothing else.

Marfa Osipovna, for her part, in the rare hours the fever loosened its grip enough to let her think clearly, thought of her mother's face and felt something that had no name in her, a grief with nothing to be sad *at* — not at the house, which had never once in two years failed to be warm and tight-doored and provisioned with fish and red wool; not at her husband, whom she addressed even in the privacy of her own skull as Foma Silovich, formal, patronymic, because there was no other Foma she had ever been permitted to know, no small name worn soft by use in the dark, no version of him that existed only for her — and so the grief simply sat in her chest with no direction to travel, the way water sits in a low place with no outlet, going stagnant because it cannot find the sea.

She thought, some nights, of sending for her mother anyway. The thought came and went like a coal flaring and dying in ash. To send for her mother would be to say: come look at what is wrong in my house. And there was nothing wrong in her house. There was only her, wrong, in it.

---

The district doctor came in the spring, as he did every spring and every autumn when the roads held, a brisk sandy-bearded man named Kirill Denisovich Struve who had trained in Dorpat and carried his instruments in a case lined with green baize and treated every household on his circuit with the same even courtesy, whether they received him with tea and deference or, as some of the more suspicious Old Believer steadings did, with the wary politeness owed to an outsider whose baptism was in doubt.

Praskovya walked the two versts to Foma Silovich's house specifically to be present for the examination, as she often did when a case troubled her, since two sets of trained eyes were better than one and Struve did not, unlike some doctors on unlike circuits, resent a znakharka's presence in the room.

He was gentle with Marfa Osipovna, who could no longer sit fully upright without support and had to be helped to the chair by the window for the light. He looked at her tongue, her eyes, the whites gone the color of old linen; he pressed at her wrists and her ankles, where the swelling sat; he had her cough into a cloth and studied what came up; he asked her, through Praskovya, since the girl's voice had gone thin and she answered slowly, about her appetite, her sleep, whether the ache moved or stayed fixed, whether she had traveled anywhere the vapors might be stronger, whether the water she drank came from the river or the well.

He ruled out, competently and in order, the quick consumption that took the lungs alone; he ruled out the childbed fevers, she having borne no child; he considered and set aside the joint-swelling that came from cold standing water underfoot, since her ankles showed a different character of swelling than that; he asked whether the household kept animals near the sleeping-quarters, a known vector for certain marsh agues, and was told they did not.

He wrote, in the ledger he carried in the same case as his instruments, in a clear practiced hand, under the date and the name of the steading:

*Wasting fever, of the marsh type, advanced stage. Attributable to prolonged exposure, damp climate, and constitutional weakness of the patient. Recommend continued rest, warmth, nourishing broth. Prognosis guarded.*

He was not wrong in any particular he examined. Every category his training furnished him a name for, he tested, and tested rightly, and found consistent with what stood before him. He did not think to ask what Foma Silovich fed his wife with his own hand, because a husband feeding his wife was not a category of illness; it was not a category of anything. It was simply what a husband did, in a well-run house, for a wife too weak to feed herself — and Struve had, in fact, noted with private approval, on his way in, how attentively Foma Silovich described his wife's diet, how carefully he'd kept a broth going these three days specifically because the doctor was expected, wanting, plainly, to show he was doing everything asked of a husband in this position.

Struve closed his case, said a few kind words to the patient that she may or may not have heard, told Foma Silovich to send word if she worsened sharply though the roads might not permit a swift return, and rode on to the next steading on his circuit, where a child had a rash that turned out, more happily, to be nothing more than the measles going through as measles will.

Praskovya walked him partway to the crossing, out of the old habit of seeing outsiders off the settlement's paths, and asked him, since she trusted his training more than her own in matters requiring words like *constitutional* and *prognosis*, whether he had ever seen a damp-case that did not answer to any remedy — not slowly worsen and improve, worsen and improve, the way damp cases did, but simply worsen, steadily, month on month, with no plateau at all.

He considered this seriously, being a serious man, and said that constitution varied greatly, that some patients simply had less to give the illness to work against, and that grief, poor diet in childhood, a naturally cold humor — many things could account for a steeper decline than the marsh alone might explain. He said it kindly. He believed it. There was nothing in his believing it that was not the honest output of everything he had ever been taught to look for.

Praskovya thanked him and turned back toward the settlement, and did not say the thing about the pot, because she had already decided, walking home from that first visit half a year before, that it was superstition, and a woman of her standing did not trade in superstition in front of a Dorpat-trained doctor, whatever she muttered to herself on empty dikes.

---

That same spring she was called to another house, three steadings over, to look at a servant-girl who'd taken sick — no kin of anyone's, an orphaned girl bonded into the household for her keep, thin to begin with and thinner now, with a cough and a grey creeping into her hands that Praskovya noted, out of long habit, the moment she took the girl's wrist to feel the pulse.

The mistress of that house stood in the doorway the whole visit, arms folded, saying the girl had always been sickly, had come to them sickly, that they'd fed her better than her own kin ever had and she still would not thrive, and Praskovya, examining the grey at the girl's nail-beds, thought — not in words, not as a thought she could have repeated to anyone, but as a cold small motion somewhere under her ribs — *I have felt this exact cold before, this month, in another house.*

She gave the girl the same nettle, the same bitter root, told the mistress to keep her from drafts and see she ate, and walked home turning the coincidence over the way she turned over any two cases that resembled each other, filing it as marshland always did this to the weak in a wet spring, filing the girl under the same damp as Marfa Osipovna and every other case that season, because there was no drawer in her mind, no drawer in anyone's mind in the whole breadth of the marsh, marked for the thing that would have let her set the two cases side by side and see that resemblance was not the same as cause.

The girl recovered, that time. Sickly girls sometimes did.

Praskovya did not think of it again until much later, and by then it had gone the way of most such small unease — filed, not followed, because there was nowhere past the filing for it to go.

---

Marfa Osipovna died in the week after the first hard rime of the following autumn, in the bed she had shared for three years with Foma Silovich, in the house whose door had never once, in all that time, let in a draft or a visitor uninvited. She had stopped, by then, being able to hold a cup without her husband's hand steadying it, and it was his hand that steadied it to the last, and she was, in the accounting of everyone who came to sit with the body — Prov Andreyevich among them, murmuring the correct words about God's will and a hard-borne affliction — a wife who had been faithfully nursed through a wasting no one could have arrested, in a house that had done everything a house could do.

Praskovya came to lay her out, as was proper, and studied the grey hands one last time, turning them the way she'd turned them at the very first visit, cataloguing without meaning to — the color gone further than she had ever seen it go and lived, the smell of the sickroom sourer than any damp-death she could set beside it in memory — and felt the cold small motion under her ribs again, stronger this time, with nothing to attach itself to and nowhere to go. She said nothing of it to Prov Andreyevich, nor to Foma Silovich, who thanked her with real feeling in his voice for all she had done these two years, and meant it, as far as Praskovya could tell, and she could tell a great deal, having spent fifteen years learning to read a room by grief and by guilt and having never once, in this house, across all those visits, caught the second on any face but the dying woman's own — turned always, she now remembered, unaccountably, against herself.

She walked home in the dark that night and did not say the thing about the pot. She thought it, once, going over the dike with her basket empty, and dismissed it in the same breath, the way she had dismissed it a year before, because a case that would not answer to remedy was, after all, still only a case — an unusually stubborn damp, the kind the marsh sent perhaps once in a healer's working life, that took a young wife despite a well-kept house and a husband who did all that could be asked, and left nothing behind it but grief without a place to put itself, in a healer who had reached, honestly, the very edge of what her long practice could tell her, and stopped there, because there was nothing on the other side of that edge that her practice, or anyone's, had ever taught her to see.

Foma Silovich remarried within two years, a girl from a steading upriver, and kept, by every account that reached Praskovya's ears in the seasons after, a good house.
