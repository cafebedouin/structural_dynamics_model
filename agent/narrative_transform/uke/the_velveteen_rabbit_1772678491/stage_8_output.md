# The Fumigation Records

## PART I

**From the medical journal of Dr. James Morrison, London Missionary Society Hospital, Guangzhou**

*15 June 1873*

I have been documenting respiratory disease among the silk workers for two years now. The pattern holds: five years of exposure, and the lungs begin their collapse. First the morning cough. Then the shortness of breath. Then the inability to stand a full shift.

The workshop managers call this *fei-gong*, "spent labor." When a worker can no longer maintain productivity, they transition to lighter tasks. The wage drops by half. The housing shifts to the attic spaces where summer heat makes breathing harder still.

I call it occupational consumption. The workshop produces silk. It also produces damaged lungs.

The cholera has reached the delta. The consular medical officer has distributed his protocol: workshops with confirmed cases will be evacuated and fumigated. Workers relocated to temporary housing outside the city walls.

The protocol's exception clause: workers in *fei-gong* status will not be evacuated. Dr. Harding's reasoning is documented with the clarity of a man who has never struggled to breathe—their damaged lungs make them high-risk for cholera mortality. They will remain in the sealed workshops during fumigation. Three days. Sulfur and lime.

The logic is consular, which is to say it operates under extraterritorial jurisdiction where Chinese workers have no legal standing to contest British medical authority.

I have written to object. I expect the response will be polite and final.

*22 June 1873*

The cholera reached Patterson's Number Two workshop yesterday. Consular police arrived this morning. I went to observe.

## PART II

The heat comes through the attic floorboards. Ah-Yong feels it before she opens her eyes—the weight of it on her chest, pressing down where her lungs already struggle. She counts her breaths while lying still. Twelve before the cough starts. Yesterday it was fifteen.

The other *fei-gong* workers are already awake. Mei-Lin sits by the small window, her face turned toward the river. Old Chen sorts through damaged cocoons, his hands moving with the automatic precision of thirty years' practice. Ah-Lin lies on her mat with one arm across her eyes. No one speaks. Speaking costs breath.

Ah-Yong sits up slowly. The tightness is there, always there now, a band around her ribs that tightens when she moves too quickly. She has thirteen months remaining on her contract. Thirteen months, and then she will be free to leave this place where she can no longer work.

The cough is not just a symptom. It is a clock.

She descends the narrow stairs to the second floor, one hand on the wall. The morning shift workers are already at their stations. The sound fills the building—the hiss of steam, the rhythmic clatter of the looms, the splash of cocoons in hot water. She remembers that sound from inside her body, the way the rhythm would enter through her feet and travel up her spine. Now she hears it from outside. She is *fei-gong*. The workshop has already extracted what it needed from her body.

In the sorting room, she takes her place at the long table. Raw silk arrives in baskets from the cocoon vats. Her job is to separate the usable thread from the waste. Her hands know this work. They move while her mind counts breaths.

Patterson appears in the doorway at mid-morning. His presence changes the room's atmosphere.

"Ah-Yong." His Cantonese is adequate but carries the accent of a man who learned the language for business. "Come with me, please."

She follows him to his office on the first floor. The air is better here—the windows face the river, and the coal smoke rises rather than settling. She allows herself three deeper breaths before he begins speaking.

"There is cholera in the workshop." He says this in English now, the language of official matters. "Consular police will come today. You understand the protocol?"

"Yes, sir." Her English is halting but functional.

"The healthy workers will go to temporary housing. You and the other *fei-gong* will remain here during fumigation. Three days. The medical officer says this is safest."

The tightness in her throat has nothing to do with her lungs now. "I stay in workshop. During fumigation."

"Yes. You'll be given cloth to cover your faces. The medical officer assures me the procedure is safe." He pauses. "It's for your own protection, Ah-Yong. Your lungs are already compromised. Cholera would kill you faster than the healthy workers."

She nods because nodding costs no breath. The protocol is written in English, enforced by consular police, designed by a medical officer who has never spent a night in the attic where heat makes breathing an act of will.

"You understand?" Patterson asks.

"Yes, sir. I understand protocol."

He looks relieved. "Good. The police will arrive this afternoon."

She climbs the stairs slowly, counting. Eighteen breaths for three flights. Yesterday it was sixteen.

---

The consular police arrive at noon. Six Sikh officers in their distinctive uniforms, led by a British sergeant who speaks no Cantonese. They are efficient.

The healthy workers are assembled in the courtyard. Ah-Yong watches from the attic window as they file out. Some look up at the attic. Most do not.

Dr. Morrison arrives with the police. Ah-Yong recognizes him—he runs the missionary hospital, treats workers when they can afford his fees. Sometimes when they cannot. He argues with the British sergeant now, his voice carrying up to the attic window though she cannot make out the words.

The sergeant shakes his head. Dr. Morrison argues more forcefully. The sergeant says something final and turns away.

Dr. Morrison looks up at the attic. Their eyes meet across the distance. He raises one hand, a gesture she cannot interpret. Then he follows the healthy workers toward the city gate.

The workshop empties. The sound changes—no more looms, no more steam, no more voices. Just the *fei-gong* workers in the attic and the police outside, sealing the doors.

Old Chen distributes the wet cloths. "Cover your face when the smoke comes," he says in Cantonese. "Breathe shallow. We survive three days, then it's over."

Mei-Lin laughs, a sound like breaking glass. "Three days. Maybe we die here."

"Maybe," Chen agrees. "But maybe not."

Ah-Yong ties the cloth across her nose and mouth. It is already hard to breathe through it, and the fumigation has not yet begun. She sits by the window, watching the river. The afternoon light makes the water look clean, though she knows it is not.

She thinks about her family in Guangdong province. Her mother, her younger brother. The money she sends them every month, half of what she used to earn. After the contract ends, what then? She cannot do heavy work. Her lungs are ruined.

The transformation happened so gradually she did not notice until it was complete. The first year, she was strong. The second year, the cough started but seemed minor. The third year, the shortness of breath. The fourth year, the *fei-gong* transition. Now she is something else, something the workshop has made her.

The smoke begins at sunset.

It comes through the floorboards first, white and acrid. Ah-Yong presses the wet cloth against her face and tries to breathe shallow. But shallow breathing is not enough. The smoke fills the attic. It fills her lungs.

She counts breaths. One. Two. Three. Each one tears. The cloth is useless. The smoke goes through it, into her, making itself at home in the damaged tissue of her lungs.

Mei-Lin is coughing. Chen is coughing. Ah-Lin is coughing. Ah-Yong is coughing. The sound fills the attic, competing with the hiss of the sulfur below.

The protocol is inside her now.

She loses time. The smoke is everything. The heat is everything. Her lungs are collapsing, or maybe they collapsed years ago and she is only now noticing.

She thinks about her mother's face. Her brother's laugh. The river in Guangdong province where she learned to swim as a child. Before the contract. Before her body became data in Dr. Morrison's journal.

The smoke thickens. Ah-Yong closes her eyes and tries to find breath in the spaces between smoke. There is none.

She stops counting breaths. The numbers are meaningless when the thing being measured is running out.

The smoke finds the places where her lungs are already damaged and settles there. She can feel it—a weight, a pressure, something taking up residence in the spaces where air should be.

Time becomes strange. She is in the attic. She is in the river in Guangdong province. She is at the looms. She is in her mother's house, small again, before the contract. The smoke is in all these places.

Somewhere nearby, someone is crying. Or coughing. Or both.

The workshop has been taking her breath for four years. The fumigation is taking the rest. When it is done, what will be left?

She thinks: Nothing.

But she is wrong.

## PART III

**From the medical journal of Dr. James Morrison**

*25 June 1873*

The fumigation is complete. I accompanied Dr. Harding and the consular police when they unsealed Patterson's Number Two workshop this morning.

We expected casualties. Three days of sulfur exposure for workers whose lungs are already compromised—the mathematics are clear.

We found the *fei-gong* workers in the attic. All four alive.

They were unconscious when we entered, collapsed near the window. But breathing. All of them breathing.

Ah-Yong was the first to wake. I knelt beside her, checking her pulse, her respiration. Both weak but steady. I expected her to cough—the cough is constant in *fei-gong* workers. She did not cough.

"Can you hear me?" I asked in Cantonese.

She opened her eyes. They focused on my face with a clarity I had not seen in her before. She sat up slowly, testing her body. Then she took a breath—a full breath, deep and easy.

"The smoke," she said. "It's gone."

I checked her lungs. No wheezing. No crackling. The signs of occupational consumption that I had documented in her file four months ago—gone.

Respiratory damage does not heal. Certainly not overnight. Certainly not after three days of sulfur exposure that should have destroyed what little lung function remained.

The other three workers showed the same impossible recovery. Old Chen, whose file documented thirty years of progressive respiratory failure. Mei-Lin, whose cough I had heard from across the hospital ward. Ah-Lin, who had transitioned to *fei-gong* status only six months prior. All of them breathing easily now.

Dr. Harding is pleased. He sees it as validation of his protocol. He did not examine them before the fumigation. He does not know what their lungs were like three days ago.

I know. I have the documentation. I have two years of data showing that occupational consumption is progressive and irreversible.

Something happened in that attic. Something the protocol could not predict and cannot measure.

Patterson has already reassigned Ah-Yong to full labor status. Her productivity is restored. She will return to the looms tomorrow.

I asked her how she feels. She smiled—a strange, distant expression. "Light," she said in Cantonese. "Like something that was holding me has let go."

I asked if she remembered anything from the fumigation. She shook her head. "Just smoke. And then waking up."

The pattern I have been documenting—five years of exposure, progressive respiratory failure, *fei-gong* transition—has been disrupted by an intervention I cannot explain.

I do not know. I have measurements and observations, but I do not know.

*28 June 1873*

I have spent three days examining the recovered workers. Their lungs sound like those of people who have never worked in a silk workshop.

I have reviewed my documentation:

**Ah-Yong, age 24:** Four years at Patterson's Number Two. *Fei-gong* status eight months ago. Examination 15 March 1873: reduced lung capacity, persistent cough, crackling in lower lobes. Prognosis: progressive deterioration.

**Old Chen, age 52:** Thirty years in silk workshops. *Fei-gong* status six years. Examination 2 April 1873: severe reduction in lung capacity, chronic cough producing bloody sputum. Prognosis: terminal, six months to two years remaining.

**Mei-Lin, age 31:** Seven years at Patterson's. *Fei-gong* status eighteen months. Examination 10 May 1873: moderate lung damage, persistent dry cough. Prognosis: continued deterioration.

**Ah-Lin, age 19:** Three years at Patterson's. *Fei-gong* status six months. Examination 1 June 1873: early-stage occupational consumption, intermittent cough. Prognosis: progression to severe damage within two years if exposure continues.

All four examinations documented irreversible damage. All four workers should have died in that attic.

I have considered every possible explanation. None fit within my medical framework.

Dr. Harding dismisses my concerns. "The workers survived," he said. "The protocol succeeded."

But I am a doctor. I need to know how. I need to know why.

*30 June 1873*

Ah-Yong returned to full labor yesterday. I observed her at the looms this morning. She works with efficiency, but there is something different in the way she moves. A fluidity. She stands for hours without visible strain. Her breathing remains steady even in the steam and heat.

Patterson is pleased. Productivity is restored.

I should feel relief. Four workers survived what should have killed them.

Instead, I feel I have witnessed something I lack the language to describe. The fumigation ran its course. The sulfur did its work. And somehow, in the space where the protocol expected death, something else occurred.

I have added a note to each worker's file: *Full recovery following fumigation exposure. Mechanism unknown.*

The note is inadequate. All my documentation is inadequate to the thing I witnessed.

The pattern has been broken. I do not know what comes next.

## PART IV

**From the private ledger of Thomas Patterson**

*15 September 1873*

Three months since the fumigation. The recovered *fei-gong* workers continue to perform at full capacity. I have been tracking their output:

**Ah-Yong:** 127% of pre-*fei-gong* productivity.

**Old Chen:** 98% of his peak productivity from twenty years ago.

**Mei-Lin:** 115% of pre-*fei-gong* productivity.

**Ah-Lin:** 132% of pre-*fei-gong* productivity.

The numbers are undeniable. Whatever happened in that attic has been economically advantageous.

I am not a sentimental man. The workshop is a mechanism for converting raw material and human labor into profit. The *fei-gong* system exists because it is efficient. When a worker's productivity declines, we shift them to tasks that match their reduced capacity. This is not cruelty. This is management.

But the fumigation has disrupted the usual trajectory. I now have four workers whose capacity was restored at no cost to me. I did not have to recruit them. I did not have to train them. They simply woke up and returned to productivity.

Dr. Morrison has been asking questions. He wants to understand the mechanism. I have been polite but firm—the workers are healthy, they are working, the matter is closed.

I do not need to understand the mechanism. I need to understand the implications.

**Question one:** Can this be replicated?

The honest answer is: I do not know. If it could be replicated, every workshop in Guangzhou would be fumigating their *fei-gong* workers.

But it has not happened elsewhere. I have made discreet inquiries. Other workshops that underwent fumigation report no similar recoveries.

Something about my workshop, or these particular workers, or this particular fumigation produced a result that cannot be systematically reproduced. This makes it worthless as a general technique but valuable as a specific outcome.

**Question two:** How long will the recovery last?

The workers are back at the looms. They are breathing steam and dye vapor. They are doing everything that damaged their lungs the first time.

Will the damage return? Will they decline back to *fei-gong* status on the same five-year trajectory?

I am tracking this carefully. If the recovery is temporary, I need to extract maximum value before the decline resumes.

**Question three:** What do I owe them?

The workers did not choose to be fumigated. They were sealed in the workshop because the protocol categorized them as too damaged to evacuate. They survived something that should have killed them. And now they are back at the looms, producing silk at rates that exceed their pre-damage productivity.

The contract specifies wages based on status: full labor receives full wage, *fei-gong* receives half wage. I restored them to full labor status. This is procedurally correct.

But they are producing at 115-130% of baseline. Should their wages reflect this? The contract does not require it.

The recovery was not something I did to them. It was something that happened to them while under my supervision, in my workshop, as a result of a protocol I was required to enforce. They paid the cost—three days sealed in that attic, breathing sulfur, believing they would die.

The benefit accrues to me.

I have decided to do nothing. The wages remain at standard rates for full labor status. The productivity gains are absorbed into the workshop's general output.

But I find myself avoiding the workshop floor. I find myself taking the route to my office that does not pass the looms. I find myself uncomfortable with the sound of Ah-Yong's breathing—steady, easy, the breathing of someone whose lungs work perfectly.

The workshop spent four years taking her breath. The fumigation gave it back. And now the workshop is taking it again.

*22 September 1873*

Dr. Morrison came to my office today. He has been examining the recovered workers weekly.

"Their lung function remains perfect," he said. "No sign of new damage despite three months back at the looms."

"That's good news," I said.

"Is it?" He looked at me with that expression he gets—the missionary doctor trying to find the moral lesson in the medical data. "Mr. Patterson, these workers should be developing new damage. The same conditions that destroyed their lungs the first time are present. And yet they show no deterioration."

"Perhaps the recovery was more thorough than you initially thought."

"No. This is something else. It is as if their lungs are no longer susceptible to the damage."

I waited.

"If this is true," he continued, "then you have four workers who can labor indefinitely without declining to *fei-gong* status. Do you understand the implications?"

"I understand that I have four productive workers with time remaining on their contracts."

"You have four people whose bodies were destroyed by this workshop, who survived a protocol designed to kill them, and who are now producing value for you at rates that exceed anything you could have extracted from them before. And you are paying them standard wages."

"I am paying them the contractually specified wage for their status."

"Their status." Dr. Morrison's voice was tight. "They have no status for what they are now. The contract categories assume a trajectory. They are outside that trajectory."

"Dr. Morrison." I kept my voice level. "The economic management of this workshop is not your concern. The workers are healthy. They are working. They are being paid according to the terms they agreed to."

"I would have you recognize that the fumigation changed the terms. They paid a cost you did not anticipate. They survived something you expected to kill them. And now you are extracting value from that survival."

"The fumigation was a public health measure. I did not design the protocol. I was enforcing consular orders."

"Yes," he said quietly. "You were enforcing orders. You were following protocol. You were operating within—" He stopped. "And four people nearly died so that you could have more productive workers."

He left before I could respond.

I sat in my office for an hour afterward, looking at the ledger. The numbers are clear. The productivity gains are real. The profit is measurable.

Dr. Morrison is wrong about one thing: I did not design this outcome.

But he is right about another thing: I am benefiting from it.

The question is whether that benefit creates an obligation. The contract says it does not. The market says it does not.

But I cannot stop thinking about Ah-Yong's face when I told her about the fumigation. The way she nodded because what else could she do.

She paid the cost. I receive the benefit.

## PART V

**Six months after fumigation**

Ah-Yong finishes her shift and climbs to the attic. Her body does not hurt. This is still strange to her—the absence of pain, the ease of breathing. She has been back at full labor for six months, and her lungs still work perfectly.

The other *fei-gong* workers are there. Not the recovered ones—Mei-Lin, Chen, and Ah-Lin are still on the floor, working late. These are the workers who remain in *fei-gong* status. Six of them now.

They look at Ah-Yong the way they used to look at the full labor workers. From across a distance.

She sits by the window. The river is dark. The foreign ships are lit up, their lamps reflecting on the water.

The door opens. Mei-Lin enters, followed by Chen and Ah-Lin. They are the only recovered workers who still come to the attic after their shifts.

Mei-Lin sits beside Ah-Yong. "There was a man today," she says in Cantonese. "In the courtyard. Talking to the new workers."

"What man?"

"From the Mutual Aid Society. He was asking about the fumigation. About us."

The Mutual Aid Society is not officially a workers' organization. It is a benevolent association, registered with the consular authorities. But everyone knows it is more than that.

"What did he want?"

"He wanted to know if we would speak. At a meeting. Tell the other workers what happened."

Chen makes a sound—not quite disagreement, not quite concern. "If Patterson finds out we are talking to them, it could affect our contracts."

"They want us to testify," Mei-Lin says. "About the fumigation protocol. About how Dr. Harding's exception clause nearly killed us. They are building a case to challenge the consular medical authority."

"That will never succeed," Chen says.

"Probably not," Mei-Lin agrees. "But they want it on record."

Silence.

"If we testify," Ah-Yong says slowly, "what happens to our contracts?"

"The Mutual Aid Society cannot promise protection. If Patterson terminates early, they can offer legal assistance. But if he simply chooses not to renew..."

Ah-Yong has seven months remaining on her contract. Mei-Lin has eleven. Chen has fourteen. Ah-Lin has sixteen. These are not abstract numbers. They are months of wages. Months of money sent home to families who depend on it.

"I will think about it," Ah-Yong says finally.

---

The man from the Mutual Aid Society returns a week later. His name is Liang. He is Cantonese, in his forties, with the careful posture of someone who has learned to move through treaty port spaces without attracting attention.

He meets them in a teahouse outside the workshop district. Not secret—the consular police know about the Mutual Aid Society—but discreet.

Liang orders tea for everyone. He does not begin with the fumigation. He begins with questions about their work, their families, how long they have been in Guangzhou.

Finally, he says: "You survived something that should have killed you. The Mutual Aid Society is filing a petition with the consular court. We are arguing that Dr. Harding's protocol violated the basic duty of care that foreign employers owe to Chinese workers. We need you to testify."

"The court will dismiss it," Chen says.

"Probably," Liang agrees. "But the petition will be on record. When the next outbreak comes, when the next protocol is written, there will be a documented challenge."

"That is a long game," Ah-Lin says.

"Yes. But it is the only game we have."

Ah-Yong drinks her tea. She thinks about the fumigation. The smoke. The certainty of dying. The impossible survival.

"If we testify," she says, "what exactly are we saying?"

Liang pulls out a document. "You would be attesting to these facts: that you were sealed in the workshop during fumigation despite having compromised lungs. That you were not given the option to evacuate. That you were told the protocol was for your protection. That you believed you would die. That you survived only by chance. That you are now working at full capacity with no change to your contract terms."

Chen shakes his head. "If we testify about wages, Patterson will see it as a contract dispute. He will terminate."

"He might. But terminating workers who are producing at such high rates is economically irrational."

"In workshops that will know we challenged a contract," Ah-Lin says.

Liang has no answer to this.

"I understand the risk," Liang says finally. "Take the time you need. If you decide to testify, contact the Mutual Aid Society office."

He pays for the tea and leaves.

The four of them sit in silence.

Finally, Chen says: "We should not do this. The petition will be dismissed. Patterson will terminate our contracts. We will be unemployed in a city where no one will hire us. And nothing will change."

"Something might change," Mei-Lin says quietly.

"What?"

"There will be a record. The next time, workers will know they can challenge."

"The next time, workers will die. Just like we should have died. Our testimony will not save them."

He is right. The petition is symbolic. The testimony is symbolic.

But Ah-Yong keeps thinking about the smoke. The feeling of her lungs closing. The moment when she stopped counting breaths.

She survived. She does not know why. But she survived.

And now she is back at the looms, breathing steam and dye vapor, producing silk for Patterson, sending money home to her mother and brother. The contract has seven months remaining. After that, she does not know.

The fumigation was supposed to kill her. Instead, it gave her breath back. But breath is not freedom. Breath is just the capacity to keep working.

"I want to testify," Ah-Yong says.

The others look at her.

"I know the risks. I know we will probably lose. I know Patterson might terminate the contracts. But I want there to be a record. I want it documented that we were sealed in that workshop. That we were told it was for our protection. That we survived something designed to kill us."

"Why?" Chen asks.

"Because the next time, the workers should know. They should know what the protocol really means."

Mei-Lin nods slowly. "I will testify."

Ah-Lin says, "I will testify."

Chen looks at them. He is the oldest. He has been in Guangzhou the longest. He has seen more workers come and go, seen more contracts end, seen more bodies used up and discarded.

"I will testify," he says finally. "But I want it on record that I think this is a mistake."

"Noted," Ah-Yong says.

They finish their tea and return to the workshop. The evening shift is starting. The looms are clattering. The steam is rising. Everything is the same as it was this morning.

But something has changed. They have decided to speak.

The fumigation was supposed to kill them quietly. Instead, they will testify loudly.

It may not matter. But it will be documented.

## PART VI

**From the medical journal of Dr. James Morrison**

*15 March 1874*

Nine months since the fumigation. The recovered workers remain healthy. I have examined them monthly. No deterioration. No sign of new damage despite sustained exposure to the conditions that destroyed their lungs originally.

Each month, I examine them. Each month, I document their perfect health. Each month, I write in their files: *No medical reason to restrict labor.* And each month, Patterson uses my documentation to justify keeping them at the looms.

My medical records have become tools.

The Mutual Aid Society filed their petition last month. The four recovered workers testified. I was called to provide medical documentation of their condition before and after the fumigation.

I testified honestly. I presented my findings. I explained that the fumigation should have killed them, that their survival was medically inexplicable.

The consular court dismissed the petition. The judge ruled that Dr. Harding's protocol was within his medical authority, that the workers' survival proved the protocol was not inherently lethal.

This is circular logic. They survived, therefore the protocol was safe. The fact that they should not have survived—this was treated as irrelevant.

Patterson terminated their contracts two weeks ago.

Not immediately after the testimony. That would have been too obvious. He waited two weeks, then called each of them into his office and explained that he would not be renewing their contracts when they expired. He cited "changing business conditions" and "workforce restructuring." All procedurally correct.

All clearly retaliation.

I went to see him. I argued that terminating his most productive workers was economically irrational. He agreed. He said it was a difficult decision.

"Necessary for what?" I asked.

"For the precedent. If I keep workers who testify against consular authority, what message does that send? I cannot run a workshop under those conditions."

I left his office knowing I had failed. I documented what happened. I testified honestly. And it changed nothing.

The workers will complete their remaining contract months. Then they will leave.

They survived the fumigation. They will not survive the retaliation.

*20 April 1874*

Ah-Yong came to the hospital today. Not for an examination. She wanted to talk.

We sat in my office. She looked tired but not sick. Her lungs still work perfectly.

"Dr. Morrison," she said in careful English, "I want to ask you something."

"Of course."

"The fumigation. What happened to us. Do you understand it now?"

I considered lying. But she deserved honesty.

"No," I said. "I do not understand it. I have documented it. I have measured it. But I cannot explain it. Your lungs were destroyed. The fumigation should have completed that destruction. Instead, you woke up healed. I have no framework for this."

She nodded. "I thought so. You look at me like I am a puzzle you cannot solve."

"You are not a puzzle. You are a person who survived something impossible."

"Yes. Impossible." She was quiet for a moment. "But possible enough that I am alive. Possible enough that I breathed for nine months without pain. Possible enough that Patterson got nine months of my best work."

"I am sorry he terminated your contract."

"Are you?" She was not angry, just curious. "You testified. You told the truth. You said the fumigation should have killed us. But you also said we are healthy now. You said there is no medical reason we cannot work. Did you think about what Patterson would do with that information?"

I had thought about it. I had known my medical documentation was serving Patterson's interests. But what else could I do?

"I documented the truth," I said.

"Yes. The truth is that we are healthy. The truth is that we can work. The truth is that Patterson benefited from our survival. And the truth is that when we spoke about it, he terminated our contracts." She looked at me directly. "All of these things are true. But you only documented some of them."

"What should I have documented?"

"I do not know. Maybe nothing. Maybe there is no way to document what happened that does not serve the workshop."

She stood to leave. At the door, she paused.

"The fumigation did something to me," she said. "I do not know what. But I know it was not just my lungs. Something else changed."

"What do you mean?"

"In the attic, when the smoke was everywhere, I thought I was dying. I stopped counting breaths. I stopped trying to survive. And then... something let go. Something that was holding me."

"What was holding you?"

"I do not know. But it was not just the smoke." She smiled slightly. "And then I woke up and I was still in the workshop. Still in the contract. Still counting. But different."

"Different how?"

"Lighter. Like I could see the shape of—" She paused. "Like I could see things I could not see before." She opened the door. "Thank you for trying, Dr. Morrison."

After she left, I sat in my office for a long time. I thought about my documentation. My measurements. My testimony. All of it accurate. All of it true. All of it inadequate.

I have been documenting the pattern for two years. Five years of exposure, progressive respiratory failure, *fei-gong* transition. The fumigation broke that pattern. But it did not break the larger pattern.

Ah-Yong survived the fumigation. But she did not escape the workshop.

I do not know what to do with this knowledge.

*15 June 1874*

One year since I first wrote about the fumigation protocol. The cholera season is beginning again. Dr. Harding has distributed the same protocol. The exception clause remains unchanged.

If there is another outbreak, the *fei-gong* workers will be sealed in the workshops again. Most of them will die.

The Mutual Aid Society's petition is on record. It will not save them.

My documentation is on record. It will not save them.

The testimony of four workers who survived the impossible is on record. It will not save them.

**Final entry**

*25 June 1874*

Ah-Yong's contract ended yesterday. I went to the workshop to say goodbye. She was packing her belongings—not much, after five years. A few clothes. Some letters from her family. The money she has saved.

"Where will you go?" I asked.

"South. Back to Guangdong province. My mother is there. My brother."

"Will you work?"

"I do not know. Maybe. If someone will hire me." She folded a shirt carefully. "But I do not think I can go back to the looms. Not after this."

"Because of the fumigation?"

"Because I can breathe now. I know what it feels like. I cannot go back to counting breaths."

We stood in silence. I wanted to say something meaningful. Something that would acknowledge what she had survived, what she had testified to, what it had cost her.

Instead, I said: "I will continue documenting. Maybe someday it will matter."

"Maybe," she agreed. "But probably not."

She picked up her bag. We walked down the stairs together—the same stairs she used to climb while counting breaths, the same stairs she descended after the fumigation with lungs that worked perfectly.

"Yes?"

"I do not think it was the fumigation that healed us. I think it was something else. Something that happened when we stopped trying to survive. When we let go."

"Let go of what?"

"Everything. The counting. The contract. The workshop. All of it." She looked back at the building. "For three days, we were not workers. We were not *fei-gong*. We were not anything with a name. We were just bodies in smoke, dying. And then we were not dying. We were something else."

"What were you?"

"I do not know. But it was not what Patterson needed us to be. It was not what the contract said we were. It was something that had no category."

"But you are inside again now."

"Yes. But I remember. I remember what it felt like to be outside the categories. Even if I never get back there, I remember."

She walked toward the river. I watched her go. Her breathing was steady. Her steps were sure. She was walking away from the workshop where she had spent five years, where she had lost her breath and found it again, where she had been categorized and finally terminated.

She was walking toward something I could not see.

I returned to my office and wrote this final entry. I have documented the pattern for two years. I have documented the fumigation. I have documented the impossible recovery. I have documented the retaliation.

I have documented everything except the thing that matters most: what Ah-Yong felt in that attic when she let go. What it means to be outside the categories, even for three days. What it costs to remember that feeling while living inside what denies it.

I do not have the language for this. I do not have the framework.

The pattern continues. The workshops continue. The contracts continue. The workers are used up.

But somewhere in Guangdong province, a woman who survived the impossible is breathing freely. She remembers what it felt like to be outside. She remembers what it cost to speak. She remembers that the categories are not natural law.

I do not know if this matters. I do not know if memory is enough.

But it is what we have.
