# The Fumigation Records

## I.

**From the medical journal of Dr. James Morrison, London Missionary Society Hospital, Guangzhou**

*15 June 1873*

I have been documenting respiratory disease among the silk workers for two years now. The pattern holds with uncomfortable consistency: five years of exposure to steam, dyes, and the particular posture required at the looms, and the lungs begin their slow collapse. First the morning cough. Then the shortness of breath. Then the inability to stand a full shift.

The workshop managers call this natural wear. They have developed a category for it — *fei-gong*, "spent labor." When a worker can no longer maintain productivity, they are transitioned to lighter tasks: sorting raw silk, maintaining equipment, training new arrivals. The wage drops by half. The housing shifts to the attic spaces where summer heat makes breathing harder still.

I call it occupational consumption. The workshop produces silk. It also produces damaged lungs. Both are outputs of the same process.

The cholera has reached the delta. We see it every few years, but this outbreak moves faster than usual. The consular medical officer has already distributed his protocol: workshops with confirmed cases will be evacuated and fumigated. Workers relocated to temporary housing outside the city walls.

I have read the protocol's exception clause three times, hoping I misunderstood. Workers in *fei-gong* status — those with compromised respiratory systems — will not be evacuated. Dr. Harding's reasoning is documented with the clarity of a man who has never struggled to breathe: their damaged lungs make them high-risk for cholera mortality, and moving them would endanger the temporary housing population. They will remain in the sealed workshops during fumigation. Three days. Sulfur and lime.

I have written to object. I expect the response will be polite and final.

*22 June 1873*

The cholera reached Patterson's Number Two workshop yesterday. Consular police arrived this morning to enforce the protocol. I went to observe. Professional duty, I told myself. In truth, I went because I could not stay away.

## II.

The heat comes through the attic floorboards. Ah-Yong feels it before she opens her eyes — the weight of it on her chest, pressing down where her lungs already struggle. She counts her breaths while lying still. Twelve before the cough starts. Yesterday it was fifteen.

The other *fei-gong* workers are already awake. Mei-Lin sits by the small window, her face turned toward the river. Old Chen sorts through a basket of damaged cocoons, his hands moving with the automatic precision of thirty years' practice. No one speaks. Speaking costs breath.

Ah-Yong sits up slowly, testing her body's capacity. The tightness is there, always there now, a band around her ribs that tightens when she moves too quickly. She has thirteen months remaining on her contract. Thirteen months, and then she will be free to leave this place where she can no longer work, free to return to Guangdong province where no one will hire a woman whose lungs sound like tearing paper.

The cough is not just a symptom. It is a clock.

She descends the narrow stairs to the second floor, one hand on the wall. The morning shift workers are already at their stations. The sound fills the building — the hiss of steam, the rhythmic clatter of the looms, the splash of cocoons in hot water. She remembers that sound from inside her body, the way the rhythm would enter through her feet and travel up her spine. Now she hears it from outside. She is *fei-gong*. Spent labor. The workshop has already extracted what it needed from her body.

In the sorting room, she takes her place at the long table. Raw silk arrives in baskets from the cocoon vats. Her job is to separate the usable thread from the waste. Her hands know this work. They move while her mind counts breaths, tracks the quality of air, measures the heat.

Patterson appears in the doorway at mid-morning. His presence changes the room's atmosphere. Everyone straightens slightly, though no one looks directly at him.

"Ah-Yong." His Cantonese is adequate but carries the accent of a man who learned the language for business, not intimacy. "Come with me, please."

She follows him to his office on the first floor. The air is better here — the windows face the river, and the coal smoke rises rather than settling. She allows herself three deeper breaths before he begins speaking.

"There is cholera in the workshop." He says this in English now, the language of official matters. "Consular police will come today. You understand the protocol?"

"Yes, sir." Her English is halting but functional. She learned it because the contract is written in English, because Patterson's instructions come in English, because the language that binds her is not the language she speaks to the other workers in the attic.

"The healthy workers will go to temporary housing. You and the other *fei-gong* will remain here during fumigation. Three days. The medical officer says this is safest."

The tightness in her throat has nothing to do with her lungs now. "I stay in workshop. During fumigation."

"Yes. You'll be given cloth to cover your faces. The medical officer assures me the procedure is safe." He pauses. "It's for your own protection, Ah-Yong. Your lungs are already compromised. Cholera would kill you faster than the healthy workers. This way, you're isolated from infection risk."

She nods because nodding costs no breath. Because what else can she do? The protocol is written in English, enforced by consular police, designed by a medical officer who has never spent a night in the attic where heat makes breathing an act of will.

"You understand?" Patterson asks.

"Yes, sir. I understand protocol."

He looks relieved. "Good. The police will arrive this afternoon. Gather whatever personal items you need for three days. Stay in the attic until they come."

She climbs the stairs slowly, counting. Eighteen breaths for three flights. Yesterday it was sixteen. The cough comes when she reaches the top, doubling her over. Mei-Lin looks up from the window but says nothing. What is there to say?

Ah-Yong sits on her sleeping mat and tries to calculate. Three days sealed in the building. Sulfur smoke. The cloth they give her to cover her face — will it be enough? Can she breathe through cloth for three days when she can barely breathe through clear air?

The workshop has been taking her breath for four years. Now the protocol will take what remains.

---

The consular police arrive at noon. Six Sikh officers in their distinctive uniforms, led by a British sergeant who speaks no Cantonese. They carry lists. They are efficient.

The healthy workers are assembled in the courtyard. Ah-Yong watches from the attic window as they file out, carrying their belongings. Some look up at the attic. Most do not. What is there to see? The *fei-gong* workers have already been categorized, their fate determined by medical logic they cannot contest.

Dr. Morrison arrives with the police. Ah-Yong recognizes him — he runs the missionary hospital, treats workers when they can afford his fees. Sometimes when they cannot. She has seen him in the workshop before, asking questions, writing in his notebook. He argues with the British sergeant now, his voice carrying up to the attic window though she cannot make out the words.

The sergeant shakes his head. Dr. Morrison argues more forcefully. The sergeant says something final and turns away.

Dr. Morrison looks up at the attic. Their eyes meet across the distance. He raises one hand, a gesture she cannot interpret. Apology? Acknowledgment? Then he follows the healthy workers toward the city gate.

The workshop empties. The sound changes — no more looms, no more steam, no more voices. Just the *fei-gong* workers in the attic and the police outside, sealing the doors.

Old Chen distributes the wet cloths. "Cover your face when the smoke comes," he says in Cantonese. "Breathe shallow. We survive three days, then it's over."

Mei-Lin laughs, a sound like breaking glass. "Three days. Maybe we die here."

"Maybe," Chen agrees. "But maybe not. We see."

Ah-Yong ties the cloth across her nose and mouth. It is already hard to breathe through it, and the fumigation has not yet begun. She sits by the window, watching the river. The afternoon light makes the water look clean, though she knows it is not. Nothing in Guangzhou is clean. The foreign ships, the workshops, the contracts — they all leave residue.

She thinks about her family in Guangdong province. Her mother, her younger brother. The money she sends them every month, half of what she used to earn before the *fei-gong* transition. After the contract ends, what then? She cannot do heavy work. Her lungs are ruined. The workshop is the only place that will employ her in this condition, and even here, she is categorized as spent.

The transformation happened so gradually she did not notice until it was complete. The first year, she was strong. The second year, the cough started but seemed minor. The third year, the shortness of breath. The fourth year, the *fei-gong* transition. Now she is something else, something the workshop has made her. Not quite sick enough to die. Not quite healthy enough to leave.

The smoke begins at sunset.

It comes through the floorboards first, white and acrid. Ah-Yong presses the wet cloth against her face and tries to breathe shallow, as Chen instructed. But shallow breathing is not enough. The smoke fills the attic. It fills her lungs. It fills the space behind her eyes.

She counts breaths. One. Two. Three. Each one tears. Each one costs. The cloth is useless. The smoke goes through it, into her, making itself at home in the damaged tissue of her lungs.

Mei-Lin is coughing. Chen is coughing. Ah-Yong is coughing. The sound fills the attic, competing with the hiss of the sulfur below.

The protocol is inside her now. The institution has made itself physical. This is what power feels like when it enters the body.

She loses time. Minutes or hours, she cannot tell. The smoke is everything. The heat is everything. Her lungs are collapsing, or maybe they collapsed years ago and she is only now noticing. Maybe the workshop killed her slowly and this is just the final accounting.

She thinks about her mother's face. Her brother's laugh. The river in Guangdong province where she learned to swim as a child. Before the contract. Before Guangzhou. Before her body became data in Dr. Morrison's journal.

The smoke thickens. Ah-Yong closes her eyes and tries to find breath in the spaces between smoke. There is none. There is only smoke and heat and the feeling of her lungs trying to close around something that cannot be expelled.

This is how it ends, she thinks. Not in the workshop's ordinary violence — the steam, the dyes, the years of standing — but in the protocol designed to protect her. The medical officer's mathematics made flesh in her throat.

She stops counting breaths. There is no point. The numbers are meaningless when the thing being measured is running out.

## III.

**From the medical journal of Dr. James Morrison, London Missionary Society Hospital, Guangzhou**

*25 June 1873*

The fumigation is complete. I accompanied Dr. Harding and the consular police when they unsealed Patterson's Number Two workshop this morning.

We expected casualties. The protocol's exception clause is, in my professional opinion, a death sentence dressed in public health language. Three days of sulfur exposure for workers whose lungs are already compromised — I wrote this in my objection, though I knew it would be overruled.

We found the *fei-gong* workers in the attic. All four alive.

This is where my medical training fails me. I have documented respiratory disease among silk workers for two years. I know what those lungs look like, what they can withstand. Three days of sulfur fumigation should have killed them. The mathematics are clear.

They were unconscious when we entered, collapsed near the window. But breathing. All of them breathing.

Ah-Yong was the first to wake. I knelt beside her, checking her pulse, her respiration. Both weak but steady. I expected her to cough — the cough is constant in *fei-gong* workers, the sound of damaged tissue trying to clear itself. She did not cough.

"Can you hear me?" I asked in Cantonese.

She opened her eyes. They focused on my face with a clarity I had not seen in her before. She sat up slowly, testing her body. Then she took a breath — a full breath, deep and easy.

"The smoke," she said. "It's gone."

I checked her lungs. No wheezing. No crackling. The signs of occupational consumption that I had documented in her file four months ago — gone.

This is impossible. Respiratory damage does not heal. Certainly not overnight. Certainly not after three days of sulfur exposure that should have destroyed what little lung function remained.

The other three workers showed the same impossible recovery. Old Chen, whose file documented thirty years of progressive respiratory failure. Mei-Lin, whose cough I had heard from across the hospital ward. All of them breathing easily now, as if the fumigation had somehow reversed years of degradation instead of accelerating it.

Dr. Harding is pleased. He sees it as validation of his protocol — the *fei-gong* workers survived precisely because they were isolated from cholera exposure. He does not find their recovery noteworthy. He did not examine them before the fumigation. He does not know what their lungs were like three days ago.

I know. I have the documentation. I have the measurements. I have two years of data showing that occupational consumption is progressive and irreversible.

Something happened in that attic. Something the protocol could not predict and cannot measure. The workshop spent four years destroying Ah-Yong's lungs. The fumigation should have completed the work. Instead—

I have no category for this. No medical framework. The data says one thing. The patient in front of me says another.

Patterson has already reassigned Ah-Yong to full labor status. Her productivity is restored. She will return to the looms tomorrow. The contract has thirteen months remaining.

I asked her how she feels. She smiled — a strange, distant expression. "Light," she said in Cantonese. "Like something that was holding me has let go."

I asked if she remembered anything from the fumigation. She shook her head. "Just smoke. And then waking up."

I am writing this because I do not know what else to do with it. The pattern I have been documenting — five years of exposure, progressive respiratory failure, *fei-gong* transition — has been disrupted by an intervention I cannot explain. The protocol that should have killed them somehow restored them.

Or perhaps it was not the protocol at all. Perhaps it was something that happened inside the constraint itself, in those three days when the institution sealed them in with its violence and expected them to die quietly.

I do not know. I have measurements and observations, but I do not know.

*30 June 1873*

Ah-Yong returned to full labor yesterday. I observed her at the looms this morning. She works with the efficiency of someone who has done this for years, but there is something different in the way she moves. A fluidity. An ease.

The other *fei-gong* workers show the same change. They have all been returned to full labor status. Patterson is pleased. Productivity is restored. The contract system continues.

I should feel relief. Four workers survived what should have killed them. They are working again, earning full wages.

Instead, I feel I have witnessed something I lack the language to describe. The workshop extracts labor. The protocol extracts compliance. Both systems assume bodies are predictable, measurable, containable.

But something happened in that attic that neither system could detect. The fumigation ran its course. The sulfur did its work. And somehow, in the space where the protocol expected death, something else occurred.

I have added a note to Ah-Yong's file: *Full recovery following fumigation exposure. Mechanism unknown. Recommend continued observation.*

The note is inadequate. The file is inadequate. All my documentation is inadequate to the thing I witnessed.

She is back at the looms now. The contract has thirteen months remaining. The workshop will extract what it can. The system will continue.

But I saw her face when she took that first full breath. I saw what the protocol could not measure.

The pattern has been broken. I do not know what comes next.
