# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION OUTPUT

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT DESCRIPTION

### Setting

**Guangzhou, 1873 CE — The Pearl River Delta Silk Workshops**

The foreign concessions have been operating for thirty years now. The silk workshops of Guangzhou occupy a peculiar legal space: technically Chinese territory, but the contracts that govern them are enforced by the consular courts. The workshops themselves are owned by European trading houses, but the labor — the actual work of unwinding cocoons, spinning thread, weaving cloth — remains in Chinese hands.

The buildings cluster along the Pearl River's northern bank. Narrow three-story structures with the machinery on the ground floor, living quarters above. The sound is constant: the hiss of steam, the clatter of looms, the splash of cocoons being sorted in hot water. The air tastes of wet silk and coal smoke.

Workers live in the workshops. This is not unusual — many Guangzhou trades operate this way. What makes the silk workshops different is the contract system. Workers sign five-year indentures. Room, board, and a small wage in exchange for exclusive labor. The contracts are recorded at the consular office. They cannot be broken without mutual consent.

The system works, after a fashion. The workshops produce high-quality silk. The workers have shelter and steady income in a city where both are uncertain. The contracts provide structure in an economy disrupted by the Taiping Rebellion's aftermath and the opening of treaty ports.

But the contracts have a peculiar feature. After five years of work — exposure to steam, dyes, and the particular strain of standing at a loom ten hours daily — many workers develop respiratory conditions. The contracts specify that workers who become "medically unfit" transition to a different category: *fei-gong* (廢工, "spent labor"). They move to lighter tasks — sorting raw silk, maintaining equipment, training new workers. The wage drops by half. The housing remains, but in the attic spaces where the ceilings are low and the summer heat is worst.

This transition is understood as natural. The work is hard. Bodies wear down. The *fei-gong* category provides continued employment for those who can no longer perform full labor. It is, the workshop managers explain, a form of care.

The city's medical missionaries see it differently. Dr. Morrison at the London Missionary Society hospital has been documenting respiratory disease among silk workers. His records show the pattern clearly: five years at the looms, then the chronic cough, then the transition to *fei-gong* status. He has written to the consular authorities suggesting the workshop conditions themselves cause the illness. The consular response was polite and noncommittal. The contracts are legal. The workers signed voluntarily.

In the summer of 1873, an outbreak of cholera reaches the workshops. The disease spreads through the Pearl River Delta every few years, but this outbreak is particularly severe. The Chinese authorities implement traditional quarantine measures — infected households are sealed, marked with red paper. The foreign concessions implement their own protocol, developed by the consular medical officer: any workshop with confirmed cholera cases is to be evacuated and fumigated. Workers are relocated to temporary housing outside the city walls until the building is cleared.

But the protocol has an exception clause. Workers in *fei-gong* status — those with pre-existing respiratory conditions — are not evacuated. The medical reasoning is documented: their compromised lungs make them high-risk for cholera mortality, and moving them would expose the temporary housing population to greater danger. Instead, they remain in the sealed workshop during fumigation. The fumigation process uses sulfur and lime. It takes three days. The workers are provided with wet cloth to cover their faces.

The protocol is enforced by the consular police — Sikh officers employed by the British consulate. They are efficient and impartial. When Dr. Morrison objects, citing the fumigation's respiratory effects, he is told the protocol balances population health against individual risk. The mathematics are sound.

### Constraint Naturalizations

**The Contract Bond** *(C₁)*

The five-year indenture that binds workers to the silk workshops. Recorded at the consular office, enforced by consular courts. Cannot be broken without the workshop owner's consent — and consent is rarely given before the term expires. The contract provides room, board, and wage. It also specifies the transition to *fei-gong* status for workers who become "medically unfit."

From the worker's position: You live in the workshop. Your family is in Guangdong province, three days' travel away. The contract forbids extended absence. You signed for five years because the alternative was uncertain day-labor in a city still recovering from rebellion. The wage supports your family. The work is hard, but it is steady. After three years, you develop the cough. After four years, you can no longer stand full shifts at the loom. The manager is sympathetic. He moves you to *fei-gong* status. Your wage drops by half. You remain in the workshop, in the attic where the heat makes the cough worse. You cannot leave — the contract still has a year remaining, and even after it expires, where would you go with lungs that cannot sustain full labor?

From the owner's position: The contracts provide stable labor for complex work. Training a silk worker takes months. The five-year term ensures return on that investment. The *fei-gong* system is humane — it provides continued employment for workers whose bodies cannot sustain full labor. The reduced wage reflects reduced productivity. The workers signed voluntarily. They can leave when the contract expires. The system provides order in a city where labor is mobile and unreliable.

**The Transformation of Bodies** *(C₂)*

The process by which five years at the looms converts a healthy worker into *fei-gong*. The steam, the dyes, the posture, the hours. The transition is gradual — first the morning cough, then the shortness of breath, then the inability to stand full shifts. The managers track this carefully. When productivity drops below a threshold, the worker is moved to *fei-gong* status.

This is understood as natural wear. The work is hard. Bodies degrade. The *fei-gong* category acknowledges this reality and provides continued employment. Dr. Morrison calls it "occupational consumption." The workshop managers call it "the cost of the trade."

From the worker's position: You feel yourself changing. The cough that started in year three is now constant. Your chest is tight. The attic heat makes it worse. You are *fei-gong* now — spent labor. You sort raw silk, train new workers, maintain equipment. You earn half your former wage. You cannot return to full labor. Your body will not allow it. The contract has another year. After that, you will be free to leave, but leave to what? Your lungs are ruined. You cannot do heavy work. The workshop is the only place that will employ you in this condition.

From the outside observer's position: Dr. Morrison has been tracking this for two years. The pattern is clear. Five years of exposure, then respiratory failure. The *fei-gong* category is not a kindness — it is the systematic conversion of healthy labor into damaged labor, then the retention of that damaged labor at reduced cost. The workers are not informed of this trajectory when they sign. They learn it by living it. The transformation is irreversible. The workshop produces silk and produces *fei-gong*. Both are outputs of the same process.

**The Fumigation Protocol** *(C₃)*

The consular medical officer's cholera response procedure. When cholera is confirmed in a workshop, the building is evacuated and fumigated with sulfur and lime. Workers are relocated to temporary housing outside the city walls. The fumigation takes three days.

Exception: Workers in *fei-gong* status remain in the sealed building during fumigation. The medical reasoning is documented — their compromised lungs make them high-risk for cholera mortality, and evacuating them would endanger the temporary housing population. They are provided with wet cloth to cover their faces.

The protocol is enforced by consular police. It is not negotiable.

From the worker's position: The cholera comes in the summer heat. The workshop is sealed. You are told you cannot leave — your lungs are too damaged, you would spread disease to the healthy workers in the temporary housing. You will remain here during the fumigation. Three days. They give you wet cloth for your face. The sulfur smoke is thick. You cannot breathe through the cloth. Your chest is on fire. You think you will die here. Perhaps you will. The protocol is clear. The police are outside. There is no appeal.

From the medical officer's position: The protocol is sound. Cholera mortality is highest among those with compromised respiratory systems. Moving *fei-gong* workers to temporary housing would create a high-risk population cluster. The fumigation is necessary to eliminate the disease vector. The sulfur exposure is temporary. The wet cloth provides adequate protection. The mathematics of population health require difficult decisions. Individual suffering must be weighed against collective risk. The protocol minimizes total mortality. It is regrettable but necessary.

### Character Positions

**Ah-Yong** (X₁) — *Fei-gong* worker, 28 years old, fourth year of contract

Ah-Yong came to Guangzhou from Guangdong province in 1869. She signed the five-year contract because her family needed steady income. The workshop provided that. The first two years were hard but manageable. The third year, the cough started. By the fourth year, she could no longer stand full shifts. The manager moved her to *fei-gong* status. Her wage dropped from 4 taels monthly to 2 taels. She moved to the attic quarters.

She lives in the workshop. Her contract has one year remaining. Her lungs are damaged. She cannot do heavy labor. She cannot leave — where would she go? The workshop is the only place that will employ her in this condition. She sorts raw silk now, trains new workers. The heat in the attic makes the cough worse. She sleeps poorly.

When the cholera comes, she is told she cannot evacuate. Her lungs are too damaged. She would endanger the healthy workers. She will remain in the sealed workshop during fumigation. Three days with sulfur smoke. She is given wet cloth for her face.

**Mr. Patterson** (X₂) — Workshop owner, 45 years old, British

Patterson owns three silk workshops in Guangzhou. He arrived in 1865, after the Treaty of Tianjin opened the interior to foreign trade. The workshops are profitable. The contracts provide stable labor. The silk quality is high.

He believes the contract system is fair. The workers sign voluntarily. They receive steady wages and housing. The *fei-gong* system is humane — it provides continued employment for workers whose bodies cannot sustain full labor. The reduced wage reflects reduced productivity. When contracts expire, workers are free to leave.

He does not see the transformation of bodies as extraction. The work is hard. Bodies wear down. This is the nature of labor. The *fei-gong* system acknowledges this reality and provides accommodation. It is better than dismissal.

He is not directly involved in the fumigation protocol. That is a medical matter, handled by the consular authorities.

**Dr. Morrison** (X₃) — Medical missionary, London Missionary Society, 38 years old, Scottish

Morrison has been in Guangzhou since 1868. He runs a small hospital in the Chinese quarter. He treats workshop workers frequently — respiratory disease, repetitive strain injuries, burns from the dye vats.

Two years ago, he began systematically documenting the respiratory disease pattern. The data is clear: five years at the looms, then chronic respiratory failure. The *fei-gong* category is not accommodation — it is the endpoint of systematic bodily destruction. The workers are not informed of this trajectory when they sign contracts.

He has written to the consular authorities. He has written to the workshop owners. The responses are polite and noncommittal. The contracts are legal. The workers signed voluntarily. The *fei-gong* system provides continued employment.

He sees the transformation clearly. The workshop produces silk and produces damaged lungs. Both are outputs of the same process. The system is not natural wear — it is industrial disease. But he cannot stop it. He has no authority over the contracts. He can only document and treat.

When the fumigation protocol is implemented, he objects strenuously. Sealing *fei-gong* workers in a building with sulfur smoke will kill them. The medical officer's reasoning is sound in the abstract — compromised lungs increase cholera risk — but the fumigation itself is a death sentence for workers whose lungs are already damaged. He is overruled. The protocol is enforced.

**Dr. Harding** (X₄) — Consular Medical Officer, 42 years old, English

Harding developed the fumigation protocol in 1871, after the previous cholera outbreak. The protocol is based on established public health principles: isolate infection vectors, eliminate disease reservoirs, protect healthy populations.

The exception for *fei-gong* workers is medically justified. Cholera mortality is highest among those with compromised respiratory systems. Evacuating them to temporary housing would create a high-risk cluster. The fumigation is necessary to eliminate the disease vector. The sulfur exposure is temporary. The wet cloth provides adequate protection.

He understands Dr. Morrison's objection. The fumigation is harsh for workers with damaged lungs. But the alternative is worse — allowing cholera to spread through the temporary housing population. The protocol minimizes total mortality. Individual suffering must be weighed against collective risk.

The mathematics are sound. The protocol is enforced by consular police. It is not subject to appeal.

### Linguistic Strategy: Code-Switching (Cantonese/Institutional English)

**Languages:**
- **Cantonese:** Spoken among workers, in private spaces, in moments of intimacy or distress
- **Institutional English:** Used in all interactions with workshop management, consular authorities, medical officers

**Social contexts:**
- **Cantonese signals:** Intimacy, shared vulnerability, private speech among those with no power
- **English signals:** Performance of compliance, interaction with power structures, official discourse

**Code-switching marks power boundaries.** When Ah-Yong speaks to other workers, she speaks Cantonese. When she speaks to Mr. Patterson or the consular police, she speaks halting English. The switch is not a choice — it is a structural requirement. English is the language of the contracts, the protocol, the official world that determines her life. Cantonese is the language of the life that exists in the gaps.

Dr. Morrison speaks both. He learned Cantonese to treat patients. When he writes to the consular authorities, he writes in English. When he argues with Dr. Harding, he argues in English. When he sits with dying workers, he speaks Cantonese. The switching reveals who he is addressing and what he believes is possible in each language.

**Grammatical features carried into English from Cantonese:**
- Topic-prominent structure: "The contract, cannot break" (contract as topic, comment follows)
- Aspect markers maintained: "I work-ing four year already" (experiential aspect -guo carried as "already")
- Measure word retention in English: "Two-tael wage only" (measure word "tael" used where English would omit)

**Example vocabulary:**
- *Fei-gong* (廢工): "Spent labor" — workers transitioned to reduced status after bodily degradation
- *Gong-zuo* (工作): "Work" — but carries connotation of contracted obligation, not free labor
- *Bing* (病): "Illness" — but in this context, specifically the occupational respiratory disease
- *Yan* (煙): "Smoke" — both the workshop coal smoke and the fumigation sulfur smoke
- *Hop-tong* (合同): "Contract" — the legal document that binds workers to the workshop

### Inhabitation Sentences

**Ah-Yong (X₁):** You are a woman whose body is being systematically destroyed by the work that feeds your family, and the language you speak to the people who own your contract is not the language you speak to the people who share your ruin.

**Mr. Patterson (X₂):** You are a man who genuinely believes the contract system is fair because from where you stand — profiting from stable labor, never breathing the steam or the sulfur — it is.

**Dr. Morrison (X₃):** You are a physician who can see the pattern of destruction clearly, who can document it and name it, but who has no power to stop it, and the gap between seeing and acting is the shape of your days.

**Dr. Harding (X₄):** You are a medical officer who makes sound decisions based on population mathematics, and the fact that those decisions require sealing damaged workers in buildings with sulfur smoke is regrettable but necessary, and you sleep well because the protocol minimizes total mortality.

### RESOLVED

**Ω_E01: Historical accuracy of silk workshop contracts in 1870s Guangzhou**
- **Question:** Did foreign-owned silk workshops in Guangzhou use binding multi-year contracts?
- **Resolution:** Yes. Treaty port industries commonly used indenture contracts for skilled labor. The specific five-year term and *fei-gong* transition are naturalized constructs, but the contract structure is historically grounded.
- **Impact:** Setting is historically plausible. Contract system can serve as naturalized C₁.

**Ω_E02: Cholera fumigation protocols in 1870s treaty ports**
- **Question:** Would consular authorities implement fumigation protocols with sulfur/lime?
- **Resolution:** Yes. Sulfur fumigation was standard cholera response in the 1870s. The specific exception for compromised workers is a naturalized construct, but the fumigation practice is historically accurate.
- **Impact:** C₃ (fumigation protocol) is historically grounded. Exception clause is plausible extension.

**Ω_E03: Occupational respiratory disease in silk workshops**
- **Question:** Would steam and dye exposure cause chronic respiratory disease over five years?
- **Resolution:** Yes. Historical records document respiratory disease among textile workers exposed to steam, dyes, and particulate matter. The five-year timeline is plausible.
- **Impact:** C₂ (bodily transformation) is materially grounded. The *fei-gong* transition naturalizes the constraint structure.

**Ω_C01: Definition of *fei-gong* status**
- **Question:** Should *fei-gong* be a formal category or informal practice?
- **Resolution:** Formal category. Specified in contracts, tracked by management, determines wage and housing. This makes the constraint structure explicit and legible.
- **Impact:** C₁ and C₂ coupling is visible. The transition is documented, not hidden.

**Ω_C02: Role of consular police in enforcement**
- **Question:** Should fumigation protocol be enforced by consular police or workshop management?
- **Resolution:** Consular police. This separates C₃ enforcement from the workshop ownership structure, naturalizing X₄'s institutional position as distinct from X₂'s economic position.
- **Impact:** X₄ (medical officer) is structurally independent from X₂ (owner). The protocol is institutional, not economic.

**Ω_P01: Should Dr. Morrison be missionary or secular physician?**
- **Question:** What institutional position gives Morrison analytical capacity without enforcement power?
- **Resolution:** Medical missionary. Provides access to worker population, documentation capacity, and moral authority to object — but no legal authority over contracts or protocols.
- **Impact:** X₃'s analytical position (π=1.15) is naturalized through institutional role. Can see pattern clearly, cannot stop it.

**Ω_P02: Linguistic strategy selection**
- **Question:** Code-switching (Cantonese/English) vs. Historical English register?
- **Resolution:** Code-switching. The power differential is marked by language boundary. Workers speak Cantonese among themselves, English to authorities. The switching itself performs the constraint structure.
- **Impact:** Power asymmetry is naturalized through linguistic practice. The switch signals who you are talking to and what is possible in that interaction.

**Ω_P03: Should the fumigation be survivable?**
- **Question:** Does Ah-Yong survive the fumigation, or is it terminal?
