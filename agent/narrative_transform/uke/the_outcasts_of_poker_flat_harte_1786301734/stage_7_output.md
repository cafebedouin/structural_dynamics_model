# Section 1: The Revised Narrative

# The True-Weight

The plate is cold at dawn. I don't know why this still surprises me, weeks into the tally room, but it does — the brass holds the night's chill long after the deck timbers have started to breathe with the first heat, and when I set the weights in their cups my fingers ache a little, a small clean ache, nothing like the ache of rope-work. I have decided I prefer it. It is the only ache on this ship that means exactly what it says.

Voss comes at first light, same as always, boots first down the companion ladder, and he doesn't speak to me beyond the nod. I set the water skins on the plate — the day's draw from the forward cask — and the needle swings out wide, hangs a moment like it's thinking, then finds its rest with a small definite click. Twenty-two pounds, four ounces. I say it aloud because that's the job. Voss repeats it, louder, so the deck can hear, and writes it in his own book, and that is the whole of the ceremony. It happens every morning. It has never once been wrong.

I used to stand where the deckhands stand, off to the side with the others, watching the mates do this. Now I stand at the scale itself, and the difference is not what I expected. I thought closeness would explain something. It has only let me see more clearly that there is nothing here to explain.

The cups I use were somebody else's cups. There's a worn place on the brass rim, thumb-shaped, that isn't from my thumb — I noticed it the first week and have gone on noticing it since, a small polished hollow where a hand rested every morning for longer than I've been alive on this ship. I fit my own thumb there sometimes without meaning to. It doesn't fit exactly. Close enough that I stopped trying to decide whether that meant anything.

Osan Duvray hauls the water skins for me most mornings, because that was his task before it was anyone's and no one told him to stop. He sets them down careful, always the same care, like the skins might complain if he were rough. He has steady hands. I have watched a lot of hands on this crossing and his are among the steadiest — no shake in them even when the swell is bad, even when Thess has us reefing sail in the dark with the wind coming sideways off the shoal water. I used to think steadiness like that meant something on a ship. I no longer think it means anything at all, but I haven't stopped noticing it, because noticing is the only thing my new post has actually given me.

"She had *sûreté* till the shoal," Farro said to me once, of the quartermaster's mate whose place I took. We were coiling line and she said it the way you'd say the wind had backed a point — flat, over her shoulder, not looking at me. "Then she didn't. Then she went to the sack." A pause, the kind that isn't really a pause, just the shape talk takes when nobody wants to fill it further. "Good with a needle, that one. Sound as a bell."

I didn't ask what had made her unsound between one dusk and the next. I have learned, these weeks, that this is not a question anyone on the *Sesostris* answers, because it is not a question anyone has an answer to. Not even, I think, Voss.

I found her name once, by accident, penciled small in the margin of an old tally sheet still folded under the scale's base plate where nobody had thought to clear it out — *Imke*, and under it a column of numbers in a hand steadier than mine, going back further than my memory of this ship goes. I didn't ask Farro if that was her. I didn't need to. The hand matched the thumb-worn place on the cup.

---

The ration mark is a notch filed into the measuring dipper, deep enough to feel with a thumbnail in the dark. Farro takes her water at that mark every third day like everyone on general ration, and I watch her count it against something I can't see — her lips move, small, almost nothing, over the dipper before she drinks, like she's saying a private grace, except I've watched her mouth long enough now to know it isn't a grace. It's numbers. She's counting the hold against the ration, the manifest against what actually reaches her hand, and she has been doing this, I think, for longer than I've had the eyes to notice it.

I know what the hold holds. That's my job now too — Voss walks me through the manifest most weeks, more from boredom than kindness, and I've seen the figures: stores laid in against a shorter crossing than the one the House ended up giving us, stretched thin over the difference. I've also seen the spare reserve figure, penciled separate, in Thess's hand, and I've never once seen anyone draw against it in daylight.

I said none of this to Farro. There is a way of saying nothing that isn't cowardice, exactly, though I don't have a better name for it. She has seen what saying something costs. She told me that once, in almost those words, and did not elaborate, and I did not ask her to, because by then I already understood that elaboration was the expensive part.

---

The middle of the crossing has no coastline in it, no headland, nothing the eye can use to measure distance made good. Just water repeating itself in every direction, the same blue going gray going black and back to blue, day on day, until the ship feels less like a vessel moving through something and more like the only fixed point in a world that has agreed to hold still around it out of spite. Nobody curses the sea here. You don't curse weather. It can't hear you and wouldn't care, and everyone on this ship learned that lesson somewhere before I came aboard, the way you learn a knot so young you can't remember your hands not knowing it.

I noticed the lock on the spare reserve cask sitting a quarter-turn off true on a morning when Thess had stood the middle watch. I noticed, too, some weeks later, that the reserve's level sat a hand's width lower than the figure in Thess's own pencil — a thing I am not, strictly, supposed to check, though the tally room key ring includes it because the previous mate's ring included it and no one thought to take it off. I noticed both of these things with the same flat attention I give the true-weight scale each dawn, and I did nothing further with either noticing.

I told myself there wasn't a door to open. That was true and also not the whole of it. The rest of it was that I could see exactly how such a door closes, once opened, and on whose fingers, and I chose the noticing without the door over the door with whatever waited behind it. I want to call that a decision, because it was one — I stood at the reserve cask longer than I needed to, that morning, turning the lock a quarter-turn back true with my thumb before I let it go again, both ways, back and forth, before I left it exactly as I'd found it and went up the ladder. Nobody saw that. It changed nothing. I have not gone back to the reserve cask since, and I don't think that not-going is innocent, whatever else it is.

Thess has keys nobody audits. That is simply a fact about the ship, like the doldrums being windless, like the water tasting of the cask by the fourth week no matter how sweet it went in.

---

Voss reads the true-weight with his eyes on the needle, always, every single dawn — that part of him never moves, never wavers, whatever the number, whether it's the water ration or the day's catch weighed for the salt-barrels or, once now since I've stood this close, a body's worth of decision made without a scale anywhere near it. We came near the doldrum flats in the fifth week, wind dying to nothing a full day before anyone said the word aloud, and on the morning the captain came down from his cabin to stand beside Voss at the mast step, I watched the whole thing from twenty feet closer than I had ever stood to it before.

They didn't weigh anyone. I want to say that plainly because for one stupid, weightless second I thought they might — that the scale I trusted more than anything else on this ship might finally be asked to answer the only question that mattered, and instead I watched the captain and Voss walk the deck together, unhurried, the way you'd walk a field checking for stones, and I watched Voss's eyes go somewhere past the mast step entirely, somewhere out past the rail into the flat gray water, before he opened his mouth.

He said Duvray's name like he was reading the weight of the water. Same voice. Same flat competence.

I have turned that over more times than I can say and I cannot find the seam in it. Duvray is sound. He is, by every measure I have ever been permitted to take of anyone on this ship — the steadiness of his hands, the evenness of his work, the fact that he has never once been late to a watch or short in a task — every bit as sound as I am, and I am the one who was moved into the tally room, and he is the one they are giving water in a boat with no name for where he's going, only the word we all use, which came out of somebody's mouth on the foredeck light as anything, light as *pass the bread*: *he's for the sack.*

Nobody flinched saying it. Not one person on that deck, myself included, made a sound that wasn't already part of the ordinary noise of a ship going about its ordinary morning. I stood at the rail with the tally book still in my hand, open to the day's water figure, and I thought of the reserve cask, its lock a quarter-turn off true where I'd left it, and I thought: there it is again, the noticing that goes nowhere. It hadn't gone anywhere in the weeks since. It wasn't going anywhere now, standing at this rail, either. I put the book under my arm and went on with the morning because the morning went on regardless of what I did with my hands.

---

I went to the tally room after and I don't know what I meant to do there. I put my hand flat on the scale plate, which I do sometimes without cargo on it, out of a habit I've never examined, and the plate gave under my palm the exact small honest amount it always gives, the needle swinging out to read my own weight back at me in numbers that have never once lied to anybody about anything.

I thought: *this is the only true thing on the ship.*

I thought it the way you think something you've thought before without noticing you'd thought it. And on the heel of that thought came another, with no more heat in it than the first: the true-weight has never once decided anything. It only ever tells you what a thing weighs. Somebody else decides, afterward, in a room with no scale in it at all, what that weight is worth keeping.

I have known this, I think, since the day they moved me here. I don't know that knowing it has done anything for me except give the knowledge somewhere warm to sit.

I thought of Imke's hand in the tally sheet margin, the column of figures going back further than I could account for, and I understood, standing there with my palm still flat on the cold plate, that she had probably thought some version of this same thought, in this same room, more mornings than I would ever be given the chance to think it. It hadn't kept her at the scale. I didn't know yet whether it would keep me.

---

Duvray's boat went out in the last dead hour before the sun cleared the mast. Farro helped provision it — that's the quartermaster's job by rights, mine now, but she came and stood at my shoulder without being asked and started laying in the biscuit herself, quick and quiet, and I let her, because her hands were steadier than mine had any business claiming to be at that hour.

I filled the water skins from the tally-room cask, not the spare, and when I brought the second skin to the boat I let it run past the ration mark before I sealed it. Not much. A finger's width over what the mark allowed, maybe less. Nobody was measuring. Nobody but me would ever know the difference between what I gave him and what the ration said he was owed, and I want to be honest and say I don't know if it will matter to him at all, out there, however many days out there turns out to be. I filled it anyway. I have done this before, I realized, filling it — some smaller version of it, weeks back, the first time Farro drew her ration and I let the dipper tip a hair past the notch before I called the reading, and neither of us said anything, and I told myself after that it hadn't happened, because saying it had happened would have made it a thing that needed a name. It didn't have a name. It still doesn't. I sealed Duvray's skin and set it in the boat by his feet and he looked at me — first time he'd really looked at me in three weeks of hauling my water skins morning after morning — and he didn't say anything, and I didn't either, because there wasn't a word sized right for it, and we both knew that, and neither of us pretended otherwise.

Farro tucked an extra twist of dried fish under the biscuit sack where the mates wouldn't see it counted against the manifest. She did it without looking at me and I did not look at her doing it, which is its own kind of courtesy on a ship this small.

They put him over the side at the turn of the tide with no wind to speak of, and the boat sat a long while against the flat gray shine of the doldrum water before the current, or something, took it out past where the eye could hold it.

Voss watched it go with the same face he reads the true-weight with every dawn. I don't say that to accuse him of anything in particular. I say it because it's what I saw, and I have gotten very good, this crossing, at saying only what I saw.

---

The next morning I was at the scale before Voss came down the ladder. I set nothing on it — no cargo, no water skin, no name. Just my own hand, flat on the plate, the brass cold the way it always is at that hour, and I watched the needle answer with the same small honest number it would have given yesterday, or the reef-shoal morning, or the morning before I ever knew this room existed. It has never once lied to me. It was not lying now.

It had also never once, in all the mornings I have stood here, told anybody a single thing about whether they'd be kept.

My thumb found the worn place on the cup rim without my telling it to. I left it there a moment, in the hollow some other hand had made, and then I took the weights down for the day's water, and Voss's boots came down the ladder behind me right on their hour, and the ship went on, because the ship always goes on, flat water in every direction and nothing on it that owed either of us an explanation.
