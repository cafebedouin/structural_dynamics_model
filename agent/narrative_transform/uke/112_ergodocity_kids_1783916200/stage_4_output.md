# The Weight of a Season

The salt came up through my palms before I ever saw it — that was the first thing each year, before the heat, before the glare off the pan turned the whole world the color of a fired tablet. I would set my hands to the channel-gate in the dark hour before the others woke, and the wood would already be sweating brine, and by the time the sun cleared the Ashborne rooftops my palms would be dry and white-rimmed, like something had been written on them and then erased, leaving only the ghost of ink.

Thirty-four years I had been doing this. Thirty-four years of hands that cracked at the same three days in the dry season, that healed the same slow way, that knew the gate-wood better than they knew the inside of my own house.

The channels were mine to keep because my mother's mother had married into Waterkeep from Panhold — a *pakka* marriage, fired into the Stone two Accountings after the fact once the magistrate's clerk got around to it — and so I had come into the vansh sideways, the only door that had ever opened for anyone in living memory. Not chosen. Not earned. A door somebody had walked through eighty years ago and sealed the frame around afterward, so that only that one shape of person could ever pass again.

I did not think about this often. There was no use in it. The gates needed opening at dawn and closing before the second heat, and the goats needed the lower channel by midmorning or Nazneen's herd would crowd the Panhold cistern and there would be shouting, and shouting drew a Reckoner's apprentice faster than almost anything else, which meant it would go in the record, which meant —

Which meant I was already thinking about the record, wasn't I. Even in the dark hour. Even before anyone had come to ask.

---

They came in the second week after the last caravan, the way they always did — a Reckoner's apprentice, young, sunburned in the particular way of someone new enough to the desert not to have learned yet how to walk in it, carrying a lap-board of soft clay and a stylus tucked behind his ear like a Roadborn boy carries a knife.

His name was Divyansut, and he had the good manners to ask before he wrote anything.

"The lower gate," he said. "Widow Panhold's cistern. You diverted water to it in the fourth week of Ashadh."

"I did."

"Witnessed?"

"Her son. And Bhairon's boy, who was passing."

He wrote this down. Not what it had cost me — three nights of the upper field going dry a day earlier than it should have, my own barley thinner for it come harvest — only that it had happened, that it was witnessed, that it could be weighed against my name if anyone ever accused me of favoring my own field over the shared channel.

And this was the strange comfort of it, the thing I could never explain to my sister-in-law's people from Roadborn, who thought all this writing-down was an indignity: when Nazneen's cousin came at me in autumn shouting that I'd starved his goats to fatten Widow Panhold's favor, I could stand in the square and say *ask the Reckoners*, and the thing was already settled, already witnessed, already safe from the shape of his anger. The record was a wall I could stand behind. I had needed that wall more than once.

What the record did not ask, what Divyansut's stylus never touched, was the taste of that diverted water on my own tongue when I'd gone to check the flow at midnight, kneeling at the gate to feel with my fingers whether the level was right because I did not trust my eyes in the dark — the specific coldness of it, the specific ache in my knees from the kneeling, the exact quality of the relief when I heard the widow's goat, which had been dying, bawl at dawn like it meant to live after all.

None of that went into the clay. It couldn't. There was no mark for it. Divyansut moved down the channel-line asking his questions and I answered them and felt, in the same breath, seen and passed over, protected and thinned — the way a shadow is a true record of your shape and no record at all of your weight.

I did not say any of this to him. There was nothing to say. It was only the Accounting, come again, the way the heat comes, the way the caravan bells come down off the ridge in the cool season. You did not resent weather. You only felt it in your joints before it arrived.

---

The dispute over Farrukh's water-debt took me to the Reckoning House in the ninth week, and I went unwillingly, because Farrukh was kin to my husband's people and because Reckoning House business had a way of taking a whole morning that the channels could not spare.

The House stood apart from everything else in Kesh Varana — stone instead of clay-brick, and cool in a way that had nothing to do with season. I felt the change in the air on the threshold, the way you feel a channel's temperature shift where an underground spring feeds in. Piped water ran along a stone gutter at the base of the wall, drawn from the deep cistern before it ever reached the town cisterns the rest of us drew from. I had known this all my life and it had never once struck me as strange until that morning, standing in the doorway with salt still white in the creases of my knuckles, watching the water run clear and cold past the Reckoner-Second's sandaled feet.

Amrasut the Elder received the dispute himself, which was more courtesy than the matter deserved, except that Farrukh's father had once done him some service nobody now living could name precisely.

He listened to the whole complaint — that the tally recorded a debt of full measure repaid, when Farrukh insisted he had repaid only three parts of four, the fourth forgiven verbally by the man who was owed, now dead, no witness living — and he did not once look impatient, or bored, or as though the matter were beneath the House. His hands were remarkable to me in a way I did not expect. Smooth. Not soft — I do not mean soft, there was a strength in them, the strength of a man who has held a stylus steady for forty years — but unmarked. No salt-crust. No crack-lines at the knuckle. No trace at all of what a season does to a hand that works ground.

He drew the old tablet from the wall himself, turned it to the light, and traced the entry with one finger.

"The mark is here," he said. "Full measure. If the fourth part was forgiven, it should have been witnessed and entered as forgiven — a *kacha* forgiveness spoken and never fired becomes, in the Stone's eye, nothing at all. This is not cruelty. This is the only way a stranger at the pan's edge can trust that a debt marked settled is settled." He took up a fresh stylus, dampened the old tablet's face where the clay had not yet fully dried from that season's re-firing, and pressed a correction mark beside the entry — not erasing, clay does not forgive that way, but adding, a small notch that any Reckoner reading it after would understand as *disputed, resolved thus*. "There. Now it says what actually happened, as far as the stone can say it."

*As far as the stone can say it.* He said this without any irony at all, the way a man says a thing he has said a thousand times and believes entirely, and watching his still, sure hand make that mark I understood — not with words, I had no words for it then, only with something that sat wrong in my chest like swallowed water — that he believed the correction *was* the truth restored. That there was no gap in his mind between what had happened between Farrukh and a dead man in a season now three years gone, and this small notch in damp clay.

I could not fault his reasoning. It was sound reasoning. Without the mark, without the Stone, Farrukh's grandsons could be shaken down by anyone claiming an old unpaid debt, with no wall to stand behind. Amrasut was not wrong. I left the House no less certain of that than when I'd entered, and no less troubled, and could not have told you, walking home past the ash-quarter with the cool of the stone floor still on the soles of my feet, which of those two feelings was supposed to give way to the other.

---

It was on that same walk home, past the downwind quarter where the air always carried a thin dry smell I had stopped truly smelling years ago, that I saw Bhairon Ashborne closing a tally.

He was kneeling at the threshold of his own low clay house, and before him on a reed mat lay the last effects of old Devkishan, a Panhold elder three days dead, and a runner from the Reckoning House stood at a respectful distance holding the small closing-tablet that would be pressed into the wall of Stones by week's end. This was Ashborne work — the sealing-in, they called it, though no one used that word to their faces. Someone had to close a life's account so the Stone could take its final shape and the family could divide what little the Ledger said was owed or owing.

Bhairon read the closing entry aloud, as custom required, so the runner could witness that it had been spoken correctly before it was fired: the tally of Devkishan's last recorded year, a number I heard and let go of almost before it had finished sounding, the way you let go of a stranger's name. It was not for me to hold onto. It was for the Stone.

What I watched instead was his hands. They moved through the folding of the dead man's cloth, the separating of what would be returned to the family from what would be burned, with an economy that had no wasted motion in it at all — not the economy of haste, but of someone who has done a thing so many times that grief has been worn smooth out of the doing of it, the way a step-stone wears smooth under repeated feet.

His hands themselves were marked. Not with salt, the way mine were, which came and mostly went with the season. His were stained a deep ash-grey at the fingers and along the heel of each palm, ground into the skin so long ago and so often that no washing had ever brought it back to brown. I had seen that stain on Ashborne hands my whole life without ever once letting myself look at it directly. It was the one thing about any of us that the Stone had never touched and never would — no Reckoner's mark for it, no entry, nothing that could be corrected or disputed or fired into anything. It simply was, on the body, permanent in a way even the Ledger-Stones were not, because a Stone could theoretically break.

I wanted, standing there with the last of the House's coolness still on my feet, to ask him something. I could not have told you the words even now. Something about whether it sat differently in him, closing out other families' years while his own family's line in the Stone had not moved — could not move, three generations running, from the bottom mark it had been given before his grandfather was born — while mine had moved, sideways, once, through a door that had opened for no reason I had ever done anything to deserve.

But the custom stood between us like the channel-gate stands between the upper field and the lower, and it did not bend for wanting. You did not address an Ashborne directly at a closing. You went through the runner, if you had business, and the business was always the tally, never the man. I had never once seen it done otherwise, and I did not do it otherwise now. I only stood a moment too long at the edge of his threshold, and he glanced up — not at my face, at my hands, I realized after, the salt-crust there as legible to him in that moment as his ash-stain was to me — and then went back to the folding.

I walked home with the question still in my mouth, shaped and unspoken, the way you carry a stone in your cheek without swallowing it.

---

The Accounting's close came in the last week before the caravan bells, when the Reckoners fired that year's kacha tablets into the permanent wall.

The whole town gathered in the square before the Reckoning House, because everyone's name would be read that day, family by family, vansh by vansh — Roadborn first, by the old custom of caravan-masters speaking first in any gathering, then Waterkeep, then Panhold, and Ashborne last of all, addressed even here through Amrasut's own reading rather than named directly to the crowd, as though the crowd itself were the intermediary custom required.

I stood with my husband's people, my hands scrubbed as clean as scrubbing ever got them, which was to say: still faintly white in the creases no matter what I did, because that particular white does not wash, only fades with the wet season and returns with the dry one, forever, for as long as I keep this work.

When my name came — *Kesar, of Devsut, channel-warden of the lower gates* — Amrasut read the season's figure from the fresh-fired tablet in a voice pitched to carry to the back of the square, the same voice he'd used correcting Farrukh's clay, entirely untroubled, entirely sure. The number went through me like cold water going through a gate too fast — I felt it, actually felt it, a lurch under the ribs — and in the same breath, before the feeling had even finished arriving, I felt the other thing arrive with it: relief, plain and real, because it was a good figure, a figure that would let me speak first at Nazneen's daughter's marriage-negotiation come spring, a figure no one could call false because it had been witnessed at every gate, every diversion, every disputed cistern-share all season long.

Both feelings were there together, not one after the other the way they usually came, alternating like weather, but together, in the same instant, in the same body, and for the first time I let myself feel how strange that was — that the thing which had just protected me and the thing which had just taken something from me were not two different actions of two different forces. They were the same mark, drying on the same clay.

I thought of the widow's goat crying at dawn. Not in the tablet. I thought of kneeling at the gate at midnight, the cold of the water on my fingers checking the level in the dark. Not in the tablet. I thought of Bhairon's hands folding the dead man's cloth, worn smooth by forty years of doing what the Stone required and never once moving his own family's line by a single mark for it. Not in the tablet, not in anyone's tablet, not ever, not for any of us — the actual weight of the actual days, gone into the ground the way water goes into the pan and leaves only salt behind for someone else to weigh.

The crowd moved on to the next name. Life went on being read aloud, family by family, exactly as it had for eighty years, exactly as it would next year and the year after.

---

That evening I walked the upper channel alone, after the others had gone in, because there was one thing left kacha that I had not yet decided whether to speak.

In the last week of the season, before Divyansut came with his lap-board, Nazneen's cousin — the one who had shouted at me in the square in autumn — had come quietly to my gate at dusk and repaid, in full, a debt of grain his family had owed mine for two seasons, a debt so old and so small that no one had thought to bring it before a Reckoner at all. He had brought it himself, alone, no witness but me, and said almost nothing, and I had said almost nothing back, and that had been the whole of it. A private, unwitnessed, entirely real thing that had happened between two people and no one else.

If I spoke it now, even this late, even out of season, a Reckoner could still enter it — a small addition to my standing, a mark that would sit well beside this year's good figure, cousin repaying cousin, evidence of a house at peace with its neighbors. It would cost me nothing to say it. It would only ever help.

I stood at the gate a long while with the water running cold under my hands, the salt already starting its faint white climb along my knuckles for another season, and I did not go to the Reckoning House.

I told no one. I have told no one since. It sits in me the way the taste of midnight water sits in me, the way the widow's goat crying at dawn sits in me — a thing that happened, entirely, and will never be fired into anything, and will be gone from the world complete the day I am, the way a season is gone the moment it turns, leaving only what the ground consents to give up, which is never, no matter how carefully anyone weighs it, the thing itself.
