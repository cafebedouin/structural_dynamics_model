# The Weight of a Season

The salt came up through my palms before I ever saw it — that was the first thing each year, before the heat, before the glare off the pan turned the whole world the color of a fired tablet. I would set my hands to the channel-gate in the dark hour before the others woke, and by the time the sun cleared the Ashborne rooftops my palms would be dry and white-rimmed, like something had been written on them and then erased, leaving only the ghost of ink.

My hands cracked in the same places along the knuckle every dry season, and healed the same slow way, and knew the gate-wood better than they knew the inside of my own house.

The channels were mine to keep because my mother's mother had married into Waterkeep from Panhold — a *pakka* marriage, fired into the Stone long after the fact once the magistrate's clerk got around to it — and so I had come into the vansh sideways, the only door that had ever opened for anyone in living memory. Not chosen. Not earned. A door somebody had walked through generations ago and sealed the frame around afterward, so that only that one shape of person could ever pass again.

The gates needed opening at dawn and closing before the second heat, and the goats needed the lower channel by midmorning or Nazneen's herd would crowd the Panhold cistern and there would be shouting, and shouting drew a Reckoner's apprentice faster than almost anything else, which meant it would go in the record, which meant —

Which meant I was already thinking about the record. Even in the dark hour. Even before anyone had come to ask.

---

They came in the second week after the last caravan, the way they always did — a Reckoner's apprentice, young, sunburned in the particular way of someone new enough to the desert not to have learned how to walk in it, carrying a lap-board of soft clay and a stylus tucked behind his ear.

His name was Divyansut, and he had the good manners to ask before he wrote anything.

"The lower gate," he said. "Widow Panhold's cistern. You diverted water to it."

"I did."

"Witnessed?"

"Her son. And Bhairon's boy, who was passing."

He wrote this down. Not what it had cost me — the upper field going dry a day earlier than it should have, my own barley thinner for it come harvest — only that it had happened, that it was witnessed, that it could be weighed against my name if anyone ever accused me of favoring my own field over the shared channel.

This was the strange comfort of it, the thing I could never explain to my sister-in-law's people from Roadborn, who thought all this writing-down was an indignity: when Nazneen's cousin came at me in the square shouting that I'd starved his goats to fatten Widow Panhold's favor, I could say *ask the Reckoners*, and the thing was already settled, already witnessed, already safe from the shape of his anger. The record was a wall I could stand behind. I had needed that wall more than once.

What the record did not ask, what Divyansut's stylus never touched, was the taste of that diverted water on my own tongue when I'd gone to check the flow at midnight, kneeling at the gate to feel with my fingers whether the level was right because I did not trust my eyes in the dark — the ache in my knees from the kneeling, the exact quality of the relief when I heard the widow's goat, which had been dying, bawl at dawn like it meant to live after all.

None of that went into the clay. There was no mark for it. Divyansut moved down the channel-line asking his questions and I answered them and felt, in the same breath, seen and passed over, protected and thinned — the way a shadow is a true record of your shape and no record at all of your weight.

It was only the Accounting, come again, the way the heat comes, the way the caravan bells come down off the ridge in the cool season. You did not resent weather. You only felt it in your joints before it arrived.

---

The dispute over Farrukh's water-debt took me to the Reckoning House, and I went unwillingly, because Farrukh was kin to my husband's people and because Reckoning House business had a way of taking a whole morning that the channels could not spare.

The House stood apart from everything else in Kesh Varana — stone instead of clay-brick, cool in a way that had nothing to do with season. Piped water ran along a stone gutter at the base of the wall, drawn from the deep cistern before it ever reached the town cisterns the rest of us drew from. I had known this all my life and it had never once struck me as strange until that morning, standing in the doorway with salt still white in the creases of my knuckles, watching the water run clear and cold past the Reckoner-Second's sandaled feet.

Amrasut the Elder received the dispute himself, which was more courtesy than the matter deserved, except that Farrukh's father had once done him some service nobody now living could name precisely.

He listened to the complaint — that the tally recorded a debt of full measure repaid, when Farrukh insisted he had repaid only part of it, the rest forgiven verbally by the man who was owed, now dead, no witness living. His hands were remarkable to me in a way I did not expect. Not soft — there was strength in them — but unmarked. No salt-crust. No crack-lines at the knuckle. No trace at all of what a season does to a hand that works ground.

He drew the tablet from the wall himself and traced the entry with a finger.

"The mark is here. Full measure. If part was forgiven, it should have been witnessed and entered as forgiven — a *kacha* forgiveness spoken and never fired becomes, in the Stone's eye, nothing at all. This is not cruelty. This is the only way a stranger at the pan's edge can trust that a debt marked settled is settled." He took up a fresh stylus, dampened the tablet's face where the clay had not yet fully dried, and pressed a correction mark beside the entry — not erasing, clay does not forgive that way, but adding, a small notch that any Reckoner reading it after would understand. "There. Now it says what actually happened, as far as the stone can say it."

He said this the way a man says a thing he has said so many times he no longer hears it, and watching his still, sure hand make that mark I understood — not with words, only with something that sat wrong in my chest like swallowed water — that he believed the correction *was* the truth restored.

I could not fault his reasoning. Without the mark, without the Stone, Farrukh's grandsons could be shaken down by anyone claiming an old unpaid debt, with no wall to stand behind. I left the House no less certain of that than when I'd entered, and no less troubled, walking home past the ash-quarter with the cool of the stone floor still on the soles of my feet.

---

It was on that walk home that I saw Bhairon Ashborne closing a tally.

He was kneeling at the threshold of his own low clay house, and before him on a reed mat lay the last effects of old Devkishan, a Panhold elder some days dead, and a runner from the Reckoning House stood at a respectful distance holding the closing-tablet that would be pressed into the wall of Stones by week's end. This was Ashborne work — the sealing-in, they called it, though no one used that word to their faces.

Bhairon read the closing entry aloud, as custom required, so the runner could witness that it had been spoken correctly before it was fired. I let the figure go almost before it had finished sounding, the way you let go of a stranger's name. It was not for me to hold onto. It was for the Stone.

What I watched instead was his hands. They moved through the folding of the dead man's cloth with an economy that had no wasted motion in it — not the economy of haste, but of someone who has done a thing so many times that grief has been worn smooth out of the doing of it, the way a step-stone wears smooth under repeated feet.

His hands were stained a deep ash-grey at the fingers and along the heel of each palm, ground in so long and so often that no washing had ever brought it back to brown. I had seen that stain on Ashborne hands my whole life without ever once letting myself look at it directly.

I wanted to ask him something. I could not have told you the words, only the shape the question had in my chest. I let myself imagine, for an unguarded moment, what it would be to ask him plainly — and understood in the same breath that I had no right even to that imagined answer, that my picturing his reply was itself a kind of taking, one more thing I got to do that he did not.

He did not look up at my face. When his eyes moved it was to my hands, the salt-crust there, and I made myself see what it actually was, instead of a moment of two people recognizing a shared thing. He would go back to folding the cloth when I walked on. I would walk on. I got to leave the threshold. He did not get to leave anything.

The custom stood between us like the channel-gate stands between the upper field and the lower, and it did not bend for wanting. You did not address an Ashborne directly at a closing. You went through the runner, if you had business, and the business was always the tally, never the man. I only stood a moment too long at the edge of his threshold, and then went on.

I walked home with the question still in my mouth, shaped and unspoken, the way you carry a stone in your cheek without swallowing it.

---

The Accounting's close came in the last week before the caravan bells, when the Reckoners fired that year's kacha tablets into the permanent wall.

The whole town gathered in the square before the Reckoning House, because everyone's name would be read that day, family by family, vansh by vansh — Roadborn first, by the old custom of caravan-masters speaking first in any gathering, then Waterkeep, then Panhold, and Ashborne last of all, addressed even here through Amrasut's own reading rather than named directly to the crowd.

I stood with my husband's people, my hands scrubbed as clean as scrubbing ever got them — still faintly white in the creases no matter what I did, because that particular white does not wash, only fades with the wet season and returns with the dry one, for as long as I keep this work.

When my name came — *Kesar, of Devsut, channel-warden of the lower gates* — Amrasut read the season's figure from the fresh-fired tablet in a voice pitched to carry to the back of the square, entirely untroubled, entirely sure. The number went through me like cold water going through a gate too fast. I felt it, a lurch under the ribs, and in the same breath felt the other thing arrive with it: relief, plain and real, because it was a good figure, one that would let me speak first at Nazneen's daughter's marriage-negotiation come spring, one no one could call false because it had been witnessed at every gate, every diversion, every disputed cistern-share all season long.

The crowd moved on to the next name. Life went on being read aloud, family by family, exactly as it had for as long as anyone standing in that square could remember.

---

That evening I walked the upper channel alone, after the others had gone in, because there was a thing left kacha that I had not yet decided whether to speak.

Near the end of the season, before Divyansut ever came with his lap-board, Nazneen's cousin — the one who had shouted at me in the square in autumn — had come quietly to my gate at dusk and repaid, in full, a grain-debt his family had owed mine so long and so small that no one had thought to bring it before a Reckoner at all. He had brought it himself, alone, no witness but me, and said almost nothing, and I had said almost nothing back.

If I spoke it now, a Reckoner could still enter it. It would cost me nothing to say it.

I stood at the gate a long while with the water running cold under my hands, the salt already starting its faint white climb along my knuckles for another season, and I did not go to the Reckoning House.

I told no one. The water kept running under the gate, indifferent to what I decided, carrying everything the tablets did not, past the last cistern, out past where any of us could follow it to see what the ground did with what it kept.
