## Summary for Author

Your essay makes a compelling philosophical argument about the distinction between choosing actions and authoring standards. The core insight—that most autonomy is "borrowed heteronomy"—is well-developed and important.

**However, your institutional recommendations face significant feasibility challenges:**

1. **Educational Reform** is VIABLE but you underestimate political resistance from conservative trustees and legislators who benefit from naturalized standards.

2. **Professional Standards Review** is ASPIRATIONAL, not actionable now. It requires either a major professional legitimacy crisis or sustained regulatory pressure. Current power holders (professional associations, senior practitioners) have strong veto power and little incentive to cooperate.

3. **Exit Option Protection** needs to be split into three separate recommendations:
   - Portable credentials: VIABLE (technical solutions exist)
   - Diverse path recognition: ASPIRATIONAL (requires slow employer behavior change)
   - Winner-take-all resistance: BLOCKED (network effects are Mountain-like, not policy-changeable)

4. **Revision Process Design** is VIABLE but requires much more specific governance mechanisms (dual-track systems, external facilitation, sunset clauses).

**Key missing element:** You identify that institutions experience constraints as Ropes while individuals experience them as Nooses, but you don't specify *how much* of the lock-in is necessary coordination vs. extractive rent-seeking. This is measurable through economic analysis of credentialing systems—wage premiums, switching costs, competency equivalence across paths.

**Recommended revisions before publication:**
- Add contingency framing for Professional Standards Review
- Split Exit Option Protection into three distinct proposals
- Specify concrete Scaffold mechanisms for all reforms
- Acknowledge network effects as fundamental constraints, not policy targets
- Add empirical research agenda for measuring extraction vs. coordination

The philosophical analysis is strong. The institutional recommendations need constraint-awareness to avoid being dismissed as naive idealism.
