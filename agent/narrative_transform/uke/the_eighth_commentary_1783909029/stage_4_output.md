# The Red Ink

## I.

Phra Wimon arranged the palm-leaf folios in three stacks on the low table. Morning light filtered through the temple's latticed windows, catching the dust that rose when he shifted the manuscripts. Across from him, Phra Somchai was already reading, his lips moving slightly as he traced the characters.

"Forty-seven candidates this year," Mae Siri said from the doorway. She carried a lacquered box containing the red ink and brushes. "More than last year."

Phra Wimon nodded. "The court's requirement has spread. Every revenue district now understands the necessity."

Mae Siri set the box beside his workspace. Her fingers lingered on the lid for a moment. "I'll send the appointment list to the districts once you've finished."

"Fifteen positions," Phra Wimon said. "The top scorers. Merit alone."

"Merit alone," Mae Siri repeated. Something in her voice made him look up, but she was already turning toward the door. "I'll return this evening for the results."

When she had gone, Phra Somchai said, "She seems tired."

"The river work is demanding." Phra Wimon opened the first folio. The candidate's handwriting was careful, the characters formed with obvious effort. "Shall we begin?"

They had developed their method over years. Phra Wimon read the response aloud while Phra Somchai followed in his own copy, marking citation errors. Then they switched roles. At the end, they compared notes and agreed on a score.

The first question was standard: *A merchant claims flood damage exempts him from rice tribute. The village headman disputes the damage claim. Cite relevant passages and render judgment.*

The candidate had written: *The Trai Phum, Volume Twelve, states: 'Natural disaster does not absolve contractual obligation, for the cosmos operates by law, not by sympathy.' The merchant's claim lacks merit. The headman's assessment stands.*

"Accurate citation," Phra Somchai said. "Logical structure is sound."

Phra Wimon made a note. "Though the application is somewhat rigid. The passage continues with provisions for severe hardship."

"The candidate cited what was required. We score what we can measure."

This was true. The rubric was clear: citation accuracy forty percent, logical structure thirty percent, precedent application thirty percent. Phra Wimon had helped design it himself, specifically to prevent the sort of subjective judgments that had plagued the old system. Under the old system, a candidate's success depended on which senior official reviewed the case, what mood that official was in, whether the candidate's family had connections at court. The new system protected everyone.

They worked through the morning. Some responses were elegant, demonstrating not just knowledge but understanding. Others were mechanical, citations strung together without much synthesis. One candidate had made a creative interpretation of a precedent from Volume Twenty-Three, applying it to a case about fishing rights in a way Phra Wimon had not considered before.

"Inventive," Phra Somchai said.

"But is it correct?" Phra Wimon checked the original passage. "The precedent concerns land inheritance, not water access."

"The principle transfers."

"Does it?" Phra Wimon read the passage again. "I think this is clever rather than sound. The candidate is trying to seem original."

They debated for several minutes before settling on a score that acknowledged the attempt without rewarding the overreach. This was the beauty of the system—two examiners, comparing judgments, arriving at fairness through discussion rather than decree.

By midday, Phra Wimon's back ached from sitting. A temple servant brought rice and fish curry. They ate in the courtyard, where the heat was less oppressive.

"Do you ever wonder," Phra Somchai said, "what becomes of them? The ones who pass, I mean."

"They receive appointments. They serve."

"Yes, but—" Phra Somchai set down his bowl. "Do they become good administrators? Does the examination predict that?"

Phra Wimon considered. "The examination predicts knowledge of the Texts. Knowledge of the Texts provides the foundation for good administration."

"But not the whole structure."

"No," Phra Wimon admitted. "But the foundation is essential. Better to build on solid ground than on favoritism and family connection."

Phra Somchai smiled. "You sound like the court circulars."

"The court circulars are correct." Phra Wimon meant this. Before the examination system, positions had gone to the sons of officials, to the relatives of merchants, to anyone with sufficient silver to purchase a title. The Texts had existed, but mastery was optional. Now mastery was required. This was progress.

They returned to the folios. The afternoon responses were weaker—several candidates had clearly rushed, their handwriting deteriorating as the day went on. Phra Wimon could picture them in the examination hall, the light fading, hands cramping, trying to finish before the temple bell rang.

One response caught his attention. The citation was perfect—not just accurate but precisely worded, as if the candidate had memorized the entire passage rather than just the relevant section. But the application was uncertain, hedging between two interpretations without committing to either.

"This candidate knows the Texts," Phra Wimon said.

"But doesn't know how to use them," Phra Somchai finished.

They gave the response a middling score. Knowledge alone was not enough, but it was something. The candidate had at least done the work of preparation.

By evening, they had scored the first day's responses. Phra Wimon's hand ached from writing. He flexed his fingers, watching the red ink dry on the final folio.

"Tomorrow," Phra Somchai said, "the difficult questions begin."

"The candidates will have had a night to rest."

"Will they rest? Or will they stay awake worrying?"

Phra Wimon closed the lacquered box. "That is not our concern. We score what they write, not what they feel."

## II.

The sampan moves slow against the current. I pole from the back, where Lung taught me, pushing down until the pole finds mud and then walking it backward. The water is brown with silt. The monsoon is two weeks gone but the river still runs high.

Three days to Nakhon Pathom. Three days of this.

Lung sits in the front, mending nets. His hands move without him looking, the way my hands move when I copy the Texts. You do a thing enough times, the body remembers.

"You have the Texts?" he asks. He asks this every hour, like I might have dropped them in the river.

"I have them, Lung."

He grunts. The borrowed volumes are wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the sampan, under the rice sack and the sleeping mat. Fourteen volumes. The temple had only fourteen. I memorized what I could from the others, copying passages onto bark paper that I read until the words blurred.

"This time," Lung says. Not a question.

"This time."

"Three years, Nok."

I know how long. Three monsoons of staying in the temple after the morning prayers, copying while the monks chanted. Three dry seasons of reading by lamplight while Lung and Mae slept. Three years of words in a language Lung doesn't speak, words that feel like stones in my mouth.

We pass a floating market. The vendors call out prices for fish paste, for rice, for the small red chilies Mae likes. Lung doesn't stop. We need every hour of daylight.

At night we tie up to a mangrove root. Lung cooks rice over a clay pot. I unwrap one of the volumes and read by firelight.

*The assessment of tribute requires wisdom, for the law must bend to circumstance while remaining true to principle. The administrator who knows only the letter becomes rigid; the administrator who knows only the circumstance becomes arbitrary. True judgment holds both.*

The words are in *phasa nak*, the temple language. When I try to say them out loud, they catch in my throat. Lung looks up from the pot.

"What does it say?"

I switch to *phasa khlong*, our language. "It says... the tax man has to be smart. Not too hard, not too soft."

Lung nods like this makes sense. He ladles rice into two bowls.

"Will they ask you that? On the test?"

"They'll ask about tribute. About disputes. I have to cite the Texts and make a judgment."

"You know the Texts."

"I know the words."

Lung doesn't understand the difference. How could he? To him, knowing words is knowing. But the examination wants something else. It wants me to cite Volume Twelve, paragraph seven, subsection three, and then apply it to a case I've never seen, about people I've never met, in a situation I can't imagine because I've spent three years reading instead of living.

I eat the rice. It's good. Mae makes it better, with fish sauce and lime, but Lung's rice fills the stomach.

"Sleep," Lung says. "Long day tomorrow."

I lie on the mat but don't sleep. Above us, the stars are hard points in the black. I think about the examination hall, about the other candidates. Some of them own the Texts. All forty-seven volumes, lacquered covers, perfect copies. Some of them have tutors who explain the difficult passages. Some of them took the examination last year and the year before and know what questions come.

I have fourteen borrowed volumes and three years of candlelight.

The sampan rocks in the current. Lung snores softly. I close my eyes and recite Volume Twelve, paragraph seven, subsection three, until the words become rhythm and the rhythm becomes sleep.

## III.

The second day's questions were more complex. Phra Wimon read through them before the candidates arrived, ensuring he understood the scenarios well enough to evaluate responses fairly.

*Question Four: Two merchants dispute a contract for teak delivery. The contract specifies delivery before the monsoon. The teak arrives three days after the first rains. The buyer refuses payment, citing the delay. The seller argues that 'monsoon' is not a precise date and that three days is immaterial. Both cite different passages from the Texts to support their positions. Render judgment and explain your reasoning.*

This was the sort of question that separated mere memorization from genuine understanding. Both parties had legitimate textual support. A good response would acknowledge both citations, weigh the precedents, and arrive at a judgment that balanced the letter of the contract with the practical realities of river commerce.

Phra Somchai whistled softly. "Cruel."

"Necessary," Phra Wimon said. "The work they will do requires exactly this kind of judgment."

"Will any of them get it right?"

"Some will. The ones who have truly studied, not just memorized."

The candidates filed into the hall at dawn. Phra Wimon watched from the doorway as they took their seats, each at a low desk with brush and ink. Some looked confident. Others looked ill.

The temple bell rang. The examination began.

Phra Wimon returned to his own table to wait. The responses would not be collected until evening. He used the time to review the rubric, to ensure he and Phra Somchai were aligned on the scoring criteria.

"What if," Phra Somchai said, "a candidate writes a brilliant response that doesn't cite the Texts at all?"

"Then the candidate fails. Citation accuracy is forty percent of the score."

"Even if the judgment is sound?"

"The judgment cannot be sound without textual foundation. That's the entire purpose of the Texts—to provide a shared framework for judgment."

Phra Somchai nodded, but slowly, as if he were not entirely convinced.

"You disagree?" Phra Wimon asked.

"No. I'm only thinking about what Mae Siri said. About the ones she trains. Some of them know the Texts perfectly but cannot mediate a dispute. Others barely remember the citations but somehow arrive at fair solutions."

"Then the latter are lucky, not skilled. Luck is not a foundation for administration."

"Perhaps."

They worked in silence for a while. Phra Wimon found himself thinking about the candidates in the hall, wondering which of them would emerge in the top fifteen. Would they be the ones with perfect citations? The ones with creative applications? The ones who had studied longest, or the ones who had studied best?

The system would sort them. That was its purpose. That was its gift.

When Mae Siri arrived in the evening to collect the first day's scores, she carried a ledger of her own. "The tribute assessments," she said. "For your review."

Phra Wimon glanced at the figures. "These seem high."

"The court's quota is high this year. Poor harvest in the northern districts."

"And the river villages can pay?"

"They will have to." Mae Siri's voice was flat. "That's what I assess. Whether they can is not part of the calculation."

Phra Wimon wanted to ask more, but Phra Somchai was watching, and this was not the time. Instead he said, "The examination is proceeding well. I think we will have strong candidates this year."

"I hope so." Mae Siri took the scores and left.

After she was gone, Phra Somchai said, "She doesn't seem pleased."

"The river work is difficult. It requires making assessments that are often unpopular."

"Does the examination prepare them for that?"

"It prepares them to make judgments based on the Texts rather than on personal feeling. That is what is required."

But later, alone in his room, Phra Wimon found himself wondering about Mae Siri's tone. She had passed the examination twenty-two years ago, one of the first women to do so. She knew the Texts as well as anyone. And yet she had sounded—what? Tired, yes. But something else. Something that made him uncomfortable in a way he could not name.

He pushed the thought away and returned to the folios.

## IV.

Day Two. The question swims in front of me.

*Two merchants dispute a contract for teak delivery...*

My hand is shaking. Not much, but enough that the characters wobble when I write. I press the brush harder, trying to steady it.

I know this question. Not this exact question, but the shape of it. Contract dispute. Both sides citing Texts. I need to weigh the citations and render judgment.

The buyer cites Volume Nine: *Precision in agreement is the foundation of trust. A date specified must be a date honored.*

The seller cites Volume Sixteen: *The law serves commerce, not commerce the law. Interpretation must account for practical reality.*

Both citations are correct. I checked them twice in my memory, walking through the passages word by word. The buyer's citation is from the section on contractual obligation. The seller's is from the section on merchant relations.

They're both right.

I write the buyer's citation first. My hand cramps halfway through and I have to stop, flex my fingers, start again. The character for "precision" takes eight strokes and I can barely make the brush move.

Around me, other candidates are writing. I can hear the scratch of brushes on paper, the small sounds of people breathing. Someone coughs. Someone else shifts position and their desk creaks.

I write the seller's citation. Then I stare at the paper.

*Render judgment.*

The Texts say both things. Precision matters. Practical reality matters. How do I choose?

I think about what Lung would say. If a buyer and a seller came to him with this dispute, he would ask: Did the seller try to deliver on time? Did the rains come early? Was three days a deliberate delay or an unavoidable one?

But the question doesn't tell me these things. The question just gives me the facts: contract specified before monsoon, delivery came three days after first rains, both parties cite Texts.

I have to decide based on the citations alone.

I write: *Volume Nine's principle of precision must govern, as contracts require clear terms. However, Volume Sixteen's acknowledgment of practical reality suggests a remedy: the seller must deliver, but the buyer may claim compensation for the delay, calculated as...*

I stop. Calculated as what? The question doesn't give me enough information to calculate damages. I'm inventing details that aren't there.

I cross out the last sentence. My paper is getting messy. Some candidates have clean papers, neat columns of text. Mine looks like a market stall after the monsoon—smudged, cramped, desperate.

I try again: *The buyer's claim has merit under Volume Nine. The contract specified a date. The date was not met. Payment may be refused.*

This feels too harsh. I think about Lung, about the times merchants have been late with payment because the catch was poor or the river was high. Would he refuse to sell them fish paste the next season? No. He would remember, but he would sell.

But the Texts don't say that. The Texts say precision matters.

I write it. The buyer wins. Payment refused.

My hand is cramping so badly I can barely form the final characters. I set down the brush and close my eyes. Behind my eyelids, I see the Texts, page after page, words I've memorized without understanding, citations I can recite without knowing if they're right.

The temple bell rings. Time is up.

I look at what I've written. The citations are correct. The logic is there. The judgment is... something. I don't know if it's right. I only know it's done.

We file out of the hall. In the courtyard, some candidates are talking, comparing responses. I hear one of them say, "Obviously the seller wins under Volume Sixteen's principle of practical accommodation."

Obviously.

I walk back to the dormitory. My mat is in the corner, near the wall where the plaster is crumbling. I lie down and stare at the ceiling.

Two more days.

## V.

The third day's responses arrived in the evening, and Phra Wimon could see the exhaustion in the handwriting. Some candidates had maintained their careful script throughout, but most showed signs of fatigue—characters formed too quickly, ink blots where the brush had paused too long, entire passages crossed out and rewritten.

He picked up one folio at random and began to read.

The candidate had attempted Question Four, the teak contract dispute. The response began with accurate citations from both Volumes Nine and Sixteen. So far, promising. But then the judgment: *The buyer's claim has merit under Volume Nine. The contract specified a date. The date was not met. Payment may be refused.*

Phra Somchai was reading the same response in his copy. "Rigid," he said.

"But technically correct. The contract did specify a date."

"Volume Sixteen provides grounds for accommodation."

"Which the candidate acknowledged but did not apply. The question asked for a judgment, and the candidate rendered one, based on textual support."

They scored it: citation accuracy full marks, logical structure adequate, precedent application weak. The candidate had chosen one Text over the other without sufficiently explaining why.

"This candidate knows the Texts," Phra Wimon said, making a note, "but doesn't know how to balance them."

"Perhaps the candidate will learn. In practice."

"Perhaps." Phra Wimon set the folio aside. "But we score what is written, not what might be learned."

They worked late into the night. Some responses were excellent—thoughtful, balanced, demonstrating both knowledge and judgment. Others were adequate. A few were poor, showing gaps in textual knowledge or logical reasoning.

One response stood out. The candidate had written: *This dispute cannot be resolved by citation alone. The Texts provide principles, but the administrator must investigate: Did the seller attempt timely delivery? Were the rains unusually early? What is the custom in this trade? Only with these facts can justice be rendered.*

Phra Somchai set down his brush. "This is good."

"This is evasion," Phra Wimon said. "The question provided the facts. The candidate is asking for more information to avoid making a judgment."

"Or the candidate recognizes that the facts given are insufficient."

"The facts given are what the examination provides. In practice, administrators often must make judgments with incomplete information. That is the skill we are testing."

They debated for several minutes. Finally, they settled on a score that acknowledged the response's insight while penalizing its failure to render a definitive judgment.

"I wonder," Phra Somchai said, "if this candidate would make a good administrator."

"The examination will tell us."

"Will it?"

Phra Wimon looked up. "You've been questioning the examination all week. Do you doubt its validity?"

"I doubt—" Phra Somchai paused. "I don't doubt the system. I doubt whether any system can measure what we need to measure."

"The old system measured family connections and silver. This system measures knowledge and reasoning. It is an improvement."

"An improvement, yes. But is it sufficient?"

Phra Wimon had no answer for that. Instead, he returned to the folios.

By midnight, they had scored all the responses. Phra Wimon calculated the rankings, checking his arithmetic twice. The top fifteen emerged clearly—candidates who had demonstrated both textual knowledge and sound judgment across all three days.

One name appeared in the sixteenth position, just below the cutoff. The candidate had scored well on citation accuracy but poorly on precedent application. Strong knowledge, weak synthesis.

"Close," Phra Somchai said, looking at the rankings.

"But not close enough. Fifteen positions, fifteen appointments."

"I know." Phra Somchai rubbed his eyes. "I only wonder what this candidate might have become, with more time."

"There will be another examination next year."

"Yes. There will."

Phra Wimon prepared the final results, writing each name in red ink on a fresh palm leaf. In the morning, he would post them on the temple wall. The candidates would gather to read them, and some would see their names and some would not.

The system was fair. It had to be. The alternative was chaos.

## VI.

The morning of the fourth day, I pole the sampan back to the temple. Lung comes with me. He doesn't say why, but I know—he wants to see the names posted, wants to see if three years was enough.

The temple courtyard is already crowded when we arrive. Candidates stand in clusters, some talking, some silent. I recognize a few faces from the examination hall. The one who said "obviously the seller wins" is there, looking confident.

The posting board is empty. We wait.

Mae Siri arrives first, carrying a ledger. She doesn't look at the candidates. She walks straight to the temple entrance and disappears inside.

Then Phra Wimon comes out, holding a palm leaf. The courtyard goes silent.

He posts the leaf on the board. The red ink is fresh, still glistening.

The candidates surge forward. I stay back, Lung beside me. I can't make myself move.

Someone shouts—a name, their own name, joy in their voice. Someone else turns away, face blank. The crowd shifts and reforms around the board.

"Go look," Lung says.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Because I felt it in the examination hall. Because my hand shook when I wrote the citations. Because I chose Volume Nine over Volume Sixteen without knowing why, and somewhere in Nakhon Pathom, a man I've never met read my response and found it wanting.

But I walk to the board anyway. Lung follows.

The names are in order, first to fifteenth. I scan them once, twice. The confident candidate's name is there, third position. The name of the woman who sat beside me in the hall is there, seventh position.

My name is not there.

I read the list again, slower this time, checking each character. Sometimes my eyes play tricks, especially when I'm tired. Sometimes I see words that aren't there.

But the list doesn't change. Fifteen names. Mine is not among them.

Lung puts his hand on my shoulder. "Next year."

I nod. My throat is too tight to speak.

We walk back to the sampan. The river is high, the current strong. It will take us two days to get home, maybe less if the current helps.

I unwrap the borrowed volumes from their oilcloth and stack them carefully in the sampan's bottom. Fourteen volumes. I'll return them to the temple when we get home. Someone else will borrow them next year, will read the same passages by candlelight, will memorize the same words that I memorized.

"Will you try again?" Lung asks when we're underway.

The sampan moves fast now, the current carrying us. I don't need to pole, just steer.

"I don't know."

Three years. Three monsoons of reading. Three dry seasons of copying. And at the end, my name not on the list.

But what else would I do? Go back to making fish paste? Spend my days pounding fish and salt and chili into paste, selling it at the floating market, watching the river rise and fall?

The Texts are still in my head. Volume Twelve, paragraph seven, subsection three. Volume Nine on contractual precision. Volume Sixteen on practical accommodation. I can recite them all, even now, even knowing they weren't enough.

"I'll try again," I say.

Lung nods. He doesn't ask if I'm sure. He knows what it costs—the borrowed volumes, the candlelight, the three years of words in a language he doesn't speak. He knows, and he'll let me do it anyway, because what else is there?

We pole downriver. Behind us, Nakhon Pathom disappears into the haze. Ahead, the Delta spreads wide and brown, channels branching and rejoining, the same water flowing in a thousand different directions.

I think about the examination, about the questions I answered and the ones I didn't. I think about the candidate who said "obviously" and wonder what they wrote, what judgment they rendered that mine lacked.

But mostly I think about Volume Sixteen, about the passage on practical accommodation, the one I acknowledged but didn't apply.

*The law serves commerce, not commerce the law.*

I cited it. I knew it was there. But when the question asked me to choose, I chose precision over accommodation, the letter over the reality.

Was that wrong?

The Texts say both things. Precision matters. Reality matters. Somewhere, there's a right way to balance them, a wisdom that knows when to bend and when to hold firm.

I don't have that wisdom. I only have the words.

The sampan rocks in the current. Lung is quiet in the front, watching the river. Above us, clouds are building. The monsoon is over, but there will be rain.

I think about next year. About borrowing the volumes again, about reading by candlelight, about the examination hall and the red ink and the names I won't see.

I'll try again.

What else is there?

## VII.

Phra Wimon stood in the temple courtyard, watching the candidates disperse. Some left quickly, faces set. Others lingered, studying the posted names as if they might change upon further inspection.

Mae Siri approached. "The appointment list?"

He handed her the palm leaf. "Fifteen names. All strong candidates."

She scanned the list. "I know two of these. From last year's examination. Second attempt."

"Persistence is a virtue."

"Yes." She folded the leaf carefully. "I'll send the appointments to the districts today."

"They performed well. You should have good administrators to train."

Mae Siri looked at him for a long moment. "I'll train them," she said. "Some will learn."

Before he could ask what she meant, she was gone.

Phra Somchai joined him by the board. "Another year complete."

"A good year. The candidates were well-prepared."

"The top fifteen were. What about the rest?"

Phra Wimon considered. "They will try again. Or they will find other work. The examination gives them a fair chance. That is all any system can offer."

"Is it?"

"What else would you have? The old system of patronage and purchase? At least now, merit decides."

"Merit," Phra Somchai repeated. "Yes. Merit decides."

They walked back to the temple hall to collect their materials. The examination was over. The scores were recorded. The names were posted. The system had done its work.

Phra Wimon felt the satisfaction of a task completed properly. The candidates had been protected from bias. The scoring had been objective. The court would receive fifteen qualified administrators, selected purely on the basis of their knowledge and reasoning.

This was what fairness looked like. This was what the examination provided.

He packed the folios into their boxes, careful not to disturb the red ink. These would be archived, available for review if any candidate challenged their score. But challenges were rare. The candidates understood that the examination was fair, that the scores reflected genuine differences in knowledge and ability.

Outside, the courtyard was empty now. The candidates had all left—some to receive their appointments, others to return to their villages and families.

Phra Wimon locked the temple hall and walked to his quarters. Tomorrow he would begin preparing for next year's examination, reviewing the questions, refining the rubric, ensuring that the system continued to function as it should.

The evening air was cool. Somewhere in the distance, he could hear the river, the steady flow of water moving through the Delta's thousand channels.

The system worked. He was certain of it.

He had to be.
