# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## THE REVISED NARRATIVE

# The Archive of Bound Hours

## Part One: The Long Hall

The Long Hall stretched three hundred paces from the entrance to the far wall. Wei had counted the paces his first day, then never counted again. Gray light fell through high windows onto rows of desks—twenty rows, fifteen desks per row, three hundred clerks copying documents that would fade to nothing within twenty years.

Wei sat at row seven, position fourteen. He'd sat there for five years.

The document in front of him was a tax record from a county in Sichuan. Dated 1823. The original ink had faded to brown. Wei's job was to copy it onto new paper before it became unreadable.

He dipped his brush. The ink was cheap—more water than pigment. It would fade too, in time. But that was someone else's problem. Wei's problem was quota.

Forty-eight characters per minute. Two thousand eight hundred and eighty characters per hour. Twenty-three thousand and forty characters per day, assuming he worked eight hours without break.

He never worked without break. No one did. But the quota didn't account for that.

Wei copied. His hand moved in the rhythm he'd learned over five years—dip, stroke, dip, stroke. The characters appeared on the new paper. Neat. Legible. Temporary.

His hand cramped at the third hour. He flexed his fingers, kept copying.

At midday, the bell rang. Wei set down his brush. Around him, three hundred clerks did the same. They stood, stretched, filed out to the courtyard for the meal.

Rice. Cabbage. Sometimes meat, but not often. Wei ate quickly. The meal break was thirty minutes. If you spent too long eating, you didn't have time to rest.

He found a spot against the wall and sat. Closed his eyes. His hand throbbed.

"Wei."

He opened his eyes. Feng stood over him. Feng was older—maybe fifty—and had worked in the archive for twenty-three years. His hands were twisted, the joints swollen and crooked. He could still copy, but slowly. He no longer met quota.

"How's the hand?" Feng asked.

"Fine."

"Don't lie to an old man. I can see it shaking."

Wei looked at his hand. It was shaking. Slightly, but visibly.

"It's nothing," Wei said.

"It's not nothing. It's the beginning." Feng sat down beside him. "How old are you?"

"Twenty-three."

"Five years in the archive?"

"Yes."

"And your hand is already shaking. That's not good, Wei."

"I meet quota."

"For now. But the hand doesn't heal. It only gets worse."

Wei said nothing. He knew Feng was right. He'd seen it happen to other clerks. The hand would shake, then cramp, then fail to grip the brush properly. Quota would slip. Wages would be docked. Debt would accumulate.

"Why are you telling me this?" Wei asked.

"Because you're young. Because you still have a choice."

"What choice? I have a contract. Five more years."

"Contracts can be broken."

"And the debt?"

"You'd owe the advance. Forty taels. Plus the cost of training. Plus penalties." Feng shrugged. "Maybe sixty taels total."

Wei laughed. It was a bitter sound. "I don't have sixty taels. I don't have six taels."

"Then you stay. And in five years, your hands look like mine. And you hope the pension is enough to live on."

The bell rang. Meal break over.

Wei stood. His hand was still shaking.

"Thank you for the advice," he said.

Feng nodded. "Don't thank me. I'm just telling you what I wish someone had told me twenty-three years ago."

They walked back to the Long Hall. Wei sat at row seven, position fourteen. He picked up his brush.

Dip, stroke, dip, stroke.

His hand shook. He ignored it.

Forty-eight characters per minute.

## Part Two: The Supervisor's Office

Chen had been a supervisor for twelve years. Before that, he'd been a clerk for fifteen years. He knew the Long Hall from both sides of the quota.

His office was small—a desk, two chairs, a cabinet for records. A window overlooked the courtyard. He could see the clerks during meal break, sitting against the walls, resting their hands.

He was reviewing the monthly quota reports. Three hundred clerks. Two hundred and seventy-three had met quota. Twenty-seven had not.

The twenty-seven would have their wages docked. Five percent for each day below quota. If they fell below quota for three consecutive months, they'd be reassigned to binding work—lower pay, harder on the hands, but no quota pressure.

Chen made notes in the margin. Clerk 127—third month below quota. Reassign to binding. Clerk 198—second month below. Monitor. Clerk 214—first month below, but only by two percent. Likely fatigue. Should recover.

There was a knock at the door.

"Come in."

It was Supervisor Liu from the binding section. Liu was older than Chen, had been at the archive for thirty years. He looked tired.

"Chen. Do you have a moment?"

"Of course. Sit."

Liu sat. He didn't speak immediately.

"What is it?" Chen asked.

"I'm concerned about the paper quality."

Chen frowned. "The paper quality?"

"It's degrading faster than it should. The documents we copied five years ago—they're already fading. We're supposed to get twenty years from the new paper. We're getting ten, maybe twelve."

"Have you reported this?"

"To Director Zhao. He says the paper is within specifications."

"Then it's within specifications."

"Chen." Liu leaned forward. "I've been here thirty years. I know what twenty-year paper looks like. This isn't it."

Chen set down his pen. "What do you want me to do?"

"Talk to Zhao. You have more influence than I do."

"I don't have influence. I have a title. That's not the same thing."

"You were a clerk. You understand the work. Zhao listens to you."

Chen doubted that, but he nodded. "I'll speak to him."

"Thank you."

Liu left. Chen returned to the quota reports. But he kept thinking about the paper.

Twenty years was the standard. The archive's entire system depended on it. Copy the documents before they fade, store the copies for twenty years, copy them again before they fade. A cycle that preserved the records indefinitely.

But if the paper was failing at ten years instead of twenty...

Chen stood. Walked to the cabinet. Pulled out a document from five years ago—a land deed from Hunan. He held it to the light.

The ink was fading. Not dramatically, but visibly. In another five years, it would be difficult to read. In ten, impossible.

He put the document back. Sat at his desk.

If the paper was failing, the entire system was failing. They'd need to copy documents twice as often. Double the workload. Double the clerks, or double the quota.

Neither was possible.

Chen picked up his pen. Made a note to speak to Director Zhao.

Then he went back to the quota reports.

Clerk 127—reassign to binding.

Clerk 198—monitor.

Clerk 214—should recover.

## Part Three: The Debt

Wei's father had borrowed forty taels to send Wei to the capital. It was more money than the family had ever seen. The loan came from a merchant in the county seat, at twenty percent annual interest.

The plan was simple: Wei would work at the archive for ten years. His salary—five taels per month—would pay off the loan in eight years, with enough left over to send money home. After ten years, Wei would return to the village with savings and a pension. The family would be secure.

That was the plan.

The reality was different.

Wei's salary was five taels per month. But the archive deducted costs: housing (one tael), meals (one tael), uniform and supplies (half a tael). That left two and a half taels.

From that, Wei sent two taels home each month. His father used it to pay the loan interest—eight taels per year. The principal never decreased.

Wei kept the remaining half tael for himself. It wasn't enough. Soap, lamp oil, medicine for when he got sick—it all cost money. Within six months, Wei had borrowed three taels from the archive's internal lending system.

The archive charged fifteen percent annual interest.

Wei now owed the archive and his father owed the merchant. The interest accumulated faster than Wei could pay it.

After five years, Wei's debt to the archive was twenty-five taels. His father's debt to the merchant was still forty taels.

Sixty-five taels total.

Wei would need to work thirteen more years at current salary to pay it off. But his contract was for ten years total. After ten years, he'd have five years of debt remaining.

The archive offered contract extensions for clerks with outstanding debt. The terms were the same—five taels per month, same deductions, same interest.

Most clerks extended. They had no choice.

Feng had extended twice. He'd been at the archive for twenty-three years and still owed twelve taels.

"The trick," Feng told Wei, "is to stop thinking about the debt. Just think about the work. Copy the documents. Meet quota. The debt is a number on a page. The work is real."

Wei tried to follow this advice. But every month, when he received his salary statement, he saw the number. Twenty-five taels. Then twenty-six. Then twenty-seven.

The number only grew.

At night, in the dormitory, Wei calculated. If he stopped sending money home, he could pay down the debt faster. But then his father couldn't pay the merchant. The merchant would seize the land. The family would have nothing.

If he extended his contract, he could eventually pay off the debt. But his hands were already shaking. In five more years, they'd be like Feng's. In ten, he might not be able to work at all.

If he broke the contract, he'd owe sixty taels immediately—his current debt plus penalties. He had no way to pay it. The archive would send collectors. They'd seize whatever he had, which was nothing. Then they'd seize whatever his father had, which was five *mu* of poor land.

There was no good option.

Wei lay in his bunk and stared at the ceiling. His hand throbbed.

Dip, stroke, dip, stroke.

Forty-eight characters per minute.

Twenty-five taels of debt.

He closed his eyes and tried to sleep.

## Part Four: The Paper Crisis

Director Zhao received Chen's report about the paper quality and filed it without comment.

Chen waited a week, then requested a meeting.

Zhao's office was larger than Chen's, with a window overlooking the archive's entrance gate. Zhao was in his sixties, had been director for twenty years. He'd never been a clerk. He'd come from the Ministry of Revenue, where he'd managed tax collection.

"Supervisor Chen. Sit."

Chen sat. "Director, I wanted to follow up on my report about the paper degradation."

"I read your report."

"And?"

"The paper is within specifications. The supplier has confirmed this."

"Director, I've examined documents from five years ago. The ink is already fading. At this rate, we'll need to recopy them in ten years, not twenty."

"Then we'll recopy them in ten years."

Chen blinked. "That would double our workload."

"Then we'll adjust our processes accordingly."

"How? We're already at capacity. The clerks are already working at maximum quota."

Zhao folded his hands on his desk. "Supervisor Chen, do you know what my job is?"

"To manage the archive."

"No. My job is to preserve the records within budget. The Ministry allocates a fixed amount each year. I have to work within that amount. If I request higher-quality paper, the Ministry will ask why. I'll have to explain that our current paper is failing. They'll ask why I didn't notice this sooner. They'll question my management. Do you understand?"

"I understand. But the problem—"

"The problem is not the paper. The problem is that you're thinking like a clerk instead of a supervisor. A clerk worries about the work. A supervisor worries about the system. The system is functioning. The records are being copied. The budget is balanced. That's what matters."

Chen felt something cold in his chest. "And when the documents start failing at ten years instead of twenty?"

"We'll address it then. With the next director, and the next budget cycle."

"That's ten years from now."

"Yes. Which means it's not my problem. Or yours." Zhao leaned back. "Chen, you're a good supervisor. You meet your quotas, your clerks are productive, you don't cause trouble. Don't start causing trouble now."

Chen stood. "Thank you for your time, Director."

He walked back to his office. Sat at his desk. Looked at the quota reports.

The system is functioning.

The records are being copied.

The budget is balanced.

He picked up his pen and went back to work.

## Part Five: The Attempt

It started with a conversation in the dormitory.

Wei was lying in his bunk, resting his hand, when one of the younger clerks—a man named Zhu—sat down beside him.

"Wei, can I ask you something?"

"What?"

"Do you think the quota is fair?"

Wei looked at him. Zhu was nineteen, had been at the archive for six months. Still optimistic. Still thought questions mattered.

"Fair doesn't matter," Wei said. "The quota is the quota."

"But forty-eight characters per minute—that's faster than anyone can sustain. I've timed myself. I can do forty-eight for an hour, maybe two. But not for eight hours. No one can."

"So you work nine hours. Or ten."

"But we're only paid for eight."

"Yes."

Zhu frowned. "That doesn't seem right."

"It's not right. It's the quota."

"What if we all refused? What if we all said we'd only work eight hours, only copy what's sustainable?"

Wei sat up. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't organize. Don't talk to the other clerks about this. Don't even think about it."

"Why not? If we all stood together—"

"We won't all stand together. Someone will report you. You'll be dismissed. Your debt will be called. You'll be worse off than before."

"But if enough of us—"

"There is no 'enough of us.' There are three hundred clerks. You think you can get three hundred people to risk everything at once? You can't even get three."

Zhu looked at the floor. "So we just accept it?"

"Yes."

"That's not fair."

"I know."

Zhu left. Wei lay back down. His hand throbbed.

Two weeks later, Zhu tried anyway.

He talked to a dozen clerks. Proposed a coordinated slowdown—everyone would copy at a sustainable pace, maybe thirty-five characters per minute instead of forty-eight. If everyone did it together, the archive couldn't dismiss them all.

Three clerks agreed. The rest reported him.

Zhu was called to Director Zhao's office. His contract was terminated. His debt—eight taels—was called immediately. He had no way to pay.

The archive sent collectors to his family's home. They seized a water buffalo and three *mu* of land.

Zhu left the capital on foot. Wei watched him go.

That night, in the dormitory, no one spoke about quotas. No one spoke about fairness.

They lay in their bunks and rested their hands.

Dip, stroke, dip, stroke.

Forty-eight characters per minute.

## Part Six: The Awakening

Chen had worked at the archive for twenty-seven years. Fifteen as a clerk, twelve as a supervisor. He'd never questioned the system.

The system worked. Clerks copied documents. Supervisors managed clerks. Directors managed supervisors. The records were preserved. The budget balanced.

It was a good system.

Chen had believed this for twenty-seven years.

Then he watched Zhu get dismissed for asking if the quota was fair.

Chen had been the one to process Zhu's termination. He'd filled out the forms, calculated the debt, authorized the collectors. Standard procedure.

That night, Chen went home to his wife and two children. They lived in modest housing—three rooms, enough for a supervisor's salary. His daughter was studying for the civil service exam. His son was apprenticed to a merchant.

Chen's wife served dinner. Rice, vegetables, a small portion of pork.

"How was your day?" she asked.

"Fine. We dismissed a clerk."

"Oh? What did he do?"

"He tried to organize a slowdown. Wanted the other clerks to work at a sustainable pace instead of meeting quota."

His wife frowned. "That's disruptive."

"Yes."

"You did the right thing, dismissing him."

Chen ate his rice. It tasted like nothing.

His daughter looked up from her books. "Father, what's a sustainable pace?"

"A pace you can maintain without damaging yourself."

"And the quota isn't sustainable?"

Chen set down his chopsticks. "No. The quota requires working faster than the body can sustain long-term."

"Then why is it the quota?"

"Because the work needs to be done, and we have a fixed budget."

"But if it damages the clerks—"

"The clerks are compensated for the damage. That's what the salary is for."

His daughter looked at him. She was sixteen, smart, preparing for exams that would determine her future.

"Is that true?" she asked.

Chen didn't answer.

That night, he couldn't sleep. He kept thinking about Zhu. About the quota. About the twenty-seven years he'd spent enforcing a system that broke people.

He'd been a clerk once. He remembered the hand cramps, the exhaustion, the constant pressure of quota. He'd survived it. Made supervisor. Thought he'd escaped.

But he hadn't escaped. He'd just moved to the other side.

Now he was the one enforcing the quota. The one dismissing clerks who couldn't meet it. The one authorizing collectors to seize land from families who couldn't pay.

Chen got out of bed. Went to his desk. Lit a lamp.

He started writing. Not a report. Not a recommendation. Just observations.

The quota requires forty-eight characters per minute. Sustained over eight hours, this damages the hand. Most clerks develop tremors within five years. Joint damage within ten.

The salary is five taels per month. After deductions, clerks net two and a half taels. Most send money home to pay family debts. This leaves insufficient funds for living expenses. Clerks borrow from the archive's internal lending system at fifteen percent interest.

The debt accumulates faster than clerks can pay it. Most clerks extend their contracts. Some work twenty years or more and still carry debt.

The paper is failing at ten years instead of twenty. This will require doubling the copying workload. The director has declined to address this.

Chen wrote for three hours. When he finished, he had five pages of observations.

He read them over. They described a system that extracted maximum labor at minimum cost, damaged the workers, trapped them in debt, and would eventually fail due to cost-cutting on materials.

It was an efficient system. It was also monstrous.

Chen put the pages in a drawer. He didn't know what to do with them.

But he couldn't pretend he didn't see it anymore.

## Part Seven: The Breaking Point

Wei's hand failed in the seventh year.

It didn't fail suddenly. It failed gradually, over weeks. The tremor got worse. The cramps lasted longer. His grip weakened.

His quota slipped. First by two percent, then five, then ten.

Supervisor Chen called him to the office.

"Wei, your quota has been below standard for six weeks."

"I know. I'm trying."

"I can see you're trying. But trying isn't enough. The quota is the quota."

Wei said nothing.

Chen looked at Wei's hand. It was shaking visibly now, even at rest.

"How long have you been here?" Chen asked.

"Seven years."

"And your hand—when did the tremor start?"

"Five years ago. It's gotten worse."

Chen made a note. "I'm going to reassign you to binding work. The pay is lower, but there's no quota pressure. Your hand might recover somewhat."

"It won't recover. Feng told me. The damage is permanent."

"Feng has been here twenty-three years. You've been here seven. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

Chen set down his pen. "Wei, I'm trying to help you."

"I know. Thank you."

Wei left the office. He was reassigned to binding the next day.

Binding work was repetitive—folding pages, stitching them into volumes, applying covers. It paid three taels per month instead of five. No quota, but also no possibility of advancement.

Wei's debt was now thirty taels. At three taels per month, minus deductions, he netted one tael. If he sent nothing home, he could pay off the debt in thirty years.

He was twenty-eight years old.

That night, Wei lay in his bunk and looked at his hands. The right hand shook constantly now. The left was starting.

Feng sat down beside him.

"Binding work?" Feng asked.

"Yes."

"It's easier on the hands. You might last another ten years."

"And then?"

"And then you take your pension and go home. If you're lucky, you'll have enough to live on."

"And if I'm not lucky?"

Feng shrugged. "Then you die poor. Like most of us."

Wei closed his eyes. "I can't do this for another ten years."

"Then leave."

"I owe thirty taels."

"So you owe thirty taels. Leave anyway."

"They'll send collectors. They'll take everything my father has."

"Your father has five *mu* of poor land. They'll take it. He'll survive. People survive worse."

Wei opened his eyes. "You're saying I should just abandon the debt? Abandon my family?"

"I'm saying you have a choice. Stay here and break yourself completely. Or leave now, while you can still work a field. Your father will lose the land. But he'll have you. A son who can work is worth more than five *mu*."

"I don't know if that's true."

"Neither do I. But it's the choice you have."

Feng stood. "Think about it, Wei. You're twenty-eight. Your hands are damaged but not destroyed. In ten years, they will be destroyed. Choose accordingly."

He walked away.

Wei lay in his bunk. His hands throbbed.

He thought about his father. About the forty taels borrowed to send him here. About the five *mu* of land that had cost everything.

He thought about ten more years of binding work. About hands that would end up like Feng's—twisted, useless, unable to grip a hoe or hold a grandchild.

He thought about leaving. About walking out the gate with thirty taels of debt and damaged hands and nothing else.

It wasn't a good choice. But it was a choice.

Wei got out of his bunk. Went to the small desk in the corner of the dormitory. Found paper and a brush.

He wrote a letter to his father.

*I'm coming home. I still owe the archive thirty taels. They'll send collectors. I'm sorry. I tried. It wasn't enough.*

He sealed the letter. He'd send it tomorrow.

Then he'd submit his termination request.

Then he'd go home.

## Part Eight: The Reassignment

Chen's awakening had consequences.

He started asking questions. Not publicly—he wasn't stupid. But in private conversations with other supervisors, with the archive's physician, with the clerk who managed the lending records.

How many clerks carried debt? (Two hundred and forty out of three hundred.)

What was the average debt? (Eighteen taels.)

How many clerks had extended their contracts due to debt? (One hundred and twelve.)

What was the average duration of employment before hand damage became severe? (Eleven years.)

How many clerks received full pension? (Thirty-seven percent. The rest were dismissed for quota failure before reaching pension eligibility.)

Chen compiled the numbers into a report. Not a complaint. Just data. He submitted it to Director Zhao with a cover note: *For your information.*

Zhao called him to his office the next day.

"Chen, what is this?"

"Data on clerk retention and debt."

"I can see that. Why did you compile it?"

"I thought it might be useful for planning purposes."

Zhao set the report on his desk. "Chen, I'm going to be direct. This report suggests systemic problems. Problems that, if raised to the Ministry, would reflect poorly on my management. Do you understand?"

"I'm not raising anything to the Ministry. I'm giving you information."

"Information I didn't ask for. Information that creates problems." Zhao leaned forward. "You've been a good supervisor for twelve years. Don't ruin that now."

"I'm not trying to ruin anything. I'm trying to understand how the system works."

"You understand how it works. You've worked here for twenty-seven years. The system works by maintaining quota, managing costs, and not asking questions that don't have good answers."

Chen said nothing.

Zhao sighed. "I'm reassigning you. Effective immediately, you'll supervise the binding section. Supervisor Liu is retiring. It's a lateral move—same salary, same rank. But binding has no quota pressure. It's less... complicated."

"You're removing me because I asked questions."

"I'm reassigning you because the binding section needs a supervisor and you're qualified. Take it or don't. But if you don't, I'll have to consider whether you're suited for supervisory work at all."

Chen took it.

He moved to the binding section the next week. The work was quieter. No quota reports. No dismissals. Just clerks folding pages and stitching volumes.

Wei was there. Chen recognized him from the termination interview.

"Wei," Chen said. "How are the hands?"

Wei held them up. Still shaking, but less than before.

"Better than they were. Binding is easier."

"Good."

They didn't speak about the quota. They didn't speak about the debt. They worked in the same building, on different sides of the same system.

Chen went home each night to his wife and children. His daughter had passed her exam. His son was doing well in his apprenticeship. The family was stable.

But Chen couldn't stop thinking about the numbers. Two hundred and forty clerks in debt. Eighteen taels average. Eleven years until severe hand damage.

He started writing again. Not reports. Documentation. A comprehensive record of how the system worked and what it cost.

He wrote in the early mornings, before work. His wife asked what he was writing.

"Just notes," he said.

She didn't ask again.

Chen wrote. The archive continued. Three hundred clerks copying documents that would fade in ten years instead of twenty. The system functioning exactly as designed.

He documented it all.

For the record.

## Part Nine: The Return

Wei left the archive on a cold morning in the tenth month. He'd submitted his termination request, accepted the garnishment of his final wages, packed his belongings into two small bags.

Feng walked him to the gate.

"You're sure about this?" Feng asked.

"No. But I'm doing it anyway."

"The collectors will come."

"Let them come."

They stood at the gate. Behind them, the archive complex—the Long Hall, the administrative building, the dormitories. Five years of Wei's life. Five years of copying documents, damaging his hands, accumulating debt.

"What will you do?" Feng asked.

"Go home. Work the land. Try to heal."

"Your hands won't fully heal."

"I know. But maybe they'll heal enough."

Feng held out his twisted hands. "This is what twenty-three years looks like. You're leaving after five. That's something."

"Is it?"

"Yes. You're leaving while you can still grip a hoe. While you can still work. That's more than most clerks get."

Wei looked at his hands. Wrapped in cloth, swollen, aching. But still functional. Barely.

"Thank you," Wei said. "For everything."

"I didn't do anything."

"You told me I had a choice."

Feng nodded. "Good luck, Wei."

Wei walked through the gate. He didn't look back.

---

The journey home took six days. He walked most of it—couldn't afford a horse, couldn't afford a cart. His hands throbbed with each step. At night, he slept in roadside inns, paying with the few coins he had left.

On the sixth day, he reached his village. Small, poor, familiar. He walked through the fields to his father's house.

His father was in the field, working the five *mu* of land. Good soil, as promised. Wheat growing in neat rows.

Wei stopped at the edge of the field. His father looked up, saw him, froze.

They stared at each other across the distance.

Wei walked forward. His father set down his hoe.

"Wei," his father said.

"Father."

His father looked at Wei's wrapped hands. At his thin frame. At his face, which had aged five years in five years.

"You're home early," his father said.

"Yes."

"Your contract—"

"I broke it."

His father's face went still. "The debt?"

"I still owe twenty-five taels. The archive will send collectors."

His father said nothing. He picked up his hoe. Set it down. Picked it up again. His hands were shaking.

"Forty taels," his father said. "I sent you forty taels. Five years of saving. The land I sold to buy this land."

"I know."

"You were supposed to work five years. Come home free. Help work this land."

"I know."

"And you come back—" His father's voice cracked. "You come back with nothing. Worse than nothing. With debt."

Wei said nothing. There was nothing to say.

His father turned away. Looked at the field. At the wheat growing in neat rows.

"Do you know what I gave up?" his father said. "Your mother and I, we ate millet for two years. Millet, Wei. Not rice. We sold the good land—the land by the river, the land my father left me. We sold it to send you to the capital. Because you were smart. Because the archive was a good position. Because forty taels could buy this land and we'd all be better."

"Father—"

"Don't." His father's voice was hard. "Don't tell me you're sorry. Don't tell me you tried. I know you tried. But trying isn't enough. Trying doesn't feed us. Trying doesn't pay the debt."

Wei felt something break in his chest.

His father stood with his back to him for a long moment. Then: "Show me your hands."

Wei unwrapped the cloth. His father turned, took Wei's hands, examined them. Pressed the joints gently. Wei winced.

"Can you grip a hoe?" his father asked.

"I don't know. I haven't tried."

His father went to the house, came back with a hoe. Handed it to Wei.

Wei tried to grip it. His fingers wouldn't close fully. The joints screamed. But he could hold it. Barely.

"You'll need to learn a different grip," his father said. His voice was still hard. "Less pressure on the joints. More on the palms. I'll show you."

Wei looked at his father. "You're not sending me away?"

"Where would you go? You have no money. No skills except copying, and you can't do that anymore. You're my son. You're home." His father's jaw was tight. "But don't mistake this for forgiveness. I'm angry, Wei. I'm so angry I can barely look at you. But you're my son. So you'll stay. You'll work. You'll help pay this debt you brought home."

Wei's mother appeared at the door. She looked at Wei, at his father, at the hoe in Wei's damaged hands.

She didn't cry. She didn't speak. She went back inside.

"Come," his father said. "I'll show you how to hold the hoe. Tomorrow we start working. The collectors will come. We'll pay what we can. Five taels per year from the land's surplus. It'll take five years. Maybe more."

They walked to the house. His younger brother was there, sixteen now, almost a man. He looked at Wei with something that wasn't quite respect and wasn't quite contempt.

That night, Wei lay in his old room. His hands throbbed. Through the wall, he could hear his parents talking in low voices. His mother's voice was sharp. His father's was tired.

He'd chosen this. Chosen to leave. Chosen to bring the debt home.

The next morning, his father woke him before dawn. They walked to the field together.

"Hold the hoe like this," his father said, demonstrating. "Palms flat, fingers loose. Don't grip. Let the weight of the hoe do the work."

Wei tried. His hands screamed. But the grip held.

"Good. Now, small movements. Don't try to break the earth. Just scratch it. Let the hoe's edge cut."

Wei worked for an hour. His hands were shaking by the end. But he'd turned over a small section of earth.

"That's enough for today," his father said. "Tomorrow, a little more. In a month, you'll be able to work a full day."

"Will my hands heal?"

His father looked at Wei's hands. "No. The damage is permanent. But they'll strengthen. You'll learn what they can do and what they can't. That's not healing. But it's something."

They walked back to the house. Wei's mother had breakfast ready—rice porridge, pickled vegetables. Simple food. But warm.

She still wouldn't look at him.

After breakfast, Wei went to the field alone. His father was repairing a fence. His brother was studying—he'd take the examinations next year.

Wei gripped the hoe and worked. The earth was hard. His hands were harder. He worked until midday, until his hands wouldn't close anymore.

He sat at the edge of the field. Looked at his hands. Looked at the wheat growing in neat rows.

Three hundred clerks were still in the Long Hall. Still copying. Still meeting quota. Still damaging their hands.

The system was still running.

He was out. Broken, but out.

He picked up the hoe and went back to work.

Chen worked binding for three years. His hands adapted to the repetitive motion, though they never stopped aching. He lived on half salary, moved his family to smaller housing, watched his children take their examinations with borrowed money.

His daughter passed. His son failed.

At the end of three years, he took his pension. It was enough to survive, not enough to maintain their previous comfort.

He started writing.

Not reports. Not recommendations. A comprehensive documentation of the archive's extraction system. How it worked. Why it worked. What it cost.

He wrote about the quota ratchet. The debt trap. The coordination barrier. The paper crisis. The clerks who broke. The supervisors who looked away.

He wrote about Wei. About Feng. About the Wang brothers. About Director Zhao's final report.

He wrote for three hours every morning, at a small desk in their small apartment. His daughter, now working as a clerk in a merchant house, asked him once why he bothered.

"Because someone needs to say it was wrong," Chen said.

She didn't ask again.

His wife stopped asking after the first year. She knew he'd keep writing whether she understood or not.

Chen wrote. The archive continued. Three hundred clerks copying documents that would fade to nothing. New clerks replacing the ones who broke. The system functioning exactly as designed.

He wrote it all down.
