# STAGE 8: PACING AND SUBTRACTION

## SECTION 1: REVISED NARRATIVE

# The Ink Debt

## Part One: The Long Hall

The tremor starts in my right thumb on the third hour. I know this the way I know morning comes after night. I'm copying a land dispute from Henan — characters I don't read, just reproduce. My brush moves right to left, column after column. The tremor makes the vertical strokes waver. Not much. Maybe Chen won't notice.

I flex my fingers. Count the completed folios stacked to my left. Nineteen. Quota is forty-two. The sun through the high windows says it's past midday. My hand says I won't make it.

Old Feng sits across from me, his brush moving with the slowness of winter honey. His joints click when he flexes his fingers. The sound carries even in the Long Hall's constant noise — three hundred brushes on paper, three hundred clerks breathing, coughing, shifting. Feng catches my eye. Nods at my stack. Raises three fingers.

Three folios. He'll cover three.

I nod back. The gratitude sits in my throat like a stone. Three folios is two hours of his pain. His hands barely work anymore. But three folios means I might make quota. Might not add to the debt that already owns the next four months of my life.

The wall examination is tomorrow. My work from yesterday is probably on the wall now. I don't look. If it's there, I'll know soon enough. The mockery carries across the hall.

I dip my brush. The ink is thin today — the supply master waters it to stretch the budget. The characters come out gray instead of black. Chen will mark that in his inspection. But what am I supposed to do? Buy my own ink? With what money?

A clerk three desks down stands suddenly. His stool scrapes. He walks toward the doors, fast but not running. Supervisor Liu intercepts him.

"Where are you going?"

"Latrine."

"You went an hour ago."

"I need to go again."

Liu studies him. The clerk's hands are shaking. Not the tremor I have — the kind that comes from something breaking inside. Liu nods. The clerk leaves. He won't come back. We all know this. Tomorrow someone new will sit at his desk. The Board has a list.

I copy. The characters blur. I blink. Focus. A magistrate's decision about water rights. The plaintiff gets the upstream position. The defendant must accept downstream flow. Fair decision. The magistrate's seal is at the bottom. I copy it carefully. The seal is the hardest part — one mistake and the whole folio is void.

My thumb trembles. The seal wobbles. I hold my breath. Finish the stroke. It's acceptable. Barely.

Twenty folios.

Feng is on his second folio since I started counting. His face shows nothing but I know that blank expression. It's the face he makes when the pain is worst. He's covering for me and it's costing him.

"Old Feng," I whisper. "Don't—"

He doesn't look up. His brush moves. I shut my mouth.

The afternoon light slants lower. The hall gets colder. My fingers stiffen. I switch to my left hand for two folios. The characters are uglier but they pass inspection if I'm careful. Left hand means I'm already in trouble. I'm twenty-eight years old. I shouldn't need my left hand yet.

A bell rings. Afternoon break. Fifteen minutes.

I stand. My back cracks. Feng stands more slowly, his hands pressed flat on the desk until his knees lock. We walk to the courtyard together. Other clerks cluster in their groups. We stand alone. Feng never had patience for gossip and I learned the habit from him.

"Your hands are worse," Feng says.

"I'm managing."

"You're not managing. You're borrowing from tomorrow to pay today."

"What else am I supposed to do?"

Feng says nothing. There is no answer. We both know this.

"The wall has your work from yesterday," he says finally.

My stomach drops. "Bad?"

"Acceptable. But they're saying it's Wei-style calligraphy." He almost smiles. "You're becoming famous."

Wei-style calligraphy. The term for barely-good-enough. The term for a clerk who's cutting corners. I should be ashamed. But shame doesn't make my hands steadier.

"Chen pulled me aside this morning," Feng says. "Asked about you."

"What did you say?"

"That you're learning. That you need time."

"I've been here five years."

"I told him five years isn't enough for some hands."

The bell rings. Break over. We walk back. I copy. Twenty-three folios. Twenty-five. My thumb trembles constantly now. I switch back to left hand. The characters are worse. I don't care. I just need the count.

Thirty folios.

Feng slides three completed folios onto my stack. I don't thank him. Thanks would make it worse. I just nod. Keep copying.

The sun sets. The hall grows dark. Attendants light lamps. The oil smoke makes my eyes water. Or maybe I'm just tired. Thirty-six folios. Thirty-eight.

I won't make quota. Even with Feng's three, I'm short. Four folios short means four copper coins deducted from tomorrow's pay. Four coins I don't have. Which means I'll borrow from Chen. Again. At interest. Again.

The evening bell rings. Work ends. I stack my folios. Sign the count sheet. Thirty-eight. The supervisor marks it without comment. I see his notation: *Shortfall: 4.*

Outside, the street is dark. Feng walks with me as far as the Archive District. His dormitory is there — a Board building for elderly clerks. My boarding house is in Pottery District. Forty minutes' walk.

"Come by tomorrow morning," Feng says. "Before the hall opens. I'll show you a grip that helps with the tremor."

"You've shown me every grip there is."

"Then I'll show you again."

I watch him walk toward the dormitory. His gait is uneven. His left knee doesn't bend right anymore. Forty years in the Long Hall did that. I have twelve years before my hands fail completely. Maybe fifteen if I'm lucky.

I walk to Pottery District. The boarding house is cold. The landlady charges extra for brazier coal. I can't afford extra. I eat cold rice and pickled vegetables. Calculate my debt.

Three months behind on quota. Borrowed fifteen silver taels from Chen at monthly interest. Borrowed eight taels from Supervisor Liu at higher interest. Send home two taels monthly to my father. That's obligation, not optional. He sold land to pay for my examination fees. I owe him everything.

The math doesn't work. It hasn't worked for months. I keep copying anyway. What else is there?

I sleep poorly. Dream of brush strokes that won't straighten. Wake before dawn with my hands already aching.

## Part Two: The Paper

Chen Daowen held the document up to the window light. Three months old. Already yellowing at the edges. He compared it to a document from six months ago — the yellowing had spread inward, a slow stain eating the characters.

He had noticed this a month ago. Written a report. The Board said they were monitoring the situation.

The documents were degrading. Not eventually. Now.

He walked to the supply master's office carrying both documents. Master Wu was sorting through paper shipments, checking them against delivery receipts.

"Master Wu, I need to show you something."

Wu glanced up. "Busy, Supervisor Chen."

"This will take a moment." Chen laid both documents on Wu's desk. "Three months versus six months. The degradation is accelerating."

Wu examined them without touching. "Jiangsu paper. The Board approved it three months ago. That's what you're seeing."

"It's not lasting."

"All paper degrades eventually."

"This is degrading in months. Fujian paper lasted years." Chen kept his voice level. "The documents we're copying won't survive."

Wu finally looked at him directly. "The Board approved Jiangsu paper because Fujian paper costs forty percent more. We employ three hundred clerks. Salaries must be paid. Paper must be purchased. The budget is what it is."

"So we compromise the archive's purpose to balance the budget?"

"We compromise nothing. The Board reviewed samples. They determined Jiangsu paper was adequate."

"Adequate for what? For ten years? Twenty? These documents are court records. Tax assessments. Land deeds. They need to last centuries."

Wu's face hardened. "Take it to the Board if you're concerned. I order what they approve. That's my job. What's yours?"

Chen walked back to his chamber. Sat at his desk. Looked at the yellowing documents.

That afternoon he attended the regular Board meeting. Five retired governors sat at the long table. Chen presented Section Three's performance reports, then raised the paper issue.

"Governor Zhao, I'm seeing continued degradation in the Jiangsu paper. Documents from three months ago are already compromised."

Governor Zhao nodded. "Your concern is noted. The Board is monitoring the situation."

"Monitoring isn't addressing. The degradation is measurable. Rapid. These documents won't last."

Governor Lin leaned forward. "Supervisor Chen, we appreciate your diligence. But the Board has reviewed this matter thoroughly. Fujian paper is cost-prohibitive. The archive's budget cannot sustain it. Jiangsu paper is adequate for current needs."

"Current needs. But what about—"

"The Board will revisit paper sourcing when budget allows. For now, our decision stands. Please focus on copying quality rather than supply matters."

Chen felt something shift in his chest. A small crack in a structure he'd believed was solid.

"Understood," he said.

He returned to his chamber. Looked at his desk — neat stacks of documents awaiting verification, his personal seal, the pot of ink he mixed himself from good pine soot. He had earned this space. Twenty-two years of believing that precision mattered. That standards meant something.

That evening he walked home through familiar streets. His house in Archive District was modest but comfortable. His wife greeted him at the door. His children ran to him — his daughter was seven, his son five. They asked about his day. He told them it was fine.

At dinner, his wife studied his face. "Something's wrong."

"Just work matters."

"What matters?"

He told her about the paper. The degradation. The Board's response. She listened, then said: "Can't you insist they fix it?"

"The Board makes those decisions, not supervisors."

"But you're responsible for quality."

"I'm responsible for copying quality. Paper sourcing is above my position."

She looked at him with something like disappointment.

That night, lying awake while his wife slept beside him, Chen thought about the word *adequate*. Thought about documents that wouldn't last. Thought about the careful work of three hundred clerks being copied onto paper that was already dying.

Thought about what it meant to maintain standards in a structure that had abandoned them.

## Part Three: The Wall

The wall examination happens every third day. This morning it's my folio on display. I know before I see it — the way other clerks glance at me, then away. The way Feng's face goes blank.

I walk to the western wall with everyone else. My work is pinned center: a tax document from Shanxi. The characters are visible from across the room. They're acceptable. They're also obviously mine — the slightly compressed vertical strokes, the way the horizontal lines tilt up at the end. Wei-style.

Supervisor Liu stands beside the wall. He doesn't speak at first. Just lets us look. This is the method: let the clerks examine, let them draw conclusions, let the mockery come from peers rather than authority. More effective that way.

A clerk named Hu speaks first. Hu's work is always perfect. He'll be promoted within a year.

"The stroke order is correct," he says. "But look at the character for 'silver.' The radical is compressed. Makes it look like 'debt.'"

Laughter. Not cruel laughter. Just... accurate. The character does look like debt. I did compress it. I was tired. My hand was shaking. But reasons don't matter. Only the work matters.

Another clerk: "The seal is wobbling. See how the circle isn't closed?"

More examination. More observations. My face is hot. I keep it blank. This is the skill you learn in the Long Hall — how to stand while your work is dissected. How to nod when the criticism is valid. How to laugh when the joke is on you.

"Wei-style calligraphy," someone says. "Acceptable but not excellent."

The phrase again. It's becoming my name. Wei-the-acceptable. Wei-the-barely-adequate. Wei-whose-work-will-never-be-promoted.

Supervisor Liu finally speaks. "The work passes inspection. But barely. Wei, you're capable of better. I've seen your early folios. What happened?"

What happened? My hands happened. The debt happened. The quota that rises faster than my skill happened. But I can't say that. Can't say the structure is grinding me down, because the structure isn't grinding anyone else down. Hu makes quota. Half the clerks in this room make quota. If I can't, it's because I'm weak.

"I'll improve, Supervisor."

"See that you do. The Board reviews below-quota clerks next month."

The examination ends. We return to our desks. I sit. Pick up my brush. My hand shakes worse than yesterday. The wall examination does this — makes the tremor worse for hours afterward. Fear response. Body knowing it's being evaluated.

I copy. Try to focus. But the characters blur. I think about next month's Board review. What happens to clerks who don't pass review? Reassignment to binding work. Half pay. Or dismissal. Return to the village. Tell my father I failed.

Can't think about that. Just copy. One character after another. Don't read. Don't think. Just reproduce.

Feng slides a cup of tea onto my desk. I didn't see him get up. The tea is still hot. He must have used his own coal ration to heat it. I drink. It helps. Warmth in my chest. Small kindness in a structure with no kindness built in.

"Ignore them," Feng says quietly.

"They're right. My work is barely acceptable."

"Acceptable is acceptable. That's why they call it acceptable."

I almost smile. Feng's logic has a way of cutting through shame. Acceptable is the standard. I meet the standard. Therefore I'm succeeding. Except the standard keeps rising and my hands keep failing and eventually acceptable won't be enough.

But for today, for this morning, Feng's words help. I copy. Make quota by evening. Barely. With Feng covering four folios. Four folios that cost him pain I can see in his face.

That night I don't go home. I stay in the courtyard after the evening bell. Other clerks leave. The hall empties. I sit on a stone bench and watch the sky darken.

A clerk named Zhao sits beside me. I know him slightly — desk in the middle section, performance average, borrowed money from Supervisor Liu last year.

"The wall examination was rough today," he says.

"I've had worse."

"We all have." Zhao pauses. "Some of us were talking. About the quotas. How they keep rising."

I look at him. His face is careful. Neutral. But there's something underneath. An invitation.

"Quotas rise," I say. "That's how it works."

"But what if they didn't have to? What if we... talked to the Board. As a group. Explained that the increases are unsustainable."

My heart speeds up. This is dangerous talk. This is the talk that gets clerks marked. Gets them assigned impossible quotas. Gets them forced out.

"The Board sets quotas for reasons we don't see," I say. "Budget reasons. Efficiency reasons."

"Or maybe they set quotas because they can. Because we don't push back."

I stand. "I need to go home."

"Wei—"

"I'm not interested."

I walk away. Fast. Not running but close. Zhao's words follow me. *Because we don't push back.* As if pushing back is possible. As if three hundred clerks could coordinate. As if the supervisors wouldn't notice. As if the Board wouldn't respond.

I've heard this before. Two years ago, a clerk named Ming tried to organize. Gathered signatures on a petition. Requested a meeting with the Board. The meeting never happened. Ming's quota was doubled. He lasted three weeks before defaulting on his loans.

I know what happened to Ming because I saw him six months later. I was walking through the industrial district, past the brick kilns, and there he was. Stacking bricks in the yard. His hands were wrapped in rags. The brick dust had gotten into his lungs — I could hear him coughing from across the street.

I didn't approach him. Didn't want him to see me. Didn't want to see his face. But I watched him work for a few minutes. Watched the way he moved — careful, slow, like every motion hurt. His hands had failed in the Long Hall. Now they were failing faster in the kiln.

That's what happens when you try to organize. That's what happens when you push back.

I walk home. Forty minutes through dark streets. My boarding house is cold. I eat cold rice. The math is worse than yesterday. Chen's loan extension helps but doesn't solve. I'm still drowning. Just drowning slower.

I sleep badly. Dream of walls covered in my flawed characters. Dream of hands that won't grip the brush. Wake before dawn with my heart racing.

## Part Four: The Attempt

Zhao approaches me again three days later. This time in the courtyard during evening break. He's careful. Speaks quietly. Checks that no supervisors are nearby.

"Four of us are meeting tonight. After work. In the tea house on Pottery Street. Just to talk."

"Talk about what?"

"The quotas. The paper. Things we all see but don't discuss."

"Discussing won't change anything."

"Maybe not. But staying silent definitely won't change anything."

I should say no. Should walk away like last time. But something in me is breaking. Three months behind on quota. Hands getting worse. Wall examination last week was humiliating. Chen's loan extension bought time but not solution. The math doesn't work and it's never going to work.

"Just to talk?" I ask.

"Just to talk."

I nod. Zhao walks away. I immediately regret it. This is stupid. Dangerous. But I don't chase him down. Don't take it back.

That evening I tell Feng I'm going home. He looks at me with that expression that says he knows I'm lying but won't press. I leave the archive. Walk to Pottery Street. The tea house is small, cheap, frequented by laborers. Not clerks. That's probably why Zhao chose it.

Four others are there when I arrive. Zhao. A woman named Lin from Section Five. Two brothers named Wang. We sit in the back corner. Order cheap tea. Speak quietly.

"The quotas are impossible," Lin says. "They raise them every year. My hands are failing and I'm thirty-two."

"The paper is degrading," one of the Wang brothers says. "We're copying the empire's memory onto garbage."

"The loans are predatory," the other Wang says. "Chen charges fair interest but Supervisor Liu charges twice that. I'm paying more in interest than I earn some months."

They all look at me. Waiting. I'm the one who was on the wall last week. The one everyone knows is struggling.

"We meet quota, they raise quota," I say. "We borrow to survive, the debt compounds. Our hands fail, they replace us."

Silence. The words hang in the air. Too direct. Too accurate. We're not supposed to name it that clearly.

"So what do we do?" Zhao asks.

"Organize," Lin says. "Present demands to the Board. All of us together."

"They'll never meet with us," I say.

"Then we stop working until they do."

"That's refusing duty. They'll dismiss us all."

"They can't dismiss three hundred clerks. The archive doesn't function without us."

We talk for two hours. Make plans. Lin will draft a petition. The Wang brothers will gather signatures. Zhao will identify which clerks might join. I'll talk to Feng, see if the older clerks will support us.

"We need to be careful," Zhao says. "Talk to people individually. Don't gather in groups. Don't discuss this in the Long Hall."

"When do we present the petition?" Lin asks.

"Two weeks. That gives us time to gather signatures. Time to build support."

We leave the tea house separately. I walk home with something like hope in my chest. Maybe this can work. Maybe if enough of us coordinate, the Board will have to listen. Maybe the math can change.

The hope lasts one day.

The next morning, Supervisor Liu calls me to his office before the hall opens. He doesn't invite me to sit.

"You were seen at a tea house on Pottery Street. With other clerks."

My stomach drops. "We were having tea."

"You were conspiring."

"We were talking. Talking isn't conspiracy."

"Talking about the Board is conspiracy. Talking about quotas is conspiracy." Liu's face is hard. "The Board has been generous with you, Wei. Extended your loan. Tolerated below-quota performance. And this is how you repay that generosity?"

"I just want to survive. We all just want to survive."

"Then survive by working harder. Not by plotting."

He dismisses me. I return to my desk. My hands shake so badly I can't hold the brush. Feng watches me. Says nothing. Knows better than to ask.

That afternoon, my quota is increased. Fifty folios instead of forty-two. The notation says: *Performance improvement required.*

I find Zhao in the courtyard during evening break. "They know."

His face goes pale. "How?"

"Someone saw us. Reported us. Liu called me in this morning. My quota's been increased."

"Mine too. Fifty folios." He looks sick. "Lin?"

"I don't know. Haven't seen her."

We find out the next day. Lin's quota has been increased to fifty-five. The Wang brothers have been reassigned to binding work. Their desks are already occupied by new clerks.

The attempt is over. We didn't even get to draft the petition. The structure saw us organizing and crushed us before we could act.

That evening, Zhao finds me in the courtyard. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have approached you."

"You were trying to help."

"I made it worse. For all of us."

He walks away. I sit alone on the bench. Watch the sky darken. Think about Ming in the brick kiln. Think about the Wang brothers in the bindery, earning half what they earned before. Think about my new quota — fifty folios. Eight more than I could barely manage before.

The math doesn't work. It's never going to work. And now the Board knows I tried to organize. And next month's review is coming.

## Part Five: The Conversation

Chen found Wei in the courtyard after the evening bell. The young clerk was sitting alone on a stone bench, staring at his hands.

"Wei. May I speak with you?"

Wei looked up. His face was wary. "Yes, Supervisor."

"Not as supervisor. Just... may I speak with you?"

Wei nodded. Chen sat beside him on the bench. For a moment neither spoke. The archive was quiet around them — three hundred clerks gone home, the Long Hall empty.

"I increased your quota," Chen said finally. "After Supervisor Liu reported your meeting at the tea house. I want to explain why."

"You don't need to explain. You were following orders."

"I was. But I want you to understand the reasoning. The Board believes that collective organization undermines individual merit. That if clerks can negotiate as a group, the structure stops rewarding excellence and starts rewarding political coordination."

"I understand the reasoning."

"Do you agree with it?"

Wei looked at him with something like surprise. "Does it matter if I agree?"

"It matters to me. I've been thinking about your situation. About why you're struggling. I've told myself it's because you lack dedication. But I'm not sure that's true anymore."

Wei said nothing. Chen continued.

"I survived the Long Hall. Twenty-two years ago. I made quota. I believed the structure rewarded dedication, and it did. I was promoted. But I've been thinking about why I succeeded. Was it because I was more dedicated than you? Or was it because my hands were stronger? Because I was lucky?"

"You worked hard," Wei said. "That's not luck."

"But I worked hard with hands that could handle the work. What if they couldn't? What if I had your tremor? Would I have succeeded? Or would I have failed the same way you're failing?"

Wei looked at his hands. "I don't know."

"I don't know either. And that uncertainty... it troubles me. Because if success is just biological luck, then the structure isn't measuring merit. It's measuring something else."

"What are you asking me, Supervisor?"

Chen took a breath. "Tell me what the quota structure feels like. From your position. Not what it's supposed to feel like. What it actually feels like."

Wei was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful.

"It feels like drowning. Every day I copy as fast as I can. My hands hurt. The tremor gets worse. But I still don't make quota. So I borrow. From you, from Supervisor Liu. The debt compounds. I fall further behind. The Board raises the quota. I fall even further behind. There's no way out. Just drowning slower or faster."

"But the quota is achievable. Other clerks make it."

"Other clerks have stronger hands. Or they started younger. Or they're just... built different. I'm not lazy, Supervisor. I work as hard as anyone in the Long Hall. But my hands can't do what the quota demands."

"Then perhaps archival work isn't your calling."

"That's what everyone says. But I passed the examinations. I can read and write. I can copy. Just not at the speed the quota demands. Does that mean I have no calling? Or does it mean the quota is wrong?"

Chen felt the crack in his chest widen. "The quota is set by the Board. Based on efficiency data."

"What efficiency data? Did they measure how fast hands can move? Did they account for the tremor? For the arthritis? For the fact that hands fail faster when you're copying on cheap paper with watered ink?"

"The Board has information we don't have. Budget constraints. Political considerations."

"Or maybe they just set the quota as high as they can and replace whoever fails. Maybe that's the information. Maybe the efficiency they're measuring isn't copying speed. Maybe it's how many clerks they can cycle through before anyone organizes."

The words hung between them. Chen felt something inside him resisting. This was too radical. Too cynical. The Board was composed of retired governors. Men with decades of administrative experience. They wouldn't deliberately design a structure to break people.

Would they?

"You think the structure is intentionally extractive," Chen said.

"I think the structure does what it does. Whether it's intentional doesn't matter. The outcome is the same. Clerks work until their hands fail. They borrow until they default. They're replaced. The archive functions. The Board's budget balances. And everyone says it's merit."

"But I succeeded. The structure rewarded me."

"You succeeded because your hands were strong enough. Not because you were more dedicated than me. You got lucky. And now you're a supervisor. You enforce the quotas. You offer the loans. You help the structure break people like me. And you tell yourself it's merit."

Chen felt the words like a physical blow. He wanted to argue. Wanted to defend himself. But Wei's face showed no anger. Just exhaustion. Just the flat truth of someone who had nothing left to lose.

"I didn't know," Chen said. "I thought... I believed the structure was fair."

"Maybe it was fair to you. From your position. But from mine, it's just extraction."

They sat in silence. The courtyard was dark now. Cold. Chen thought about his twenty-two years in the archive. The comfort he'd earned. The house in Archive District. His children's future. All of it built on a structure that looked like merit from his position but looked like extraction from Wei's.

"What would you change?" Chen asked. "If you could?"

"Lower the quotas. Stop raising them every year. Forgive the debts. Use better paper. Let clerks organize without punishment. Acknowledge that hands fail and that's not a moral failure."

"The Board would never accept those changes."

"I know. That's why I'm drowning."

Chen stood. His legs felt unsteady. "I'm sorry, Wei. For not seeing this sooner. For not understanding."

"You're seeing it now. That's something."

"Is it? What difference does it make if I see it but can't change it?"

Wei looked at him. "I don't know, Supervisor. You tell me."

Chen walked home through dark streets. His house was warm. His wife had left dinner prepared. His children greeted him at the door. Everything was as it should be.

Except it wasn't. Because his comfort was built on Wei's pain. His success was enabled by a structure that broke people and called it merit. And he had enforced that structure for eight years without questioning it.

That night he couldn't sleep. He lay awake thinking about Wei's words. About the quota structure. About what it meant to maintain standards in a structure that had abandoned fairness.

About what it meant to be complicit.

## Part Six: The Hand

Feng's hands fail completely on a Tuesday morning in the ninth month. He's covering my quota — his daily gift of four folios that keeps me barely surviving. His brush stops mid-stroke. His fingers won't close. The brush falls.

I look up. Feng's face is gray. He's holding his right hand with his left, trying to force the fingers to bend. They won't.

"Old Feng—"

"It's nothing. Just stiff."

But it's not nothing. I've watched his hands decline for five years. This is the moment we both knew was coming. The moment when the arthritis wins completely.

I stand. Help him to the courtyard. Sit him on a bench. His hand is swollen. The joints are hot to touch. He's been pushing too hard, covering my quota on top of his training duties. His hands couldn't take it.

"I need to get you to a physician."

"Physicians cost money. Money I don't have."

"I'll borrow—"

"From who? You're already drowning in debt. I won't add to it."

We sit in silence. Other clerks pass by. Some glance at us. Most don't. An old clerk with failed hands is common enough. Not worth noticing.

"You can't cover my quota anymore," I say.

"I know."

"I can't make quota without you."

"I know that too."

The math is simple. Without Feng's four folios, I'm eight folios short daily. Eight folios means sixteen copper coins deducted per day. Eighty coins per week. More than I earn. The debt will compound faster. I'll default within a month.

"You should leave," Feng says. "Go home. Tell your father you tried. It's not shameful to admit when something's impossible."

"What about you?"

"I'm old. I've had my time. The Board will reassign me to binding work or dismiss me. Either way, I'll manage."

"You won't manage. Binding work pays half what you earn now. And you can't do binding work with those hands anyway."

"Then I'll do something else."

His voice is bitter. Not at me. At the structure. At forty years of service that bought him nothing but ruined hands and a cold dormitory room.

"I'm not leaving you," I say.

"Wei—"

"I'm not."

We sit until the break ends. Return to the hall. Feng sits at his desk. Stares at his hands. Doesn't pick up the brush. Can't pick up the brush.

I copy. Try to focus. But my own hands are shaking worse than ever. Fear response. Knowing that Feng's failure is preview of my own. Twelve years from now. Maybe less.

I make twenty-six folios that day. Twenty-four short of quota. The deduction is noted. Supervisor Liu watches me sign the count sheet. Says nothing. Doesn't need to say anything. The numbers speak.

That night I don't go home. I stay in the courtyard with Feng. We don't talk. Just sit. Watch the sky darken. The archive empties around us.

"I should have helped you organize," Feng says finally. "When you and Zhao tried. I should have supported it."

"Wouldn't have mattered. They crushed us before we started."

"Maybe. But at least we would have tried together."

"You've helped me every day for five years. That's enough."

"It's not enough. It didn't save you. It just delayed the inevitable."

The guard comes to close the archive. We leave. Feng walks slowly toward his dormitory. I walk toward Pottery District. Forty minutes through dark streets that feel longer tonight.

My boarding house is cold. I eat nothing. The math is impossible now. Without Feng's coverage, I have maybe three weeks before default. Three weeks before the Board review. Three weeks before everything collapses.

I don't sleep. Sit in darkness. Watch dawn come. Walk to the archive. Sit at my desk.

My hands won't stop shaking. I try to pick up the brush. It falls. I pick it up again. It falls again.

I put my head down on the desk. Just for a moment. Just to breathe.

And then I'm sobbing. Not quiet tears. My shoulders shake. I can't stop. Can't breathe. Snot runs down my face. My throat makes sounds I can't control.

Clerks around me stop working. Stare. I don't care. Let them stare. Let them see what the structure does. Let them see what happens when the math stops working.

Feng is beside me. His hand on my shoulder. His ruined hand that can't hold a brush anymore but can still offer this.

"Come on," he says gently. "Let's go outside."

He helps me stand. Walks me to the courtyard. Sits me on the bench. Waits while I cry. Doesn't tell me to stop. Doesn't tell me it will be okay. Just sits with me.

When I finally stop, when I can breathe again, he says: "We're leaving. Today. Both of us. We'll walk to your village. Your father will take us in or he won't. But we're done here."

"The debt—"

"The debt doesn't matter. They can pursue it or not. We won't be here to pay it."

"They'll come after my father."

"Maybe. But your father would rather have you alive and in debt than dead and paid up."

I look at Feng. His face is calm. Decided. He's already let go.

"What about you?" I ask. "You have nothing. No family. Nowhere to go."

"I have you. And your father's land. That's more than I have here."

We sit for a while longer. Then we stand. Walk back into the Long Hall. Collect our few things from our desks. Other clerks watch us. No one speaks.

We walk out through the main doors. Into the street. The morning sun is bright. Cold but bright.

We don't look back.

## Part Seven: The Review

The Board review happened in the Verification Chambers, not the Long Hall. More formal that way. More intimidating. Chen had attended dozens of these reviews over the years, usually as observer. Today he was presenting.

Five governors sat at the long table. Chen stood before them with performance reports for Section Three. Fourteen clerks were being reviewed. Wei's name was on the list, though Chen knew Wei had already left. Had walked out three days ago with Old Feng. Neither had returned.

Governor Zhao spoke first. "Section Three's performance has declined significantly over the past quarter. What's your assessment, Supervisor Chen?"

Chen had prepared his answer carefully. Had revised it three times. Had finally decided to speak the truth.

"The clerks are being broken by the quota structure. The increases are unsustainable. The paper is degrading. The loan arrangement traps them in debt. What we're calling merit is actually just biological luck."

Silence. The governors stared at him. Chen felt his career ending in real time. But he couldn't stop. The words came from somewhere deep. From his conversation with Wei. From twenty-two years of believing a lie.

"Wei should not be dismissed. None of these clerks should. They should be given reduced quotas, hand therapy, debt forgiveness. Not because it's generous. Because the current structure is destroying them."

Governor Zhao's face was cold. "Your assessment is noted, Supervisor Chen. But it's incorrect. The archive has functioned for centuries on these principles. If clerks cannot meet standards, they are not suited for archival work. That is not extraction. That is selection."

"Selection for what? For clerks with stronger hands? That's not merit."

"It's dedication. It's discipline. It's what you yourself demonstrated."

"I survived because I was lucky. Because my hands were stronger. Because I was promoted before they failed. Not because I was more dedicated than Wei."

Governor Lin leaned forward. "Supervisor Chen, you've been an exemplary employee for eight years. But this outburst suggests stress. Perhaps you need rest. A temporary reassignment to less demanding duties."

The threat was clear. Back down or lose your position.

Chen thought about his house in Archive District. His wife. His children. The comfort he'd earned. All of it built on a structure that broke people and called it merit.

He thought about Wei crying at his desk. About Feng's ruined hands. About the yellowing documents that wouldn't last. About twenty-two years of enforcing standards that were really just extraction.

"I don't need rest," Chen said. "The structure needs reform. And if you won't reform it, I won't enforce it."

Governor Zhao stood. "This review is concluded. Supervisor Chen, you are reassigned to binding work, effective immediately. Half salary. Report to the bindery tomorrow morning."

The governors left. Chen stood alone in the chamber. His hands were shaking. Not from hand decline. From fear. From relief.

He walked home through streets that felt different now. His house was the same. His wife was the same. His children greeted him at the door the same way they always did.

But he was different.

At dinner, his wife asked about his day. He told her everything. About the review. About the reassignment. About the salary cut.

She listened. Then said: "Can we afford this?"

"No. We'll have to move. Leave Archive District. Find somewhere cheaper."

"And the children's tutors?"

"We'll have to let them go."

She was quiet for a long time. Then: "Was it worth it?"

Chen thought about Wei. About Feng. About the fourteen clerks being dismissed or reassigned. About the structure that would continue grinding people down whether he enforced it or not.

"I don't know," he said. "But I couldn't keep enforcing it. Couldn't keep pretending it was merit when it was extraction."

His wife nodded. "Then we'll manage. We always do."

---

Two weeks later, Chen's family moved to Pottery District. The new house was smaller. One main room, two sleeping alcoves. The roof leaked. The walls were thin. At night they could hear their neighbors arguing.

His daughter cried when she learned they were leaving Archive District. "But all my friends are there."

"You'll make new friends," Chen's wife said.

"I don't want new friends. I want my old friends."

His son was quieter about it. But Chen saw the way he looked at their old house as they packed. The way his face closed when Chen explained they couldn't afford the tutors anymore.

"Will I still go to school?" his son asked.

"Yes. But a different school. A cheaper one."

"Will it be as good?"

Chen didn't answer. They both knew it wouldn't be.

The move took two days. Chen's colleagues from the archive didn't help. Didn't visit. Didn't acknowledge what had happened. He was a binding worker now. Half salary. No status. Invisible.

The first night in the new house, his daughter wouldn't stop crying. His wife held her, tried to comfort her, but the girl was inconsolable. "I hate it here. I hate this house. I want to go home."

"This is home now," Chen's wife said.

"It's not home. It's horrible."

Chen sat in the corner, listening. Felt the weight of what his choice had cost. Not just him. His children. His wife. They were paying for his refusal to enforce the structure. They were paying for his conscience.

His wife came to him after the children finally slept. Sat beside him in the dark.

"She'll adjust," she said. "Children do."

"She shouldn't have to adjust. She shouldn't have to pay for my choice."

"It wasn't just your choice. We're a family. We pay together."

"You didn't agree to this. I didn't ask you."

"You told me. That night. You told me what you were going to do. I could have stopped you."

"Would you have?"

She was quiet for a long time. "No. Because you were right. The structure is extraction. And someone had to say it."

"But our children—"

"Will grow up knowing their father stood for something. That's worth more than Archive District."

Chen wanted to believe her. But he could still hear his daughter's crying. Could still see his son's closed face. Could still feel the weight of what his conscience had cost them.

---

A month later, his son came home from the new school with a black eye. Chen's wife cleaned it, asked what happened. The boy wouldn't say.

"Tell me," Chen said.

"They said you were dismissed from the archive. That you're a binding worker now. That we're poor."

"What did you say?"

"I said it wasn't true. That you chose to leave. That you were standing up for something."

"And then?"

"They laughed. Said standing up for something is what failures say when they can't succeed."

Chen felt something break inside him. His son was being mocked because of his choice. His son was learning that conscience costs more than it's worth.

"They're wrong," Chen said. "Standing up for something matters."

"Does it? We lost our house. We lost our friends. We lost everything. For what?"

Chen had no answer.

That night he lay awake beside his wife. Thought about Wei. About whether Wei had made it to his village. About whether Wei's father had taken them in or turned them away. About whether any of it had mattered.

The structure persisted. The archive still functioned. The quotas still rose. The clerks still broke. Chen's refusal had changed nothing except his family's circumstances.

He had spoken the truth. And the truth had cost him everything. And the structure didn't care.

## Part Eight: The Village

We reach my village on the third day. My father is working his field. He sees us approaching. Puts down his hoe. Walks to meet us.

He looks at my hands. At Feng's hands. At our faces. Says nothing for a long moment.

"The archive?" he asks.

"Failed," I say. "I'm sorry. The examination fees, the land you sold—"

"You tried?"

"I tried. Every day for five years."

He nods slowly. Looks at Feng. "You're welcome here. Both of you. The land is small but it's enough."

Relief floods through me. But then I see my father's face more clearly. The new lines around his eyes. The way his shoulders slope. The thinness of his frame. The land sale aged him. My failure aged him.

We walk to his house. It's smaller than I remember. One room. Dirt floor. The roof sags. This is what he has left after selling the good land to pay for my examination fees.

That night we eat thin soup. Rice stretched with water. A few vegetables. My father apologizes for the meal. "The harvest was poor. Next year will be better."

But I can see it won't be better. The land he kept is poor land. Rocky. Badly drained. He sold the good parcels to fund my attempt. What remains barely sustains one person. It won't sustain three.

"The debt will follow us," I say. "The archive will pursue it. They might take what land you have left."

My father's face hardens. "Let them try."

"I'm sorry. I've destroyed everything you worked for."

"You tried to improve yourself. That's not destruction."

But it is destruction. I can see it in the small house. In the thin soup. In the neighbors who don't visit. They remember the land my father sold. The loans he took. The son who was supposed to bring honor. They remember and they judge.

The days develop a rhythm. We work the land. My hands still hurt but the work is different. Slower. No quotas. No supervisors. No wall examinations.

But the village isn't freedom. It's just a different trap.

The harvest is poor. My father's remaining land isn't enough to feed three people. We eat less. Ration carefully. The neighbors don't help. My father's pride won't let him ask and they wouldn't offer anyway.

Winter comes. The house is cold. We have no money for coal. We burn what wood we can find. It's not enough.

Feng gets sick. A cough that won't stop. We have no money for a physician. We try home remedies. They don't help. The cough gets worse.

One morning I wake and Feng is cold beside me. His face is peaceful. His hands are finally still.

We bury him in the field. My father says words. I can't speak. Can only stand there and feel the weight of it. Feng spent forty years in the Long Hall. Survived. Escaped. Made it three months in the village before dying in a cold house because we couldn't afford a physician.

After the burial, I sit in the field. My father sits beside me.

"I brought him here to die," I say.

"You brought him here to live. He had three months of freedom. That's more than he would have had in the Long Hall."

"Three months. And then death."

"We all die. The question is what we do with the time before."

Spring comes. We plant. My hands hurt but they work well enough for this. The tremor is still there but it doesn't matter for farm work. No quotas. No precision required. Just steady effort.

The crop grows. Not abundantly. But enough. We'll eat this year. Maybe.

One evening, a young man comes to our door. He's maybe twenty. His hands are ink-stained. He's wearing the jacket of an archive clerk.

"Are you Wei?" he asks.

"Yes."

"I was told you used to work at Zhongshan Archive. That you left."

"Who told you that?"

"Zhao. He said if I was thinking of leaving, I should talk to you first."

I look at him. See myself five years ago. See the hope. The belief that things might be different. The not-yet-broken hands.

"Come in," I say.

He sits. Tells his story. It's the same story. Quota too high. Hands failing. Debt compounding. Wall examinations. The structure grinding him down.

"Should I leave?" he asks.

I look around my father's small house. Think about the poor harvest. Think about Feng dying because we couldn't afford a physician. Think about my father's thinned frame and aged face.

"Leaving means poverty," I say. "Staying means being broken. There's no good choice."

"But you left."

"I did. And I'm alive. And I'm not copying. But I'm also not free. Just trapped differently."

He's quiet for a moment. Then: "Is it better? Being trapped differently?"

I think about this. About the Long Hall versus the village. About quota pressure versus hunger. About wall examinations versus my father's disappointed silence.

"I don't know," I say. "Ask me in a year. If I'm still alive."

He leaves. I watch him walk away in the darkness. Don't know if he'll stay at the archive or leave. Don't know if it matters.

That night I lie awake. Think about the young clerk. About Zhao still in the Long Hall. About Chen in the bindery. About the three hundred clerks copying on degrading paper with failing hands.

About how nothing I did mattered. The organizing attempt failed. My escape changed nothing. The structure persists.

My father is awake too. I can tell by his breathing.

"Do you regret it?" he asks. "Selling the land. Sending me to the examinations."

Long silence. Then: "Every day."

The words hit like a blow. But there's something honest in them. Something that feels more real than all the absolution I've been telling myself.

"I'm sorry," I say.

"So am I."

We lie in darkness. No comfort between us. Just the truth of what my attempt cost. What his investment bought. Nothing. Nothing but debt and hunger and a son who failed.

In the morning we work the field. The same work as yesterday. The same work as tomorrow. My hands hurt. The tremor persists. But there's no quota to meet. No supervisor to please. No wall to face.

Just this. Just survival. Just the slow grinding of days that look the same.

It's not freedom.

But it's not the archive either.

And maybe that's all there is.
