# The Breathing Contract

I count twelve breaths while the manipulator reboots. Always twelve. The floodlights go dark, and for those twelve breaths I'm suspended in absolute black at the bottom of the Pacific, four thousand two hundred meters down, and the only sound is the hull creaking and my own breathing and the distant hum of the recyclers processing the same air over and over.

I started counting in my second week. Not because anyone told me to. Because the darkness is absolute down here, and counting gives the darkness a shape. Twelve breaths: that's how long it takes for the system to flush and restart. Twelve breaths: that's how long I have to remember I'm still breathing.

The lights flicker back. The manipulator's claws open and close, testing. I guide them toward the nodule field, watching the density scanner. Forty-three percent below projected. Again.

My shift was supposed to end two hours ago, but the reboot cost me fourteen nodules. I need to make up the difference or Jin will file another penalty notice. I've had four this month. The tablet on my wrist shows the running total: 127,240 yuan. Yesterday it was 126,890. I made quota yesterday. The debt still grew.

Equipment rental: 340 yuan per shift. Habitat fees: 180 yuan per day. Food costs: 95 yuan per day. Medical expenses: 220 yuan this week for the antibiotics after I cut my hand on a manipulator housing. Penalty fees: 580 yuan for the day before yesterday when the current surged and scattered the nodules before I could harvest them.

The numbers are precise. The debt grows.

*A pressão está pior hoje*, Tomás whispers when I finally climb out of the control pod. The pressure is worse today. He's right. I can feel it in my chest, in the recycled air that tastes like metal and sweat, in the way the walls seem closer than yesterday.

"Debt?" he asks in Portuguese, quiet enough that the Engineers can't hear.

"One twenty-seven."

He winces. His is ninety-eight. He's been here four months less than me.

I climb to my bunk in pod C-14, fourth tier, and lie down in the space between the bodies above and below me. The woman beneath me is crying softly. She's new, only three weeks in. She still thinks she'll make quota.

I want to tell her it doesn't matter. That I made quota seventeen days last month and the debt grew anyway. But I don't. She'll learn.

I count my breaths until I fall asleep. Twelve, always twelve. The rhythm that means the system is working, the lights will come back, I'm still breathing.

---

Rafael arrives on the quarterly inspection submersible, and we're all called to the common area on the upper deck. I've never been up here before. The ceilings are three meters high. There's a viewport—an actual window looking out into the black water. I can see our floodlights cutting through the darkness, illuminating the seafloor.

Forty-three of us stand in rows. Paulo is beside me, and I can hear his breathing—fast, shallow. He's nervous. He's been talking about the petition for two weeks.

Rafael stands in front of the viewport in a suit that probably costs more than my debt. He speaks in English, and Jin translates into Mandarin. I understand maybe half the English—enough to know that Jin's translation is smoother, more technical.

"The Consortium appreciates your dedication to operational excellence," he says. His voice is warm, collegial. "The Quota Contract represents a sophisticated risk-sharing mechanism. We provide the capital infrastructure—these stations, the submersibles, the processing equipment. You provide the labor. When extraction succeeds, we all benefit proportionally. When it fails, we all bear the cost. That's fair symmetry."

Jin translates. *检查设备日志.* Check equipment logs. *配额合规.* Quota compliance.

I watch Rafael's face while he talks. He believes what he's saying. His eyes don't move, don't calculate.

"This quarter's extraction rates have been excellent," he continues. "We're on track for an eight-point-two percent return, which means debt reduction for those of you who maintained quota compliance."

I look at the viewport behind him. In the floodlights, I can see a cloud of sediment where someone's manipulator is harvesting. The nodules look like dark potatoes scattered across pale mud. The current is stronger than usual. The manipulator keeps missing, the claws closing on empty water.

Rafael doesn't seem to notice. He's looking at a tablet, reviewing numbers that must show something different from what I'm seeing through the viewport.

"Any questions?" Jin asks in Mandarin.

Paulo's breathing gets faster. I can feel him gathering himself, preparing to speak. Then his breathing slows. Stops. Starts again, normal rhythm.

No one speaks.

We file back down to the lower decks. The ceilings drop to two meters, then one-point-eight. By the time I reach pod C-14, I have to duck.

That night I count my breaths during the manipulator reboot, and it takes sixteen. Not twelve. Sixteen.

I count again during the next reboot. Sixteen.

The manipulator is getting slower, or the current is getting stronger, or something in the system is degrading. I make a note on my tablet: "Reboot time increased 33%." I don't know why I'm recording this. Jin files equipment reports every day. She must know.

But I keep counting. Sixteen breaths. The darkness lasts longer now.

---

Cristina arrives on a black-market submersible three weeks later. I see her through the viewport in the airlock—an actual viewport, not just a camera feed. She's negotiating with the pilot, and I can hear them through the intercom.

She speaks English. Not Jin's technical Mandarin-inflected English, but smooth, educated English with a São Paulo accent underneath. "I'm sourcing components for a private contract. The Consortium's procurement channels are backed up, and my client needs faster turnaround."

The pilot nods. "Cost?"

"Competitive with your regular rates. Plus discretion premium."

They shake hands. The pilot doesn't ask for verification, doesn't check credentials. Cristina's performing something I don't have a name for—some signal that makes her trustworthy without proof. She stands a certain way. Her suit is worn but clean. Her voice has a rhythm that suggests she's done this before, that she belongs in this transaction.

Later, in the lower deck, Tomás tells me Cristina used to be an Engineer. Station 11. She reported safety violations and the Consortium buried the report, so she fled to the abandoned stations where the Salvagers live. Now she trades with black-market pilots by pretending to be what she used to be.

"How does that work?" I ask in Portuguese.

"The Surface Trust," he says, using the English words because there's no Portuguese equivalent. "Reputation, not contracts. If you look like you belong, they don't check."

"But she's a fugitive."

"They don't know that. And checking would cost money."

I think about Rafael's warm voice, his talk of fair symmetry. I think about Jin's compliance reports. I think about Cristina's smooth English and the pilot's handshake.

I count my breaths. Sixteen, still sixteen. I've started counting outside the reboots now. During meals. During the climb to my bunk. Sixteen breaths per minute. My body has adjusted to something.

---

The collective action starts in whispers. Paulo—forty-two years old, eighteen months in, debt at 156,000—starts talking about a petition. In Portuguese, late at night, when the Engineers are asleep.

"The quotas assume ideal conditions," he says. "Perfect nodule density, functioning equipment, calm currents. But the density's down forty percent this month. The equipment fails twice per shift. The current's been surging for three weeks. The quota should adjust."

There are seven of us in the circle: Paulo, Tomás, me, and four others. We meet in the equipment bay where the camera coverage has blind spots.

"They'll never agree," Tomás says.

"They have to. We have the data. Jin files it every day. They know the conditions aren't ideal."

Paulo has a notebook—actual paper, which must have cost him. He's been recording the equipment failures, the density readings, the current measurements. Three months of data.

"Look," he says, showing us the numbers. "August: density at seventy-two percent of projection. September: sixty-eight percent. October: fifty-four percent. This month: forty-three percent. The nodules are depleting, but the quotas haven't changed."

"What do you want to do?" I ask.

"Petition. Formal request for quota adjustment. We cite the data, request a twenty percent reduction for next quarter. They're rational actors. They'll see the numbers don't work."

I look at the notebook. The numbers are careful, precise. Paulo has been counting something too.

"I'll sign," I say.

We draft the petition over five days. Paulo writes in Portuguese, and Tomás translates to English. We revise the English, trying to match the technical register of Jin's reports. We cite specific equipment log entries, density measurements, current data. We request a twenty percent quota reduction for the next quarter, adjusting for observed conditions.

Paulo practices reading it aloud. His English is rough, but he wants to deliver it in person.

The next morning, Paulo submits the petition to Jin. Seven of us wait in the equipment bay, listening through the intercom.

Paulo's voice is steady. "We're requesting a quota adjustment based on observed conditions. The data is in the petition. We're not refusing work—we're asking for the quota to reflect reality."

Jin's voice is neutral. "I'll file this with the compliance office."

"When will we hear back?"

"The compliance office reviews all submissions. You'll be notified of their decision."

"How long does that take?"

"It varies."

Paulo returns to the equipment bay. "She filed it," he says. "Now we wait."

We wait three days. Then a week. Then two weeks.

The woman beneath my bunk asks me about it. She's stopped crying now. Five weeks in. She wants to know if the petition will help.

"I don't know," I say.

"But Paulo has the data. He showed me. The density is down forty percent."

"I know."

"So they'll adjust the quota?"

I count my breaths. Sixteen. "Maybe," I say.

Three weeks after Paulo submits the petition, three Miners disappear. They took an emergency submersible during a shift change. They were trying to reach the Salvager stations.

One of them was named Yuki. She worked the pod next to mine. The day before she disappeared, her manipulator failed during a reboot, and she lost two hours of work. She had to stay an extra shift to make quota. When she climbed out of the pod, her hands were shaking.

"Debt?" I asked.

"One forty-three."

"Mine's one thirty-two."

She looked at me. Her eyes were red, but she wasn't crying. "How long have you been here?"

"Eleven months."

"I've been here thirteen. The debt was eighty thousand when I started."

She didn't say anything else. The next day, she was gone.

Jin files it as a contract violation. Their families will receive the debt bills.

We never hear back about the petition.

I count my breaths during the next manipulator reboot. Eighteen now. Definitely eighteen. The darkness lasts longer. The system takes more time to flush and restart.

I make a note on my tablet: "Reboot time: 18 breaths. 50% increase from baseline." I don't know why I'm still recording this. But I keep counting.

---

I hear about Dao from Cristina. She's trading with the black-market pilot again, and I'm working the airlock. She switches to Portuguese when she sees me.

"You knew Dao?" she asks.

"No."

"He was a Salvager. Proposed coordinating with the Miners, organizing together to demand quota reforms. Mateo called a council meeting. The code forbids it—Miners are still under contract, still part of the system, compromised. The council voted to exile him."

"Where did he go?"

"Station 9. Abandoned. His oxygen recycler failed after nine days."

The submersible departs. I watch it disappear into the black water, its lights fading.

I count my breaths. I've stopped counting only during manipulator reboots. I count all the time now. Eighteen breaths per minute, every minute, awake or asleep.

The woman beneath my bunk asks me about the debt arithmetic. She's been here seven weeks now. She wants to understand how the numbers work.

"Show me," she says, handing me her tablet.

I pull up her account. Debt: 43,680 yuan. She started at 28,000.

"You made quota last week," I say. "Four days out of five."

"So the debt should decrease."

"Look at the expenses." I scroll through the itemized list. "Equipment rental: 340 per shift. Habitat fees: 180 per day. Food: 95 per day. Medical: you went to the clinic twice—that's 440 total. And here: penalty fee for the day you missed quota. 580 yuan."

She does the math in her head. I can see her eyes moving. "But I made quota four days. That should cover it."

"Making quota means you don't get penalized. It doesn't mean you earn more than the expenses."

"So how do I pay it off?"

"You have to exceed quota. Consistently. For months."

"What's the over-quota rate?"

"Twenty percent of the nodule value. But you have to exceed quota by more than the daily expenses. That's 615 yuan per day, minimum. You'd need to harvest about thirty extra nodules per shift."

"Can you do that?"

"I've done it twice. Both times the current was calm and the density was above projection. That hasn't happened in six weeks."

She stares at her tablet. "So the debt just grows."

"Unless conditions improve."

"Will they—" I open my mouth to answer and lose count. I was at eleven. Or twelve. I have to start over.

One. Two. Three.

"I don't know," I say.

She's quiet for a moment. Then: "Thank you for showing me."

That night, I lie in my bunk and try to count my breaths, but the rhythm won't hold. I keep starting over. One. Two. Three. Losing it at seven, at nine. Starting again.

Eighteen per minute. That's baseline now. But I can't find eighteen. I can't hold the count.

I force myself to breathe slower. Focus on the inhale, the exhale.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

The woman beneath me is crying again. Not like before. Quieter.

Six. Seven. Eight.

I taught her the arithmetic. I showed her the itemized list, the compounding mechanism, the gap between making quota and paying down debt.

Nine. Ten.

I lose it again.

Eventually I sleep. I don't know how many breaths it takes.

---

Jin calls me to her office on the upper deck. The ceiling is three meters high. There's a viewport showing the black water and the floodlights. She sits behind a desk with a tablet.

*坐*, she says in Mandarin. Sit.

I sit.

"Your contract expires in four months," she says. "You have three options. First: pay off your debt and leave. Current balance is one hundred thirty-eight thousand four hundred yuan. Second: transfer to a different station. Your debt transfers with you. Third: sign a contract extension. Same terms, six-month rotation."

She slides the tablet across the desk. The contract is in English. I can read maybe a third of it. The quota numbers are clear: same as my current contract. The expense structure is the same. Equipment rental, habitat fees, food costs, medical expenses, penalties for shortfalls.

"What happens if I don't choose?" I ask in Mandarin.

"The debt goes to collection. Your family receives the bill."

My family is in the Mekong Delta. What's left of it. The water took most of it ten years ago. My mother is in a relocation camp in Hanoi. The debt would destroy her. She's sixty-three. She can't work.

"If I extend," I say, "does the debt decrease?"

"If you maintain quota compliance."

"And if the nodule density stays at forty percent below projection?"

Jin's face doesn't change. "The quotas are based on geological surveys and market analysis. They're objective. If you're failing to meet them, that's a performance issue."

I look at the tablet. The contract specifies the same quota as before. The same equipment rental fees, habitat fees, food costs. The same penalty structure for shortfalls. The same compounding mechanism.

I think about Paulo's petition, filed three months ago. I think about Yuki's shaking hands the day before she ran. I think about the woman beneath my bunk, crying quietly because she's done the math.

I count my breaths.

Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one.

My breathing is getting faster. I try to slow it down. Focus on the exhale. Make it longer.

Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two.

My body won't obey. The breathing stays fast, stays wrong. I can count it but I can't control it.

Twenty-three.

"Can I think about it?" I ask. My voice sounds normal. My breathing doesn't.

"You have forty-eight hours."

I return to the lower deck. The ceilings drop. The air thickens. The hull creaks.

My breathing is still fast. Twenty-one per minute. Twenty-two. I count it all the way down to pod C-14, and it doesn't slow.

That night, I lie in my bunk and count my breaths. Twenty-one per minute. Not eighteen. Not baseline. Twenty-one.

I think about signing the extension. I think about my mother receiving a bill for one hundred thirty-eight thousand yuan. I think about the nodule density at forty-three percent below projection, the manipulator reboots taking eighteen breaths instead of twelve, the debt growing even when I make quota.

I think about Paulo, who believed documentation mattered. I think about Yuki, whose hands were shaking. I think about Dao, who died in Station 9 after the Salvagers exiled him for proposing coordination.

My breathing won't slow down. Twenty-one per minute. My body knows something my mind is still trying to count around.

The woman beneath me is quiet. She's been here eight weeks now. She's stopped crying. She's done the math.

The next morning, I go back to Jin's office. My breathing is still fast. Twenty-one per minute.

"I'll sign," I say.

She slides the tablet across the desk. I sign it. Not because I believe I'll pay off the debt. Not because I think the quotas are achievable. I sign it because the alternative is my mother receiving a bill for one hundred thirty-eight thousand yuan, and because I am four thousand two hundred meters underwater in a habitat owned by the company I owe money to, operating equipment I rent from them, eating food I buy from them, breathing air that cycles through their recyclers, and there is nowhere else to go.

Jin files the extension. "Return to your shift," she says in Mandarin.

I return to the lower deck. The ceilings drop. The air thickens. The hull creaks.

I climb into my control pod and start my shift. The manipulator claws open and close. The density scanner shows forty-one percent below projection. The current is strong today.

The manipulator reboots during the third hour. The lights go dark. I count my breaths in the absolute black.

Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. Twenty-five. Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. Twenty-eight.

The reboot is taking longer. The system is degrading further.

I make a note on my tablet: "Reboot time: 28 breaths. 133% increase from baseline."

The lights flicker back. The manipulator resumes. I guide the claws toward the nodule field.

I count my breaths. Twenty-eight during the reboot. Twenty-one during normal operation. My body has adapted to the pressure. Not to survive it—to endure it. The breathing rate that used to mean panic is baseline now. The darkness lasts longer and my lungs have learned to wait.

Above me, someone is whispering in Portuguese. *A pressão está pior hoje.* The pressure is worse today.

Below me, in pod C-14, the woman is silent. She's been here eight weeks. She's stopped crying. She's done the math.

Outside, the hull creaks under four hundred twenty atmospheres of pressure. The floodlights cut through the darkness, illuminating nodules scattered across pale mud. The manipulators harvest them, one by one, twelve-hour shifts extending to fourteen, to sixteen. The debt grows. The quotas remain unchanged. The system continues.

Rafael is in São Paulo, reviewing quarterly reports. The extraction rates are excellent. Eight-point-two percent return.

Jin is filing compliance reports. All metrics met. Station integrity maintained.

Cristina is trading with the black-market pilot, performing the Surface Trust signals that grant her access to systems that should exclude her.

Mateo is enforcing the code, exiling anyone who threatens Salvager survival through coordination with the compromised.

And I am in my control pod, counting my breaths—twenty-eight during reboots, twenty-one during operation—documenting the system's degradation in notes that no one will read, four thousand two hundred meters underwater, with a contract that expires in six months and a debt that will take years to pay off if I make quota every single day under ideal conditions that never exist.

*O contrato que respira.*

The contract breathes.

And so do I.
