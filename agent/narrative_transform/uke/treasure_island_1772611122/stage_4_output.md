# The Breathing Contract

I count twelve breaths while the manipulator reboots. Always twelve. The floodlights go dark, and for those twelve breaths I'm suspended in absolute black at the bottom of the Pacific, four thousand two hundred meters down, and the only sound is the hull creaking and my own breathing and the distant hum of the recyclers processing the same air over and over.

The lights flicker back. The manipulator's claws open and close, testing. I guide them toward the nodule field, watching the density scanner. Forty-three percent below projected. Again.

My shift was supposed to end two hours ago, but the reboot cost me fourteen nodules. I need to make up the difference or Jin will file another penalty notice. I've had four this month.

*A pressão está pior hoje*, Tomás whispers when I finally climb out of the control pod. The pressure is worse today. He's right. I can feel it in my chest, in the recycled air that tastes like metal and sweat, in the way the walls seem closer than yesterday.

"Debt?" he asks in Portuguese, quiet enough that the Engineers can't hear.

"One twenty-seven."

He winces. His is ninety-eight. He's been here four months less than me.

I climb to my bunk in pod C-14, fourth tier, and lie down in the space between the bodies above and below me. The woman beneath me is crying softly. She's new, only three weeks in. She still thinks she'll make quota.

I check my tablet. Debt: 127,240 yuan. Yesterday it was 126,890. I made quota yesterday. The debt still grew.

Equipment rental, habitat fees, food costs, medical expenses, penalty fees for the day before when the current surged and scattered the nodules. The contract itemizes everything. The numbers are precise, rational, objective. The debt grows.

I count my breaths until I fall asleep. Twelve, always twelve.

---

Rafael arrives on the quarterly inspection submersible, and we're all called to the common area on the upper deck. I've never been up here before. The ceilings are three meters high. There's a viewport—an actual window looking out into the black water. I can see our floodlights cutting through the darkness, illuminating the seafloor.

Rafael stands in front of the viewport in a suit that probably costs more than my debt. He speaks in English, and Jin translates into Mandarin.

"The Consortium appreciates your dedication to operational excellence," he says. His voice is warm, collegial. "The Quota Contract represents a sophisticated risk-sharing mechanism. We provide the capital infrastructure—these stations, the submersibles, the processing equipment. You provide the labor. When extraction succeeds, we all benefit proportionally. When it fails, we all bear the cost. That's fair symmetry."

I understand maybe half the English. Jin's Mandarin translation is crisp, technical. *检查设备日志.* Check equipment logs. *配额合规.* Quota compliance.

Rafael continues. "This quarter's extraction rates have been excellent. We're on track for an eight-point-two percent return, which means debt reduction for those of you who maintained quota compliance. The system works because it's designed to work. Risk and reward, properly aligned."

I look at the viewport behind him. In the floodlights, I can see a cloud of sediment where someone's manipulator is harvesting. The nodules look like dark potatoes scattered across pale mud. The current is stronger than usual. The manipulator keeps missing.

Rafael doesn't seem to notice. He's looking at a tablet, reviewing numbers.

"Any questions?" Jin asks in Mandarin.

No one speaks. We know better.

We file back down to the lower decks. The ceilings drop to two meters, then one-point-eight. By the time I reach pod C-14, I have to duck. The woman beneath me is still crying.

That night I count sixteen breaths during the manipulator reboot. Not twelve. Sixteen.

The manipulator must be getting slower. That's all.

---

Cristina arrives on a black-market submersible three weeks later. I see her through the viewport in the airlock—an actual viewport, not just a camera feed. She's negotiating with the pilot, and I can hear them through the intercom.

She speaks English. Not Jin's technical Mandarin-inflected English, but smooth, educated English with a São Paulo accent. "I'm sourcing components for a private contract. The Consortium's procurement channels are backed up, and my client needs faster turnaround."

The pilot nods. "Cost?"

"Competitive with your regular rates. Plus discretion premium."

They shake hands. The pilot doesn't ask for verification, doesn't check credentials. Cristina's performing something I don't have a name for—some signal that makes her trustworthy without proof.

Later, in the lower deck, Tomás tells me Cristina used to be an Engineer. Station 11. She reported safety violations and the Consortium buried the report, so she fled to the abandoned stations where the Salvagers live. Now she trades with black-market pilots by pretending to be what she used to be.

"How does that work?" I ask in Portuguese.

"The Surface Trust," he says, using the English words because there's no Portuguese equivalent. "It's how they do business up there. Reputation, not contracts. If you look like you belong, they don't check."

"But she's a fugitive."

"They don't know that. And checking would cost money. Cheaper to trust the signals."

I think about Rafael's warm voice, his talk of fair symmetry. I think about Jin's compliance reports. I think about Cristina's smooth English and the pilot's handshake.

I count my breaths. Sixteen, still sixteen. The manipulator is definitely slower.

---

The collective action starts in whispers. A Miner named Paulo—forty-two years old, eighteen months in, debt at 156,000—starts talking about a petition. In Portuguese, late at night, when the Engineers are asleep.

"The quotas assume ideal conditions," he says. "Perfect nodule density, functioning equipment, calm currents. But the density's down forty percent this month. The equipment fails twice per shift. The current's been surging for three weeks. The quota should adjust."

"They'll never agree," Tomás says.

"They have to. We have the data. Jin files it every day. They know the conditions aren't ideal."

We draft the petition in broken English. It takes us five days. We cite the density reports, the equipment logs, the current measurements. We request a twenty percent quota reduction for the next quarter, adjusting for actual conditions.

Paulo submits it to Jin.

She files it as a compliance issue. Not a negotiation. A compliance issue. It goes into the system with the equipment malfunction reports and the medical expense claims.

We never hear back.

Two weeks later, three Miners disappear. They took an emergency submersible during a shift change. They were trying to reach the Salvager stations.

Jin files it as a contract violation. Their families will receive the debt bills.

I count my breaths during the next manipulator reboot. Eighteen now. Definitely eighteen.

The manipulator is getting slower, or the current is getting stronger, or the nodules are getting deeper in the mud. Something is changing. The debt is 132,670 yuan. I made quota last week. The debt grew anyway.

*A pressão*, Tomás says. The pressure.

I know what he means. It's not just the water pressure at four thousand two hundred meters, though that's real enough—four hundred twenty atmospheres pressing against the hull, pressing against my lungs, pressing against my thoughts. It's the other pressure. The quota pressure. The debt pressure. The pressure of time running out while the debt grows faster than I can pay it.

*O contrato que respira*, the woman beneath me whispers. The breathing contract. It expands when you try to escape.

---

I hear about Dao from Cristina. She's trading with the black-market pilot again, and I'm working the airlock. She switches to Portuguese when she sees me.

"You knew Dao?" she asks.

"No."

"He was a Salvager. Proposed coordinating with the Miners, organizing together to demand quota reforms. Mateo called a council meeting. The code forbids it—Miners are still under contract, still part of the system, compromised. The council voted to exile him."

"Where did he go?"

"Station 9. Abandoned. His oxygen recycler failed after nine days. The Consortium found his body three weeks later."

She says it matter-of-factly, without emotion. She's been a Salvager for four years. She's seen this before.

"Why does the code forbid it?" I ask. "We're not compromised. We'd help."

"Because you're still under contract. Still in the system. The code is about survival through exit, not survival through reform. Organizing with Miners means organizing within the system. That's collaboration."

"But we're trapped. We can't exit."

"Then you're trapped." She loads her components into the submersible. "The code protects Salvagers. That's all it's designed to do."

The submersible departs. I watch it disappear into the black water, its lights fading.

I count my breaths. I've stopped counting during manipulator reboots. I count all the time now. Eighteen breaths per minute, every minute, awake or asleep. My body has adjusted to the pressure. The breathing rate that used to signal stress is now baseline.

I don't calculate the debt anymore. The numbers don't matter. The contract breathes, and I breathe with it.

---

Jin calls me to her office on the upper deck. The ceiling is three meters high. There's a viewport showing the black water and the floodlights. She sits behind a desk with a tablet.

*坐*, she says in Mandarin. Sit.

I sit.

"Your contract expires in four months," she says. "You have three options. First: pay off your debt and leave. Current balance is one hundred thirty-eight thousand four hundred yuan. Second: transfer to a different station. Your debt transfers with you. Third: sign a contract extension. Same terms, six-month rotation."

She slides the tablet across the desk. The contract is in English. I can read maybe a third of it.

"What happens if I don't choose?" I ask in Mandarin.

"The debt goes to collection. Your family receives the bill."

My family is in the Mekong Delta. What's left of it. The water took most of it ten years ago. My mother is in a relocation camp in Hanoi. The debt would destroy her.

"If I extend," I say, "does the debt decrease?"

"If you maintain quota compliance."

"And if the nodule density stays at forty percent below projection?"

Jin's face doesn't change. "The quotas are based on geological surveys and market analysis. They're objective. If you're failing to meet them, that's a performance issue."

I look at the tablet. The contract specifies the same quota as before. The same equipment rental fees, habitat fees, food costs. The same penalty structure for shortfalls. The same compounding mechanism.

*O contrato que respira.*

I sign it. Not because I believe I'll pay off the debt. Not because I think the quotas are achievable. I sign it because the alternative is my mother receiving a bill for one hundred thirty-eight thousand yuan, and because I am four thousand two hundred meters underwater in a habitat owned by the company I owe money to, operating equipment I rent from them, eating food I buy from them, and there is nowhere else to go.

Jin files the extension. "Return to your shift," she says in Mandarin.

I return to the lower deck. The ceilings drop. The air thickens. The hull creaks.

I climb into my bunk in pod C-14, fourth tier. The woman beneath me has stopped crying. She's been here two months now. She's learned.

I don't count my breaths anymore. The breaths come as they come. Eighteen per minute, steady, constant. My body has adapted to the pressure. Not to survive it—to endure it.

Above me, someone is whispering in Portuguese. *A pressão está pior hoje.* The pressure is worse today.

Below me, silence.

Outside, the hull creaks under four hundred twenty atmospheres of pressure. The floodlights cut through the darkness, illuminating nodules scattered across pale mud. The manipulators harvest them, one by one, twelve-hour shifts extending to fourteen, to sixteen. The debt grows. The quotas remain unchanged. The system continues.

Rafael is in São Paulo, reviewing quarterly reports. The extraction rates are excellent. Eight-point-two percent return. The risk-sharing mechanism is working as designed.

Jin is filing compliance reports. All metrics met. Station integrity maintained.

Cristina is trading with the black-market pilot, performing the Surface Trust signals that grant her access to systems that should exclude her.

Mateo is enforcing the code, exiling anyone who threatens Salvager survival through coordination with the compromised.

And I am in my bunk, fourth tier, listening to the hull creak, breathing eighteen breaths per minute, four thousand two hundred meters underwater, with a contract that expires in six months and a debt that will take years to pay off if I make quota every single day under ideal conditions that never exist.

*O contrato que respira.*

The contract breathes.

And so do I.
