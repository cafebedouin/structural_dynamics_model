# STAGE 2: NATURALIZATION OUTPUT

## SECTION 0: INVARIANT CONTRACT

**The Invariant (Commitment + Falsifier):**
The world contains a real — absolute correctness in the state of a thing — that the system's own instruments cannot read without corrupting it. The act of measuring for certification transforms what it measures. **Falsifier:** If the finished story contains a recoverable "true value" that the system merely measured wrong, the invariant was lost and the naturalization failed.

**The Substrate:**
Raw mineral ore in its unextracted state holds a purity that exists only while unclaimed. The moment an assay certifies its grade for commerce, the certification itself becomes the tradeable thing, and the ore's actual state becomes irrelevant to the transaction. The substrate is *ore-as-it-stands* versus *ore-as-certified* — the gap between them is the world's foundational physics.

**Substrate Inhabitation Sentence:**
You break stone that has been holding metal for longer than any empire, and the moment you write its worth on paper, the paper becomes the thing that moves through the world while the stone stays behind, mute.

**The Missing Floor (Detector B):**
Beneath the certification system there is a founding choice with no neutral ground under it: the selection of the reference standard against which all ore is measured. Every measurement presupposes this standard, yet the standard itself was chosen by those who controlled the first certified scales. **Falsifier:** If the story frames the injustice as a local error a better authority could correct, the floor invariant was lost.

## SECTION 1: CONTEXT

### Setting Description

**Potosí, Alto Perú, 1683 CE.** Thirty-seven years after the great cave-in that killed four hundred miners and collapsed the richest veins in Cerro Rico. The mountain still produces, but the easy silver is gone. What remains requires deeper shafts, longer hours, more bodies.

The Villa Imperial de Potosí sits at 4,090 meters, where the air is thin enough that newcomers from the coast spend their first week dizzy and their first month coughing. The city exists because the mountain exists. Forty thousand souls, maybe more — nobody counts the indios who work the *mita* and die before their year is up. The Spanish count themselves: 800 families of pure blood, 3,000 *mestizos* who keep the machinery running, and the rest who don't matter except as hands.

The mountain is divided into claims, and the claims into *varas* — sections measured by the *vara castellana*, the official rod kept in the Cabildo and checked yearly against the standard rod sent from Lima, which was itself certified against the rod in Seville. Every measurement flows from that rod. Every vara of mountain, every marco of silver, every real of tribute.

The *Casa de Moneda* — the mint — sits in the plaza, a fortress of gray stone where silver becomes coin. But before silver reaches the mint, it must pass through the assayers. The *Gremio de Ensayadores* controls the scales. They certify the grade: how much pure silver in each marco of ore. The miners bring raw ore. The assayers test it, write the certification, take their fee. The certification is what sells, not the ore itself. A sack of ore with no paper is worthless. A paper claiming 85% purity moves through the market whether the ore it describes still exists or not.

The *mita* — the mandatory labor draft — pulls men from the *ayllus* (indigenous communities) in a rotating cycle. One year of service in the mines, then six years home. Except the mountain kills enough that many never return, and the *ayllus* must send replacements mid-cycle to meet their quota. The *mitayos* work the deepest shafts where the air is bad and the rock unstable. They are paid in *jornales* — daily wages calculated in reales — but the wages are set against certified ore values that presuppose the *vara* and the marco and the assayer's fee.

Spanish overseers manage the claims. *Mestizo* foremen manage the *mitayos*. The foremen speak both Quechua and Spanish, keep the work moving, and write reports in the margins of the official logs — small annotations in Quechua that the overseers can't read, tracking which shafts are near collapse, which *mitayos* are coughing blood, which assayers can be trusted.

The air is always cold. The plaza smells of llama dung and smelter smoke. The mountain is visible from every street, a conical mass of red-brown rock with a thousand holes in its sides like a termite mound. At night you can see torchlight moving inside the shafts — the *mitayos* work in shifts, day and night, because the mountain never stops.

### Constraint Naturalizations

**C₁ (Natural Law Baseline) → *La Verdad del Metal* (The Truth of the Metal)**

The ore holds what it holds. Silver content is a fact of geology — how the metal crystallized in the rock over millennia. This is unchangeable. No human action can make low-grade ore high-grade. The mountain's truth exists independent of measurement.

**However:** That truth cannot be known without extraction and testing, which destroys the sample. The assayer burns a piece of ore to separate silver from dross. The test consumes the ore. What remains is an estimate, not the thing itself. The mountain's truth is real but unreachable — you can approach it, but never possess it whole.

**What locals call it:** *La ley del cerro* — "the law of the mountain." Unchangeable, like gravity or weather.

**Example in use:**
A *mitayo* breaks a vein of crystalline ore shot through with dark threads. He holds it up to torchlight. "This one is rich," he says in Quechua. But he knows the richness stays in the mountain until the assayer burns it and writes a number. The number will be close to true, but never exact. The mountain keeps its secrets even as it gives up its metal.

**Plot shift (C₁ is backdrop, not plot):**
No transformation. The mountain's truth does not change. Characters route around it.

---

**C₂ (Constructed Baseline) → *La Vara Imperial* (The Imperial Rod)**

All measurements in Potosí derive from the *vara castellana* — the official measuring rod kept in the Cabildo. The vara defines the marco (unit of weight for silver). The marco defines the real (unit of currency). The entire system rests on this rod.

**Why it has these properties:**
The vara was chosen by Spanish imperial decree in 1497. It could have been longer or shorter. The choice was arbitrary, but once made, it became the foundation of all commerce in the empire. The rod in Potosí is checked yearly against the rod in Lima, which is checked against the rod in Seville. Any deviation is corrected. The system enforces uniformity with high suppression — officials who use non-standard measures are fined or stripped of office.

From the *mitayo* index (powerless, biographical, identity-locked, local): The vara appears as natural law. It has always been this length. The mountain is measured in varas. Wages are calculated in reales derived from marcos derived from varas. The system is invisible — you cannot imagine measuring differently because you have never seen an alternative. **This is the False Mountain.** The vara feels unchangeable, but it is maintained by enforcement and could be revised by those with institutional power.

From the foreman index (analytical, biographical, identity-locked, local): The vara is a convention. The foreman has seen the Cabildo officials check the rod, seen them file the edges to match Lima's standard. He knows it is maintained, not natural. But he also knows the vara enables coordination — without a shared standard, commerce collapses. It is a Rope: constructed, but functional.

**What locals call it:** *La medida justa* — "the just measure." The term hides the arbitrariness.

**Example in use:**
A Spanish overseer measures a new shaft with the vara rod, marking depths on the wall. A *mitayo* watches. To the *mitayo*, the vara is simply how depth is measured — he has never questioned it. To the overseer, the vara is a tool of empire, enforced from Seville to ensure uniform taxation. Both are correct from their positions.

**Plot shift in the new world's language:**
If *mitayos* organized collectively and proposed an alternative standard (a *vara andina* based on local measures), they would reveal the vara's constructed nature. But this transformation is **blocked** by high suppression (0.8) and the False Mountain perception. The vara appears too fundamental to question. Collective organization never forms because the target is invisible.

---

**C₃ (Primary Extraction Mechanism) → *El Ensayo y la Tasa* (The Assay and the Fee)**

Before ore can be sold, it must be certified by the *Gremio de Ensayadores*. The assayer tests a sample, writes the grade (percentage of pure silver), and charges a fee — the *tasa de ensayo*. The certification is what moves through the market. A sack of ore with a paper claiming 80% purity is worth more than identical ore with a paper claiming 75%, regardless of actual content.

**Why it has these properties:**
The assay is necessary (coordination: buyers need to know what they're buying). But the assayers control the scales, set the fees, and write the certifications. The fee is high — 8% of certified value. For a *mitayo* bringing raw ore, this means: dig for a week, bring 100 marcos of ore, receive certification for 85 marcos (if the ore tests at 85% purity), then pay 6.8 marcos as the assay fee. Net: 78.2 marcos of value from 100 marcos of ore and a week of labor.

The *mitayo* experiences this as a Snare (χ = 0.96): trapped in a system that takes almost everything. He cannot sell uncertified ore. He cannot become an assayer (the guild is closed to indios). He cannot leave (the *mita* is mandatory). The system extracts systematically.

The foreman experiences this as a Tangled Rope (χ = 0.64): The assay system is corrupt (the fees are too high, some assayers skim), but it is also functional (standardization prevents chaos). He writes in the margins of the official logs, tracking which assayers are trustworthy, which ones inflate grades for bribes. He accepts the system as "how things work" while quietly documenting its abuses.

The assayer experiences this as a Rope (χ = -0.128): He provides a necessary service (certification enables trade) and is compensated fairly (the fee covers his expertise and equipment). From his index, the system is pure coordination. He does not see the extraction because he is the net beneficiary.

**What locals call it:** *El tributo del papel* — "the tribute of the paper." The paper (certification) is what costs, not the testing itself.

**Example in use:**
A *mitayo* brings ore to the assayer's shop. The assayer burns a sample, watches the silver pool, writes "82% ley" on the certificate. He charges 6.56 marcos as his fee. The *mitayo* takes the paper and sells the ore for 75.44 marcos of value (82% of 100 marcos, minus the fee). He has worked a week for this. The assayer has worked an hour. The *mitayo* feels the extraction but cannot name it — the system is naturalized as "the cost of doing business." The foreman, watching, writes in Quechua in the margin of his log: "Assayer Mendoza, honest scale, high fee. Avoid if possible."

**Plot shift in the new world's language:**

**Trigger:** A *mitayo* (X₁) survives his year, returns for a second *mita* (rare — most die or flee). Over time, he learns the system: which assayers are honest, how to negotiate fees, how to time sales when silver prices are high.

**What changes:** His power position shifts from powerless (π = 1.5) to moderate (π = 1.0). He is still trapped in the *mita*, but he has insider knowledge. His experience of the assay system shifts from Snare (pure extraction trap) to Tangled Rope (corrupt but navigable).

**What it feels like from his position:** The system still takes, but he has learned to minimize the taking. He no longer fights it — he works within it. This is normalization. The extraction continues, but he stops seeing it as resistible.

**What it feels like from the foreman's position:** He sees the *mitayo* become competent, learn the tricks. He adds a note in the margin: "This one understands the scales now. He will survive."

**What it feels like from the assayer's position:** No change. The system continues to function. The assayer does not notice individual *mitayos* — they are interchangeable.

### Character Roles as Positions

**X₁ → Túpac** (mitayo, first year)
- **Occupation:** Mandatory laborer in the silver mines under the *mita* system
- **Material circumstances:** Lives in a *ranchería* (workers' barracks) outside the villa. Sleeps on stone floor with forty others. Eats *chuño* (freeze-dried potatoes) and *charqui* (dried llama meat) when available. Paid 3 reales per day, but half goes to the *mita* overseer as "lodging fee." Owns: one wool poncho, one *ch'ullu* (knit cap), one pair of *ojotas* (sandals). No Spanish. Speaks Quechua and Aymara.
- **Position in system:** Powerless, biographical time horizon (cannot see beyond his one-year service), identity-locked (cannot leave the *mita*), local scope (has never been outside the *ayllu* and Potosí).

**X₂ → Túpac** (same person, second *mita*, three years later)
- **Material circumstances:** Still in the *ranchería*, but now has a corner spot (small status marker). Has learned enough Spanish to negotiate with overseers. Knows which assayers are honest, which market days have better prices. Still poor, but survives where others die.
- **Position in system:** Moderate power (insider knowledge), biographical time horizon, identity-locked, local scope.

**X₃ → Martín Quispe** (mestizo foreman)
- **Occupation:** Foreman managing *mitayo* crews in the mines
- **Material circumstances:** Rents a small house in the *mestizo* quarter. Married, two children. Earns 12 reales per day. Owns: wooden furniture, iron tools, three sets of clothes, a mule. Literate in Spanish and Quechua. Keeps personal logs.
- **Position in system:** Analytical power (sees the system's constructed nature), biographical time horizon, identity-locked (cannot rise to Spanish overseer, cannot return to *ayllu*), local scope.

**X₄ → Los Mineros Libres** (independent miners, collective agent)
- **Occupation:** Freelance miners working marginal claims outside the *mita* system
- **Material circumstances:** Live in the *rancherías* or in caves on the mountain. Poorer than *mitayos* (no guaranteed wage), but not bound to the *mita*. Sell ore directly to assayers, pay same fees as *mitayos*.
- **Position in system:** Powerless (no institutional backing), immediate time horizon (day-to-day survival), constrained exit (can leave Potosí but have nowhere else to go), local scope.

**I₁ → Don Francisco de Ulloa** (master assayer, guild member)
- **Occupation:** Certified assayer, member of the *Gremio de Ensayadores*
- **Material circumstances:** Owns a house on the plaza. Employs two apprentices. Earns 200 reales per month from assay fees. Wears black wool, silver buttons, leather boots. Has a private chapel.
- **Position in system:** Institutional power (controls certification), biographical time horizon, identity-locked (cannot leave the guild without losing certification), local scope (operates only in Potosí, bound by Lima's regulations).

### Linguistic Strategy: Code-Switching (Quechua/Spanish) + Historical Spanish Register

**Languages:**
- **Quechua:** Language of the *mitayos* and *ayllu* communities. Used for intimacy, private speech, resistance. The foreman uses Quechua in his margin notes because the Spanish overseers cannot read it.
- **Spanish:** Language of the empire, the market, the law. Used for official transactions, written records, interactions with institutional power.

**Code-switching logic:**
- **Quechua for:**
  - Conversations between *mitayos*
  - Foreman's margin notes (covert documentation)
  - Moments of intimacy or solidarity
  - Naming things the Spanish system does not recognize (e.g., *ayllu* obligations, *apus* — mountain spirits)

- **Spanish for:**
  - Interactions with overseers, assayers, officials
  - Market transactions
  - Written certifications, logs, official records
  - Performing compliance with the imperial system

**The switch signals:** Who you are talking to, whether you are performing for the system or speaking your own truth.

**Historical Spanish register (1680s):**
- **Formal address:** *Vuestra Merced* (Your Grace) for officials, *Señor* for social superiors
- **Grammatical features:**
  - Vosotros forms still in use (e.g., *habéis*, *sois*)
  - Future subjunctive (e.g., *si fuere necesario* — "if it should be necessary")
  - Archaic vocabulary: *ensayador* (assayer), *marco* (unit of weight), *real* (currency), *vara* (rod), *ley* (grade/purity)

**Examples:**
- **Quechua (mitayo to mitayo):** *"Kay quri q'illay mana allinchu. Ensayadorqa llullakun."* ("This ore is not good. The assayer lies.")
- **Spanish (mitayo to assayer, performing compliance):** *"Señor ensayador, traigo cien marcos de metal. ¿Cuál es la ley?"* ("Master assayer, I bring one hundred marcos of ore. What is the grade?")
- **Foreman's margin note (Quechua, covert):** *"Mendoza, chiqan balanza, hatun tasa. Ama riy."* ("Mendoza, honest scale, high fee. Do not go.")

### Inhabitation Sentences

**Túpac (X₁, first *mita*):**
You are a body the mountain consumes, and the only language that is yours is the one you speak to the man who sleeps beside you on the stone floor, and the system measures the metal you break but cannot measure what breaking it costs you.

**Túpac (X₂, second *mita*, post-normalization):**
You have learned which assayer will not cheat you, which market day brings better prices, and you no longer ask why the paper costs more than the metal, because the question leads nowhere and you have a week to live through.

**Martín Quispe (X₃, foreman):**
You write in the margins in a language your superiors cannot read, tracking which shafts will collapse and which men will not return, and you do this because the official log has no space for what you know, and you are the one who will write the report when the mountain kills them.

**Los Mineros Libres (X₄, independent miners):**
You are free in the way a man is free when he has no master and no food, and the assayer charges you the same fee he charges the *mitayos*, and you sell your ore for the same price, and the only difference is that you chose this trap instead of being born into it.

**Don Francisco de Ulloa (I₁, assayer):**
You certify the grade, write the number, take your fee, and you do this because commerce requires standards and standards require certification, and the fact that the *mitayos* cannot afford your fee is not your concern — you are not the one who set the *mita*, you only measure what the mountain gives.

**Substrate (the ore itself, from Túpac's position):**
You press your hand to stone that has been holding silver since before the Inca, and the moment you break it and bring it to the assayer, the stone becomes a number on paper, and the paper is what moves through the world while the stone stays behind in the dark, and the number is never quite true because the testing burns the sample and the truth stays in the mountain, unmeasured.

## SECTION 2: OMEGA LOG

### RESOLVED

**Ω_E01: What was the actual silver content measurement process in 1680s Potosí?**
- **Resolution:** Assayers used *copellation* — burning ore samples in a clay crucible to separate silver from dross. The process consumed the sample. Result was an estimate, not a complete measurement of the original ore.
- **Impact:** Confirms the invariant — the testing process destroys what it measures. The ore's "true" silver content cannot be fully known without consuming the ore itself.

**Ω_E02: What was the structure of the *mita* labor system?**
- **Resolution:** Mandatory draft pulling 1/7 of adult males from designated *ayllus* for one year of mine labor. Rotational but deadly — many did not return. Wages paid but set against certified ore values, creating extraction loop.
- **Impact:** Naturalizes the "identity-locked" index for X₁ (cannot leave) and the "powerless" position (no negotiating power).

**Ω_E03: Did assayer guilds in colonial Spanish America actually control certification monopolies?**
- **Resolution:** Yes. The *Gremio de Ensayadores* in Potosí held royal charter. Only guild members could certify ore. Fees were regulated by the Cabildo but effectively set by the guild.
- **Impact:** Confirms institutional control (I₁ position) and the asymmetric extraction structure (guild members net beneficiaries).

**Ω_C01: Should the *vara castellana* be treated as the "missing floor" invariant or as C₂?**
- **Resolution:** The *vara* is C₂ (the constructed baseline). The "missing floor" is the *choice to use the vara as the universal standard* — the founding decision that there must be one imperial measure. The vara could have been different; the fact that there is a vara at all is the ungrounded choice.
- **Impact:** Clarifies that C₂ naturalizes the *vara itself*, while the missing-floor invariant is the prior choice to impose uniformity. Both are present, but they operate at different levels.

**Ω_C02: How to handle the fact that the historical *mita* system did not use percentage-based assay fees?**
- **Resolution:** The historical fee structure was complex (fixed fees + percentage of certified value + bribes). For narrative purposes, simplify to a single percentage-based fee (8% of certified value) that captures the extraction mechanism without requiring the reader to track multiple fee types. The structural extraction is historically accurate even if the specific arithmetic is streamlined.
- **Impact:** Allows the Scored-Snare exception (inherent_instrument: yes) to apply — the assay fee is denominated in marcos, but the *felt experience* is "the paper costs almost as much as the metal," not "the fee is 6.8 marcos."

**Ω_P01: Should Túpac's normalization (TR₁) be shown on-page or referenced as backstory?**
- **Resolution:** Reference as backstory. The narrative will show Túpac in his second *mita* (post-normalization), with flashbacks or dialogue revealing the shift. This avoids a slow, grinding normalization arc and allows the story to start with the more complex Tangled Rope position.
- **Impact:** Stage 4 will receive Túpac as X₂ (moderate, normalized) with his X₁ (powerless, first *mita*) past as context.

**Ω_P02: Should the foreman's margin notes be translated into English or left in Quechua with context?**
- **Resolution:** Write the notes in Quechua (or a plausible Quechua-inflected Spanish), then provide context through the foreman's actions or dialogue. Do not translate inline. The opacity to Spanish-speaking characters is part of the point.
- **Impact:** Maintains code-switching as a structural feature, not just flavor. The reader experiences the same information asymmetry the Spanish overseers experience.

**Ω_E04: What percentage of *mitayos* survived their year of service?**
- **Question:** Historical mortality rates in the Potosí mines during the 1680s.
- **Recommendation:** Research suggests 20-40% mortality for *mitayos* during their service year, but sources vary. Use 25% as a baseline (1 in 4 do not return). This is high enough to naturalize the "biographical time horizon" (characters plan only for survival, not future) without being so extreme that the system collapses.
- **Awaiting decision:** Confirm this rate with user or proceed with 25%.

**Ω_C03: How to handle the fact that the *vara* was not actually arbitrary — it had historical precedent in medieval Spain?**
- **Question:** The *vara castellana* was standardized in 1497, but it derived from earlier regional measures. Is it still "arbitrary" if it has historical continuity?
- **Recommendation:** Yes, for narrative purposes. The choice to impose the Castilian vara (rather than Aragonese, Portuguese, or indigenous Andean measures) was an imperial decision. The continuity within Spain does not make it non-arbitrary in the context of colonial Peru. The "missing floor" invariant holds: there is no neutral ground beneath the choice of this vara over other possible standards.
- **Awaiting decision:** Confirm this framing or revise.
