# The Margins

I write this in the hour before dawn, when the overseers are still in their beds and the *mitayos* are already descending into the mountain. I write in Quechua, in the margins of the official log, where Spanish eyes cannot follow. The ruled lines hold what the Cabildo requires: shaft depths, ore weights, daily wages. The margins hold what matters.

Túpac Mamani returned for a second *mita* after his first. Most do not return. Most die in the shafts, or flee to the *yungas*, or go home and never come back even when the *ayllu* sends them. Túpac came back. I asked him why. He said, "I know the mountain now."

This is what I recorded.

---

The air was thinner than I remembered. I stood in the plaza with my bundle — poncho, *ch'ullu*, spare *ojotas* — and looked up at Cerro Rico. The mountain was the same. Red-brown stone, holes in its sides like mouths, smoke from the smelters hanging in the cold. The same, but I was not.

Before, I had come here afraid. The *kuraka* had called my name at the *ayllu* meeting. *Mita* service: the year in Potosí, then the years home. That was the law. I had walked the days from the valley with others from the *ayllu*. Half of us did not walk back.

Now I walked alone. The *ranchería* smelled of stone dust and unwashed wool. Men sleeping on the floor, bodies pressed together for warmth. I found a space near the wall, not the corner — corners went to men who had been here longer — and spread my poncho. The man next to me was coughing. New arrival. First week. His lungs would stop fighting the altitude in a month, or he would die. I did not ask his name.

The foreman came at dawn. Martín Quispe, *mestizo*, leather hat, iron tools on his belt. He spoke Quechua with a Spanish rhythm. "You," he said, looking at me. "You were here before."

"Yes, señor."

"You survived."

"Yes, señor."

"Then you know which shaft."

I knew. The deep shaft, where the air goes bad and the rock creaks. Where the silver is.

---

The first time I brought ore to an assayer, I did not understand the paper.

It was early in the first *mita*. I had broken stone for days, filled a sack with ore that made my shoulders ache. Rich ore, I guessed. Rich ore, shot through with dark veins that caught the torchlight. I carried it down the mountain at dusk, the weight pressing into my spine.

The assayer's shop was on the street behind the plaza. Stone building, iron door, smell of burning metal. I had been told to go to Mendoza. "He has an honest scale," the other *mitayos* said. "He does not skim."

Don Francisco de Ulloa y Mendoza. That was his full name, written on the sign above the door in letters I could not read. Inside, the shop was hot. A furnace in the corner, a workbench, scales hanging from chains. Mendoza was older than my father, gray in his beard, black wool coat with silver buttons. He looked at me the way you look at a mule.

"How much?" he said in Spanish.

I told him what I guessed.

He took a piece of ore from my sack, no bigger than my thumb. Placed it in a clay crucible, put the crucible in the furnace. I watched the ore glow, melt, the dross burning away. The smell made my eyes water. When it cooled, he poured the silver into a small mold, weighed it on the scale. The needle swung, settled. He wrote a number on a piece of paper.

He read it aloud as he wrote, the way he always did: the *ley*, the percentage of pure silver. He looked up. "Pure silver in your sack." He wrote more numbers. Took a pen, made marks I could not read. Then he looked at me. "The *tasa de ensayo*. The certification fee."

I did not understand. The ore was what the ore was worth. Why did I owe him anything?

"For the testing," he said, seeing my face. "For the certification. For the paper. Without the paper, you cannot sell."

He took his fee. I left with a piece of paper I could not read and less value than I had carried down the mountain. The paper was lighter than the ore, but when I went to the market, it was the paper the buyer wanted to see. The ore itself, he barely looked at.

That night I lay on the stone floor of the *ranchería* and felt the missing marcos like a hole in my chest. I had worked days. The assayer had worked an hour. Where was the justice in that?

The man next to me — older, third *mita*, hands like tree roots — heard me muttering. "The fee is the fee," he said in Quechua. "Mendoza is honest. His scale does not lie. There are assayers who will write lower *ley* when the ore is richer, then sell the difference to their cousins. Mendoza does not do this. You will learn."

I learned. But learning felt like forgetting.

---

Second *mita*, first week. I carried ore down the mountain on a Tuesday, when the market was quiet and the buyers were hungry. I did not guess the weight anymore. I knew by the way my knees bent, by the ache in my shoulders.

Mendoza's shop. Iron door. Smell of burning. He looked up when I entered, did not recognize me. Why would he? Years, *mitayos* like rain. I was a body that brought stone.

"*Señor ensayador*," I said. "*Traigo metal*."

He took a sample. Into the furnace. I watched the ore burn. I knew what the number would be before he wrote it. Good ore. I had chosen the vein carefully, broken only the richest stone.

He wrote the certification. Took his fee. I paid without speaking. I left with the paper.

At the market, I waited until the sun was low. The buyers paid more when they were tired, when they wanted to close their stalls and go home. I sold for more than the certified value. The buyer did not test the ore. He looked at the paper, looked at Mendoza's seal, paid.

I walked back to the *ranchería* with coin in my pocket. Enough for weeks of *chuño* and *charqui*. Enough to send a little back to the *ayllu*. The hole in my chest was still there, but smaller. I had learned to carry it.

---

*Martín's margin note, that night:*

*Túpac, segundo año. Ya conoce las balanzas. Sobrevivirá.*

Túpac, second year. He knows the scales now. He will survive.

---

The new *mitayo* in the *ranchería* did not understand the paper.

His name was Sami. First week, first *mita*. Face still round with youth, not yet hollowed by the mountain. He came back from Mendoza's shop shaking.

"The assayer is a thief," he said in Quechua, loud enough that the whole room heard. "I brought a week's breaking. He took half."

I was mending my *ojotas* by the wall. I looked up. "Mendoza?"

"Yes. Don Francisco de Ulloa y Mendoza. The honest scale." Sami's voice cracked. "His scale is a lie."

"His scale is true," I said.

"Then why did he take so much?"

I put down the sandal. Looked at him. Saw myself before, angry and uncomprehending. "What was the *ley*?"

He told me the percentage Mendoza had read aloud. I worked through the calculation in my head — the certified value, the fee as a portion of that value. Mendoza had not stolen. The fee was the fee.

"The certification fee is of the certified value," I said. "Not of what you brought. You brought ore. But the ore was only part silver. So the value was less. The fee is a portion of that."

Sami stared at me. "That is theft."

"That is the *tasa*."

"It is the same thing."

"No," I said. I heard my voice, patient, explaining. The voice of the older *mitayo* who had explained it to me before. "Mendoza provides a service. He tests the ore. He writes the certification. Without the paper, you cannot sell. The paper costs."

"The paper costs more than the work."

"The paper *is* the work. The ore is just stone until someone writes its worth."

Sami looked at me like I had struck him. Then he turned away, lay down on his poncho, faced the wall.

I went back to mending my sandals. My hands were steady. I had explained it correctly. The system was corrupt, yes, but it was also functional. Mendoza's scale was honest. His paper was accepted everywhere. If all assayers were like him, the *mita* would be bearable.

I believed this. I had learned to believe it.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Túpac le dijo al joven que trabaje más rápido. No ve que trabajar más rápido solo aumenta la tasa del ensayador. La tasa es un porcentaje. Más metal = más tasa. Ha olvidado las matemáticas que importan.*

Túpac told the young man to work faster. He does not see that working faster only increases the assayer's take. The fee is a percentage. More ore = more fee. He has forgotten the mathematics that matter.

---

Sami died in the fourth month.

Cave-in. The timber supports in the old shaft gave way. *Mitayos* were inside. They pulled some out alive. Sami they found the next day, crushed under rock.

We carried him up in pieces. Wrapped him in his poncho, buried him on the mountain. No priest. The priests charged for burials, and none of us had the coin. We said the old words, the ones from before the Spanish, and left him there.

That night I lay on the stone floor and thought about the paper in Mendoza's shop. Sami's certification, still on file. Good *ley*, it said. Value after the fee. The paper existed. The ore existed, somewhere in the market, sold and resold. But Sami was gone. The paper had outlived him.

I borrowed paper from Martín — he kept scraps for the *mitayos* who wanted to send word home. I sat by the wall with a piece of charcoal and tried to write the words.

*Sami*. I wrote his name. The charcoal made a mark that looked like a mark.

*Ayllu*. I wrote the name of his home.

Then I stopped. What came next? *Wañupun*. He died. But that was not what I needed to say. The *ayllu* would know he died when he did not return. What they needed to know was how. What it cost. What we said on the mountain.

I tried the system's language. The *ley* was good, I wrote. The certification was filed. The ore was sold. These were true things. But they said nothing about Sami crushed under rock.

I crossed them out. Tried different words. *Ch'ullpa*. Old words. But I could not remember how to write them — they were sounds, not marks. Breath, not paper. I had said them on the mountain with the others, but now, sitting by the wall with charcoal in my hand, they would not come.

I tried Spanish. *Murió en un derrumbe*. Died in a collapse. But that was for the Cabildo, for the official log. It was not for the *ayllu*.

I tried Quechua again. Different words. *Urqupi wañupun*. Died in the mountain. *Mana qullqiyuq*. No money for the priest. *Ñawpaq simita rimaspa*. Speaking the old words.

But when I looked at what I had written, the marks looked like nothing. They looked like I was trying to certify his death the way Mendoza certified ore. Percentage of loss. Value after extraction.

I crossed them out. Tried again. The charcoal broke. I picked up the pieces, tried to write smaller, tried to fit what mattered into the space on the paper. But the only words that came were the words I had learned: *ley*, certified value, marcos. The language of the system. And the system had no words for this.

The paper stayed blank except for his name and the name of his *ayllu*. I could not write what mattered.

The *ayllu* would know when he did not return. They would send another. The *kuraka* would call another name at the meeting. The system would continue.

I put down the charcoal. The blank paper with Sami's name stayed on the floor beside me.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Sami, cuarto mes. Murió en el derrumbe. Túpac intentó escribir a su ayllu. No pudo. Las palabras que tiene son las palabras del sistema, y el sistema no tiene palabras para esto.*

Sami, fourth month. Died in the collapse. Túpac tried to write to his *ayllu*. He could not. The words he has are the system's words, and the system has no words for this.

---

I saw Martín in the plaza on a Sunday, the day we did not work. He was buying *ch'arki* from a vendor, dried llama meat, tough as leather. I was buying *chuño*. We nodded to each other.

"Walk with me," he said in Quechua.

We walked. The plaza was crowded. Spanish families in their black wool, *mestizos* in leather, *indios* like us in our ponchos. The mountain visible over the rooftops, always visible, smoke rising from its holes.

"You have learned the assayers," Martín said.

"Yes."

"Mendoza is honest."

"Yes."

"His scale does not lie."

"No."

"But his fee is the same as the others."

"Yes. The *tasa* is the *tasa*."

Martín stopped walking. Looked at me. "Túpac, do you remember your first year?"

"I remember."

"You were angry then."

"I was stupid then."

"You knew something then that you do not know now."

I did not understand. "I know more now. I know which assayers are honest. I know when to sell. I know how to survive."

"Yes," Martín said. "You have learned to survive."

He turned and walked toward the foreman's quarters. I did not follow.

That night, I saw him writing in the margin of the log, his pen moving quickly in the hour before dawn. I did not ask what he wrote. It was not my business.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Túpac defiende a Mendoza ahora. No ve que la honestidad de Mendoza no reduce la extracción. La balanza es verdadera, pero la tasa es la trampa. Se ha normalizado. Ya no pelea. Ha aprendido a cargar el peso sin verlo.*

Túpac defends Mendoza now. He does not see that Mendoza's honesty does not reduce the extraction. The scale is true, but the fee is the trap. He has normalized. He no longer fights. He has learned to carry the weight without seeing it.

---

The second *mita* ended in the dry season, when the air was so thin you could see stars at noon.

I brought my last ore to Mendoza on a Tuesday. Good *ley*. Good ore. I had saved the best vein for last, broken only the richest stone. The fee was what the fee was. The certified value was what it was. I sold for slightly more than the paper said. The buyer did not test the ore. He looked at Mendoza's seal, paid.

I walked back to the *ranchería* with coin in my pocket. Enough to pay for my journey home. Enough to send back to the *ayllu*. Enough to last until the *kuraka* called my name again.

Because he would call my name again. The *ayllu* sent *mitayos* in rotation. I had served my year. Others would serve theirs. Then my turn would come again.

---

On my last day, I saw Martín in the plaza. He was carrying the official log, heading to the Cabildo to file the month's report. I stopped him.

"*Señor* Martín," I said in Spanish, because there were overseers nearby.

"Túpac."

"I wanted to thank you. For the work."

He looked at me. His eyes were tired. "You survived."

"Yes."

"You will return."

It was not a question. "Yes. When the *kuraka* calls my name."

He nodded. Shifted the log under his arm. I saw the leather cover, the ruled pages, the careful Spanish script. I wondered what he had written in the margins, in Quechua, where the Spanish could not read.

"Come with me," he said quietly, in Quechua.

I followed him. Not to the Cabildo. To a room behind the foreman's quarters, small, with a table and a lamp. He set the log down, opened it. Turned pages. The ruled lines filled with Spanish: shaft depths, ore weights, daily wages. And in the margins, in smaller script, Quechua words.

"Can you read this?" he asked.

"A little," I said. "I learned some, between *mitas*."

"Then read." He pointed to a margin note from years ago.

I leaned close, squinted at the script. The words came slowly. "*Túpac Mamani, primer año. Llegó enojado. Dijo que Mendoza es un ladrón. Todavía ve la extracción.*"

Túpac Mamani, first year. Arrived angry. Said Mendoza is a thief. Still sees the extraction.

He turned pages. Found another note. I read: "*Túpac, segundo año. Ya conoce las balanzas. Sobrevivirá.*"

Túpac, second year. He knows the scales now. He will survive.

Another page. "*Túpac defiende a Mendoza ahora. Ha olvidado lo que sabía.*"

Túpac defends Mendoza now. He has forgotten what he knew.

I looked at the margins. Years of notes, years of names. *Mitayos* who had come and gone. Some marked *murió* — died. Some marked *huyó* — fled. Some marked *regresó* — returned. All in Quechua, all in the margins, all invisible to the Spanish who read only the ruled lines.

"Why do you write this?" I asked.

"Because the ruled lines are not the only record," Martín said.

I looked at the words again. *Mendoza es un ladrón*. Mendoza is a thief. I had said that. I remembered saying it. But I also remembered learning that it was not true, that Mendoza was honest, that the fee was the fee.

Both were in the margins. Both were written. The distance between them was the space of years, the space of learning to survive.

"I still return," I said. "Even knowing this. Even reading this."

"Yes," Martín said.

"Then what does the writing do?"

He closed the log. "It records what the change cost."

He picked up the log, turned to leave. At the door, he stopped. "When you return for your third *mita*, I will write this again. I will write that you came back. I will write what you said."

He left. I stood in the small room with the lamp burning. The log was gone. The margins were gone. But I had read them.

---

I write this in the hour before dawn, when the overseers are still in their beds and the *mitayos* are already descending into the mountain. I write in Quechua, in the margins of the official log, where Spanish eyes cannot follow.

The ruled lines hold what the Cabildo requires. Second *mita* completed. No incidents. Ore production this month. Shaft depth to the new vein. Daily wages paid. All in order.

The margins hold what the ruled lines cannot.

*Túpac Mamani, segundo año completo. Regresará cuando el kuraka llame su nombre. Le mostré los márgenes. Leyó su propio nombre, vio lo que ha olvidado. Volverá de todos modos.*

Túpac Mamani, second year complete. He will return when the *kuraka* calls his name. I showed him the margins. He read his own name, saw what he has forgotten. He will return anyway.

*Ya no ve la tasa como robo. La ve como el precio de hacer negocios. Esto es lo que el sistema hace: te enseña a cargar tu propia cadena y llamarla una herramienta.*

He no longer sees the fee as theft. He sees it as the price of doing business. This is what the system does: it teaches you to carry your own chain and call it a tool.

*Mendoza, balanza honesta, tasa alta. Túpac volverá a él. Yo seguiré escribiendo. No porque cambie algo. Porque la alternativa es el silencio.*

Mendoza, honest scale, high fee. Túpac will return to him. I will keep writing. Not because it changes anything. Because the alternative is silence.

The sun is rising. The overseers are waking. I close the log, place it on the shelf with the others. Years of logs, years of margins. Quechua words in the spaces between Spanish lines. A language they cannot read, recording what they cannot see.

The mountain is still there. The *mitayos* are still descending. The system is still working.

I pick up my pen.

I write.
