# The Margins

I write this in the hour before dawn, when the overseers are still in their beds and the *mitayos* are already descending into the mountain. I write in Quechua, in the margins of the official log, where Spanish eyes cannot follow. The ruled lines hold what the Cabildo requires: shaft depths, ore weights, daily wages. The margins hold what matters.

Túpac Mamani returned for a second *mita* three years after his first. Most do not return. Most die in the shafts, or flee to the *yungas*, or go home and never come back even when the *ayllu* sends them. Túpac came back. I asked him why. He said, "I know the mountain now."

This is what I recorded.

---

The air was thinner than I remembered. I stood in the plaza with my bundle — one poncho, one *ch'ullu*, spare *ojotas* — and looked up at Cerro Rico. The mountain was the same. Red-brown stone, holes in its sides like a thousand mouths, smoke from the smelters hanging in the cold. The same, but I was not.

Three years ago I had come here afraid. The *kuraka* had called my name at the *ayllu* meeting. One year in Potosí, then six years home. That was the law. I had walked the four days from the valley with twenty others. Half of us did not walk back.

Now I walked alone. The *ranchería* smelled of stone dust and unwashed wool. Forty men in one room, sleeping on the floor. I found a space near the wall, not the corner — corners went to men who had been here longer — and spread my poncho. The man next to me was coughing. New arrival. First week. His lungs would stop fighting the altitude in a month, or he would die. I did not ask his name.

The foreman came at dawn. Martín Quispe, *mestizo*, leather hat, iron tools on his belt. He spoke Quechua with a Spanish rhythm. "You," he said, looking at me. "You were here before."

"Yes, señor."

"You survived."

"Yes, señor."

"Then you know which shaft."

I knew. The deep one, where the air goes bad and the rock creaks. Where the silver is.

---

The first time I brought ore to an assayer, I did not understand the paper.

It was my second week of the first *mita*. I had broken stone for six days, filled a sack with ore that made my shoulders ache. One hundred marcos, I guessed, maybe more. Rich ore, shot through with dark veins that caught the torchlight. I carried it down the mountain at dusk, the weight pressing into my spine.

The assayer's shop was on the street behind the plaza. Stone building, iron door, smell of burning metal. I had been told to go to Mendoza. "He has an honest scale," the other *mitayos* said. "He does not skim."

Don Francisco de Ulloa y Mendoza. That was his full name, written on the sign above the door in letters I could not read. Inside, the shop was hot. A furnace in the corner, a workbench, scales hanging from chains. Mendoza was older than my father, gray in his beard, black wool coat with silver buttons. He looked at me the way you look at a mule.

"How much?" he said in Spanish.

"*Cien marcos*," I said. One hundred marcos. My Spanish was bad then.

He took a piece of ore from my sack, no bigger than my thumb. Placed it in a clay crucible, put the crucible in the furnace. I watched the ore glow, melt, the dross burning away. The smell made my eyes water. When it cooled, he poured the silver into a small mold, weighed it on the scale. The needle swung, settled. He wrote a number on a piece of paper. Eighty-two.

"Eighty-two percent *ley*," he said. "Eighty-two marcos of pure silver in your sack." He wrote more numbers. Took a pen, made marks I could not read. Then he looked at me. "The *tasa de ensayo*. Eight percent of certified value."

I did not understand. Eighty-two marcos was what the ore was worth. Why did I owe him eight percent?

"For the testing," he said, seeing my face. "For the certification. For the paper. Without the paper, you cannot sell."

He took his fee. Six marcos and change, he said. I left with seventy-five marcos of value and a piece of paper I could not read. The paper was lighter than the ore I had carried down the mountain, but when I went to the market, it was the paper the buyer wanted to see. The ore itself, he barely looked at.

That night I lay on the stone floor of the *ranchería* and felt the missing six marcos like a hole in my chest. I had worked six days. The assayer had worked one hour. Where was the justice in that?

The man next to me — older, third *mita*, hands like tree roots — heard me muttering. "The fee is the fee," he said in Quechua. "Mendoza is honest. His scale does not lie. There are assayers who will write seventy percent when the ore is eighty-two, then sell the difference to their cousins. Mendoza does not do this. You will learn."

I learned. But learning felt like forgetting.

---

Second *mita*, first week. I carried ore down the mountain on a Tuesday, when the market was quiet and the buyers were hungry. One hundred marcos, maybe ninety. I did not guess anymore. I knew by the weight on my back, by the way my knees bent.

Mendoza's shop. Iron door. Smell of burning. He looked up when I entered, did not recognize me. Why would he? Three years, a thousand *mitayos*. I was a body that brought stone.

"*Señor ensayador*," I said. "*Traigo metal*."

He took a sample. Into the furnace. I watched the ore burn. I knew what the number would be before he wrote it. Eighty-four percent *ley*. Good ore. I had chosen the vein carefully, broken only the richest stone.

He wrote the certification. Took his fee. I paid without speaking. Seven marcos, near enough. I left with seventy-seven marcos of value and the paper.

At the market, I waited until the sun was low. The buyers paid more when they were tired, when they wanted to close their stalls and go home. I sold for seventy-eight marcos — one more than the certified value. The buyer did not test the ore. He looked at the paper, looked at Mendoza's seal, paid.

I walked back to the *ranchería* with coin in my pocket. Enough for two weeks of *chuño* and *charqui*. Enough to send a little back to the *ayllu*. The hole in my chest was still there, but smaller. I had learned to carry it.

---

*Martín's margin note, that night:*

*Túpac, segundo año. Ya conoce las balanzas. Sobrevivirá.*

Túpac, second year. He knows the scales now. He will survive.

---

The new *mitayo* in the *ranchería* did not understand the paper.

His name was Sami. First week, first *mita*. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Face still round with youth, not yet hollowed by the mountain. He came back from Mendoza's shop shaking.

"The assayer is a thief," he said in Quechua, loud enough that the whole room heard. "I brought a week's breaking. He took half."

I was mending my *ojotas* by the wall. I looked up. "Mendoza?"

"Yes. Don Francisco de Ulloa y Mendoza. The one everyone says is honest." Sami's voice cracked. "His scale is a lie."

"His scale is true," I said.

"Then why did he take so much?"

I put down the sandal. Looked at him. Saw myself three years ago, angry and uncomprehending. "What was the *ley*?"

"Seventy-nine percent."

"And the fee?"

"Eight percent, he said. But eight percent of what? I brought one hundred marcos. He gave me seventy-two."

I did the arithmetic in my head. Seventy-nine marcos of certified value. Eight percent of that was six and change. Seventy-nine minus six was seventy-three, not seventy-two, but close enough. Mendoza had not stolen. The fee was the fee.

"The eight percent is of the certified value," I said. "Not of what you brought. You brought one hundred marcos of ore. But the ore was only seventy-nine percent silver. So the value was seventy-nine marcos. The fee is eight percent of that."

Sami stared at me. "That is theft."

"That is the *tasa*."

"It is the same thing."

"No," I said. I heard my voice, patient, explaining. The voice of the older *mitayo* who had explained it to me three years ago. "Mendoza provides a service. He tests the ore. He writes the certification. Without the paper, you cannot sell. The paper costs."

"The paper costs more than the work."

"The paper *is* the work. The ore is just stone until someone writes its worth."

Sami looked at me like I had struck him. Then he turned away, lay down on his poncho, faced the wall.

I went back to mending my sandals. My hands were steady. I had explained it correctly. The system was corrupt, yes, but it was also functional. Mendoza's scale was honest. His paper was accepted everywhere. If all assayers were like him, the *mita* would be bearable.

I believed this. I had learned to believe it.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Túpac le dijo al nuevo que trabaje más rápido. No ve que trabajar más rápido solo aumenta la tasa del ensayador. La tasa es un porcentaje. Más metal = más tasa. Ha olvidado las cuentas.*

Túpac told the new one to work faster. He does not see that working faster only increases the assayer's take. The fee is a percentage. More ore = more fee. He has forgotten the math.

---

We measured the new shaft in *varas*.

Martín held one end of the *vara* rod — iron, length of a man's stride, certified by the Cabildo — and I held the other. We stretched it down the shaft, marked the wall with chalk. One *vara*. Two. Three. At fifteen *varas* we hit the vein.

"Fifteen *varas* to silver," Martín said, writing in the log. His handwriting was careful, each letter formed precisely. "Mark it."

I marked it. Fifteen chalk lines on the stone, each one a *vara* apart. The other *mitayos* would know how deep to dig, how much timber to bring for the supports.

Martín watched me work. Then he said, in Quechua, quiet so the Spanish overseer twenty *varas* up the shaft would not hear: "Do you know where the *vara* comes from?"

"The Cabildo," I said.

"Before that."

I shrugged. "Spain."

"Sevilla. The capital. They made the first *vara* there, hundreds of years ago. Sent copies to every city in the empire. Lima has one. Potosí has one. They check them every year, make sure they match. If a *vara* is wrong, they file it down, make it right."

I did not understand why he was telling me this. The *vara* was the *vara*. It was how we measured. How else would you measure?

"Before the Spanish came," Martín said, "we measured differently. The *rikra* — the length from elbow to fingertip. The *sikya* — the length of a foot. Our measures."

"Those are not precise," I said.

"No," Martín said. "But they were ours."

He wrote something in the margin of the log, in Quechua, where the Spanish could not read it. I saw the words but did not ask what they meant. It was not my business. I was not a foreman. I was a body that broke stone.

We climbed back up the shaft. At the top, the overseer asked Martín, "How deep?"

"Fifteen *varas*," Martín said in Spanish. "*Hay plata*."

There is silver.

---

Sami died in the fourth month.

Cave-in. The timber supports in the old shaft gave way. Three *mitayos* were inside. They pulled two out alive. Sami they found the next day, crushed under rock.

We carried him up in pieces. Wrapped him in his poncho, buried him on the mountain. No priest. The priests charged for burials, and none of us had the coin. We said the old words, the ones from before the Spanish, and left him there.

That night I lay on the stone floor and thought about the paper in Mendoza's shop. Sami's certification, still on file. Eighty-one percent *ley*, it said. Seventy-three marcos of value after the fee. The paper existed. The ore existed, somewhere in the market, sold and resold. But Sami was gone. The paper had outlived him.

I thought about writing home to his *ayllu*. Telling them he had died quickly, that he had not suffered. But I did not know how to write, and even if I did, what would I say? Your son is dead, but his ore tested at eighty-one percent? The paper is still good?

I did not write. The *ayllu* would know when he did not return. They would send another.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Sami, cuatro meses. Murió en el derrumbe. Túpac no escribió a su ayllu. No sé si es porque no puede escribir o porque ya no le importa.*

Sami, four months. Died in the collapse. Túpac did not write to his *ayllu*. I do not know if it is because he cannot write or because he no longer cares.

---

I saw Martín in the plaza on a Sunday, the one day we did not work. He was buying *ch'arki* from a vendor, dried llama meat, tough as leather. I was buying *chuño*. We nodded to each other.

"Walk with me," he said in Quechua.

We walked. The plaza was crowded. Spanish families in their black wool, *mestizos* in leather, *indios* like us in our ponchos. The mountain visible over the rooftops, always visible, smoke rising from its holes.

"You have learned the assayers," Martín said.

"Yes."

"Mendoza is honest."

"Yes."

"His scale does not lie."

"No."

"But his fee is eight percent."

"Yes. The same as the others."

"The same," Martín said. He stopped walking, looked at me. "Túpac, do you remember your first year?"

"I remember."

"You were angry then."

"I was stupid then."

"You knew something then that you do not know now."

I did not understand. "I know more now. I know which assayers are honest. I know when to sell. I know how to survive."

"Yes," Martín said. "You know how to survive. But you have forgotten what you are surviving."

We stood in silence. Around us, the market moved. Buyers and sellers, coin changing hands, papers changing hands. The mountain watching.

"Mendoza is honest," I said finally. "The system is corrupt, but he is honest within it. If all assayers were like him, the *mita* would be bearable."

"The fee itself is the corruption," Martín said.

"The fee is the fee. It pays for the testing, the certification. Without it, we could not sell."

"You could sell without the paper."

"No one would buy."

"Because the system requires the paper."

"Yes. And the system works."

Martín looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "You have forgotten what you knew in your first year."

"I have learned what works," I said.

We did not speak after that. We walked back to the *ranchería* in silence. That night, I saw him writing in the margin of the log, his pen moving quickly in the hour before dawn. I did not ask what he wrote. It was not my business.

---

*Martín's margin note:*

*Túpac defiende a Mendoza ahora. No ve que la honestidad de Mendoza no reduce la extracción. La balanza es verdadera, pero la tasa es la trampa. Se ha normalizado. Ya no pelea. Ha aprendido a cargar el peso sin verlo.*

Túpac defends Mendoza now. He does not see that Mendoza's honesty does not reduce the extraction. The scale is true, but the fee is the trap. He has normalized. He no longer fights. He has learned to carry the weight without seeing it.

---

The second *mita* ended in the dry season, when the air was so thin you could see stars at noon.

I brought my last ore to Mendoza on a Tuesday. Eighty-six percent *ley*. Good ore. I had saved the best vein for last, broken only the richest stone. The fee was higher — six marcos and change — but the certified value was higher too. I sold for eighty-one marcos, one more than the paper said. The buyer did not test the ore. He looked at Mendoza's seal, paid.

I walked back to the *ranchería* with coin in my pocket. Enough to pay for my journey home. Enough to send back to the *ayllu*. Enough to last until I returned.

Because I would return. I knew this now. The *ayllu* would call my name again in three years, maybe four, and I would walk the four days from the valley to Potosí. I would find my space in the *ranchería*, near the wall, not the corner. I would descend into the mountain. I would bring ore to Mendoza, pay the fee, sell the paper. I would survive.

This was what I had learned.

---

On my last day, I saw Martín in the plaza. He was carrying the official log, heading to the Cabildo to file the month's report. I stopped him.

"*Señor* Martín," I said in Spanish, because there were overseers nearby.

"Túpac."

"I wanted to thank you. For the work."

He looked at me. His eyes were tired. "You survived."

"Yes."

"You will return."

"Yes."

He nodded. Shifted the log under his arm. I saw the leather cover, the ruled pages, the careful Spanish script. I wondered what he had written in the margins, in Quechua, where the Spanish could not read. I wondered if anyone would ever read it.

"Go home," Martín said. "Rest."

"I will."

I walked away. Behind me, I heard him say, very quietly, in Quechua: "*Yuyariy*."

Remember.

But I did not know what I was supposed to remember. I had learned to survive. What else was there?

---

I write this in the hour before dawn, when the overseers are still in their beds and the *mitayos* are already descending into the mountain. I write in Quechua, in the margins of the official log, where Spanish eyes cannot follow.

The ruled lines hold what the Cabildo requires. Second *mita* completed. No incidents. Ore production: 847 marcos certified this month. Shaft depth: 23 *varas* to the new vein. Daily wages paid. All in order.

The margins hold what matters.

*Túpac Mamani, segundo año completo. Regresará. Escribiré esto otra vez cuando vuelva. Escribiré hasta que alguien lo lea, o hasta que muera, lo que venga primero.*

Túpac Mamani, second year complete. He will return. I will write this again when he comes back. I will write until someone reads this, or until I die, whichever comes first.

*Ya no ve la tasa como robo. La ve como el precio de hacer negocios. Esto es lo que el sistema hace: te enseña a cargar tu propia cadena y llamarla una herramienta.*

He no longer sees the fee as theft. He sees it as the price of doing business. This is what the system does: it teaches you to carry your own chain and call it a tool.

*Mendoza, balanza honesta, tasa alta. Túpac volverá a él. Yo seguiré escribiendo. Alguien tiene que recordar lo que él ha olvidado.*

Mendoza, honest scale, high fee. Túpac will return to him. I will keep writing. Someone has to remember what he has forgotten.

The sun is rising. The overseers are waking. I close the log, place it on the shelf with the others. Forty years of logs, forty years of margins. Quechua words in the spaces between Spanish lines. A language they cannot read, recording what they cannot see.

The mountain is still there. The *mitayos* are still descending. The system is still working.

I pick up my pen.

I write.
