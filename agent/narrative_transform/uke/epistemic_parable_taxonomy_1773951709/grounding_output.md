### The Substrate Split

`✗` **Contrary position:** The empirical/social substrate distinction may be a false dichotomy. Consider:

**Goodhart's Law** (classified as empirical substrate, Family 5): "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The essay treats this as an empirical failure—mistaking noise for signal as optimization pressure changes the data-generating process.

But Goodhart's Law is *fundamentally social*. It describes what happens when intelligent agents respond strategically to measurement systems. The "failure" isn't in reasoning about reality—it's in failing to model other agents as strategic actors who will game your metrics.

Similarly, **Chesterton's Fence** (empirical substrate, Family 7) involves removing constraints without understanding their function. But in organizational contexts, those constraints often exist because of *past strategic conflicts*—the fence marks a truce in a game-theoretic equilibrium. Removing it isn't an epistemic error about physical reality; it's a failure to recognize you're in a multi-player game.

## Alternative Explanations

`✗` **Stronger contrary:** The entire framework may be solving the wrong problem.

The essay treats "epistemic warnings that don't propagate" as a failure of transmission technology. But what if the *lack* of propagation is the correct equilibrium?

Consider: If a methodological insight (like p-hacking awareness) propagates widely, it creates common knowledge that changes strategic incentives. Researchers who previously p-hacked unconsciously must now either:
1. Stop p-hacking (costly: reduces publication rate)
2. P-hack consciously (costly: psychological/reputational)
3. Develop more sophisticated gaming strategies (arms race)

From this perspective, the "transmission failure" of formal frameworks isn't a bug—it's a feature that maintains plausible deniability. Parables propagate because they're *safe*: you can cite survivorship bias without committing to actually correcting for it in your own work.

The framework's proposed solution (create better parables for gap families) might make the problem worse by spreading common knowledge of gaming strategies without changing underlying incentives.
