# The Autonomy We Don't Have: On Borrowed Standards and the Illusion of Self-Authorship

We speak casually of "living by your own values" as if the hard part were the living, not the owning. But most personal autonomy is borrowed heteronomy—we escape one set of external standards only to adopt another without examination, mistaking the relief of changed constraints for the achievement of self-authorship. The real question is not whether you choose your actions, but whether you choose the measures by which you judge a life well-lived.

## The Recognition Trap

Consider the graduate student who abandons corporate ambitions for academic research, convinced she's rejecting conventional success metrics for intellectual integrity. She experiences this as liberation—and in one sense it is. She's traded one boss for another, one set of deadlines for different ones, one status hierarchy for a new pecking order. But has she authored her own standards, or merely selected from a different menu of pre-packaged values?

The psychological mechanism is well-documented: humans are profoundly dependent on external validation. We are social primates whose survival historically depended on group membership, and that dependence has left deep grooves in our motivational architecture. Approval functions as a narcotic—not metaphorically, but in terms of measurable neurochemical reward. When we adopt standards that earn recognition from communities we respect, we experience genuine satisfaction. The problem is that this satisfaction feels identical whether we've authored those standards or merely borrowed them.

This creates what we might call the recognition dependency mechanism: a structural constraint on autonomy that operates regardless of individual psychology or cultural context. You cannot escape needing validation any more than you can escape needing food. The question is whether you recognize this dependency and account for it in your self-understanding, or whether you mistake the pleasure of meeting borrowed standards for the achievement of self-authorship.

## The Heteronomy Adoption Pathway

The graduate student's case illustrates a broader pattern: what looks like autonomy is often heteronomy adoption—the internalization of externally-given standards that are then misrecognized as self-authored. This happens through a specific mechanism:

**Stage 1: Dissatisfaction with Current Standards**
You experience genuine conflict with the values of your current community. The corporate emphasis on quarterly earnings feels hollow. The suburban focus on lawn care and property values seems trivial. This dissatisfaction is real.

**Stage 2: Discovery of Alternative Community**
You encounter a group with different standards—academics who value intellectual contribution, artists who prize creative expression, activists who emphasize social justice. Their values resonate. This resonance is also real.

**Stage 3: Adoption and Internalization**
You adopt the new community's standards. You learn their language, their measures of success, their criteria for judgment. Over time, these standards feel natural, obvious, self-evident. You experience them as your own values.

**Stage 4: Misrecognition as Self-Authorship**
Because you chose to join this community (unlike your family or hometown), and because the values feel authentic (they resolve your earlier dissatisfaction), you experience this as autonomy. You believe you've authored your own standards.

But examine the genealogy: At what point did you *generate* these standards rather than *select* them? The academic values you adopted were already fully formed when you encountered them. The intellectual standards, the methodological commitments, the status markers—all were given to you by the discipline. You chose which menu to order from, but you didn't write the menu.

This is not a criticism of the graduate student. It's a description of how standard-adoption typically works. We are not, most of us, in the business of generating novel evaluative frameworks. We are in the business of finding communities whose existing frameworks fit us well enough that we can thrive within them. The error lies in mistaking this selection process for self-authorship.

### The Structural Asymmetry

The heteronomy adoption pathway creates an asymmetric benefit structure. Communities with legible, socially-recognized standards—academia, professional guilds, religious institutions, political movements—gain adherents who provide labor, loyalty, and legitimacy. The individual gains belonging, purpose, and the psychological rewards of meeting standards that feel meaningful. Both parties benefit, which is why the arrangement persists.

But there's an extraction mechanism hidden in this apparent mutual benefit: the individual surrenders the capacity to revise the standards themselves. You can succeed or fail by academic standards, but you cannot easily question whether those standards are the right ones. Doing so marks you as confused about what academia *is*. The standards become definitional—not just measures of success within the institution, but the very meaning of the enterprise.

This is what makes heteronomy adoption a tangled rope rather than simple coordination: it mixes genuine coordination (the institution provides real benefits) with extraction (it captures your capacity for standard-revision). You cannot untangle these without potentially destroying the coordination benefits. The graduate student who questions whether citation counts and publication venues are the right measures of intellectual contribution risks being marked as someone who "doesn't understand how scholarship works"—even if her criticism is correct.

## The Revision-Rationalization Boundary

The deepest problem emerges when you try to revise your own standards. How do you distinguish legitimate learning from motivated reasoning? How do you know whether you're becoming wiser or just weaker?

Consider three cases:

**Case 1: The Activist's Revision**
A longtime activist realizes that the movement's emphasis on ideological purity has become counterproductive. She begins prioritizing coalition-building over doctrinal correctness, working with groups whose politics she finds incomplete but whose practical efforts align with hers. Is this mature pragmatism or a rationalization for avoiding difficult confrontations?

**Case 2: The Artist's Revision**
An experimental artist who spent decades pursuing formal innovation decides to focus on accessibility and emotional connection. His new work is more popular, more lucrative, and—he insists—more meaningful. Is this genuine evolution or a convenient justification for commercial success?

**Case 3: The Scholar's Revision**
An academic who built her career on rigorous empirical work becomes interested in speculative theory. Her new writing is less constrained by data, more ambitious in its claims, and—critics say—less defensible. Is this intellectual growth or a rationalization for reduced rigor?

In each case, the person has revised their standards in a direction that *happens* to make their life easier, more comfortable, or more rewarding. This doesn't prove the revision is rationalization—sometimes legitimate learning does make life easier. But it creates an epistemic problem: from inside the revision, how can you tell?

### The Meta-Standard Problem

You might think: "Use meta-standards to evaluate the revision. Ask whether the new standards better serve your deeper values." But this just pushes the problem back a level. How do you know your meta-standards are right? And if you revise those too, what stops the infinite regress?

The standard philosophical answer is coherence: your standards should form a coherent system, and revisions should increase rather than decrease that coherence. But this is insufficient. You can be coherently heteronomous—perfectly consistent in following borrowed standards you've never examined. Coherence distinguishes good reasoning from bad reasoning within a framework, but it doesn't distinguish self-authored frameworks from adopted ones.

Moreover, the demand for coherence can itself be a form of suppression. Real self-authorship might require tolerating incoherence as you work out genuinely novel standards. The person who revises their values and then immediately demands perfect consistency may be prematurely foreclosing the exploratory process that genuine self-authorship requires.

### The Temporal Self Problem

There's a further complication: you are not one self but many selves across time. The person who adopted the standards ten years ago is not quite the same person evaluating them today. Should your current self be bound by commitments made by your past self? Or does self-authorship require the capacity to renegotiate with your temporal selves?

Frantz Fanon's principle—that principles should serve the life generating them, not execute that life—suggests the latter. Standards are instruments, not natural laws. They exist to enable flourishing, not to judge and constrain it. When standards become obstacles to the life they were meant to serve, revision is not weakness but necessity.

But this principle, however compelling, doesn't resolve the epistemic problem. How do you distinguish "standards that have become obstacles" from "standards that are difficult to meet"? The person rationalizing their way out of commitments will say the former; the person maintaining discipline will say the latter. From inside your own case, both interpretations are available.

## The Extraction Blindness

Here's what makes this particularly difficult: the heteronomy adoption pathway and the revision-rationalization boundary create what we might call extraction blindness—a systematic inability to recognize when you're being exploited by your own standards.

From the powerless individual's perspective, both constraints appear as traps. You adopted standards that seemed liberating, but now they constrain you in ways you didn't anticipate. When you try to revise them, you face accusations of rationalization, weakness, or betrayal. You experience this as a snare—an extractive arrangement you cannot easily escape.

But from the institutional perspective, these same constraints appear functional and fair. The academic institution sees standards as necessary for quality control. The activist movement sees ideological commitment as essential for effectiveness. The artistic community sees formal standards as what distinguishes art from decoration. From this view, the constraints are ropes—coordination mechanisms that benefit everyone by maintaining shared standards.

This perspectival gap is not accidental. It's built into the structure of how standards operate. Institutions benefit from stable standards that don't require constant renegotiation. Individuals benefit from the flexibility to revise standards as they learn and grow. These interests are not perfectly aligned, and the misalignment creates an extraction mechanism that's difficult to see from either position alone.

The person trapped in borrowed standards experiences the constraint as natural—"this is just what it means to be a scholar/artist/activist." The institution maintaining those standards also experiences them as natural—"these are the standards that define the practice." Both are wrong. The standards are constructed, historically contingent, and revisable. But the very success of heteronomy adoption depends on both parties forgetting this.

## What Self-Authorship Would Require

If most autonomy is borrowed heteronomy, what would genuine self-authorship look like? Not complete independence from external standards—that's impossible and undesirable. We are social beings who need recognition and community. But something different from the unreflective adoption of pre-packaged values.

Self-authorship would require:

**1. Genealogical Awareness**
Understanding where your standards came from. Not just "I value intellectual integrity," but "I adopted this value from the academic community I joined at age 22, which itself inherited it from a particular tradition of European scholarship with specific historical origins and blind spots."

**2. Ongoing Revision Capacity**
Maintaining the ability to question and revise your standards, not just your actions. This means resisting the naturalization of standards—the process by which historically contingent values come to feel like natural laws.

**3. Tolerance for Incoherence**
Accepting that genuine exploration of values may require periods of inconsistency. The demand for immediate coherence can suppress the experimental process through which novel standards emerge.

**4. Meta-Standard Examination**
Questioning not just your first-order standards but the meta-standards you use to evaluate them. Why do you value coherence? Why do you prioritize certain kinds of evidence? These too are choices, not givens.

**5. Community Accountability Without Capture**
Finding ways to remain accountable to communities without surrendering your capacity for standard-revision. This is perhaps the hardest requirement—how do you stay connected to communities whose standards you're questioning?

## Unresolved Questions

This analysis raises more questions than it answers:

**On Recognition Dependency:** Is the need for external validation truly universal, or does it vary across cultures and developmental stages? Collectivist cultures may have different relationships to external standards than individualist ones. What would self-authorship look like in a context where heteronomy is normatively preferred?

**On the Revision-Rationalization Boundary:** We lack reliable criteria for distinguishing legitimate revision from motivated reasoning. Is this an epistemic problem with a solution, or a fundamental limitation of first-person perspective? Can external accountability mechanisms help, or do they just displace rationalization to a different level?

**On Temporal Stability:** How stable must meta-standards be to serve their function? If you revise your criteria for revision too frequently, you lose the capacity for meaningful evaluation. But if they're too stable, you risk the same heteronomy problem at a higher level.

**On Extraction Mechanisms:** The perspectival gap between individual experience (snare) and institutional function (rope) suggests that extraction is being masked by the structure itself. But how do we measure this? What would constitute evidence that the constraint is genuinely extractive rather than just difficult?

## Institutional Implications

If this analysis is correct, several institutional responses become necessary:

**1. Educational Reform**
Schools and universities should teach genealogical awareness—not just transmitting standards but examining their origins and contingency. Students should learn to ask not just "What should I value?" but "Where do these values come from, and what alternatives exist?"

**2. Professional Standards Review**
Institutions should regularly examine whether their standards serve their stated purposes or have become self-perpetuating. Academic departments should ask whether citation metrics actually measure intellectual contribution. Medical boards should ask whether licensing requirements actually protect patients or just protect incumbents.

**3. Exit Option Protection**
If heteronomy adoption creates lock-in effects, institutions should actively protect exit options. This means not just formal freedom to leave but practical alternatives—portable credentials, recognition of diverse paths, resistance to winner-take-all dynamics.

**4. Revision Process Design**
Communities need explicit processes for standard-revision that don't require leaving the community entirely. How can academic disciplines evolve their standards without forcing dissenters into exile? How can activist movements remain ideologically coherent while allowing internal criticism?

## Conclusion

The distinction between autonomy of choices and autonomy of standards reveals something uncomfortable: most of what we experience as self-authorship is actually sophisticated standard-selection from pre-existing menus. We choose which constraints to adopt, but we rarely generate the constraints themselves.

This is not a counsel of despair. Recognizing borrowed heteronomy for what it is doesn't require rejecting all external standards or pursuing some impossible ideal of complete independence. But it does require honesty about the difference between choosing from a menu and writing one. It requires maintaining the capacity for genuine revision, not just the appearance of choice. And it requires building institutions that support rather than suppress this capacity.

The person who escapes corporate values for academic ones, who trades suburban conformity for artistic bohemianism, who rejects traditional religion for social justice activism—they have made real choices with real consequences. But they have not necessarily authored their own standards. They have traded one form of heteronomy for another, hopefully one that fits better. The question is whether they know this, and whether knowing it changes anything.

Perhaps the goal is not to eliminate heteronomy—that may be impossible—but to make it conscious, provisional, and revisable. To hold standards lightly enough that they serve life rather than judging it. To remember that the measures by which we evaluate a life well-lived are themselves instruments, not natural laws. And to resist the institutional pressures that would make us forget this, that would naturalize the contingent and extract from us the capacity for genuine self-authorship.

## Evidence Framework

### Documented in Public Records (Tier 1):
- Recognition dependency has neurobiological basis: social approval activates reward circuitry in measurable ways (neuroscience literature on social reward)
- Standard-adoption follows documented patterns in professional socialization (sociology of professions literature)
- Institutional standards show historical contingency and variation across cultures (comparative institutional analysis)

### Reasonable Inferences from Documented Facts (Tier 2):
- The gap between experienced autonomy and actual standard-authorship follows from the documented mechanisms of social learning and reward
- Extraction mechanisms in standard-adoption are inferred from the documented asymmetry between institutional and individual perspectives
- The revision-rationalization boundary problem follows necessarily from the first-person epistemic position

### Structural Hypotheses Requiring Additional Evidence (Tier 3):
- The universality of recognition dependency across cultures requires cross-cultural psychological research
- The base rate of rationalization among self-assessed principled revisions requires longitudinal empirical study
- The effectiveness of institutional interventions (education reform, exit option protection) requires experimental validation

## Alternative Explanations Considered

**Simpler Explanation 1: Individual Variation**
Perhaps some people genuinely author their standards while others adopt them. This could be a personality variable rather than a structural constraint.

**Why Insufficient:** While individual variation exists, the mechanisms described (recognition dependency, social learning, institutional pressure) operate on everyone. The question is not whether anyone can author standards, but whether standard-authorship is as common as people believe. The evidence suggests widespread misrecognition of heteronomy as autonomy.

**Simpler Explanation 2: Maturity and Development**
Perhaps young people adopt standards heteronomously, but mature adults achieve genuine self-authorship through experience.

**Why Insufficient:** While development matters, the structural constraints don't disappear with age. Older adults may have more sophisticated heteronomy (better at selecting standards that fit), but sophistication in selection is not the same as authorship. The revision-rationalization boundary problem persists regardless of age.

## Unresolved Questions

**Empirical Psychology:** What does longitudinal research show about the stability of value systems across the lifespan? Do people who report major value revisions show different patterns than those who maintain consistent values? Can we measure the difference between adopted and authored standards?

**Cultural Variance:** Does this analysis hold in collectivist cultures where individual autonomy is not the primary value? Is heteronomy adoption experienced differently when it's culturally normative rather than hidden?

**Fanon Textual Fidelity:** The essay attributes to Frantz Fanon the principle that "principles should serve the life generating them, not execute that life." This requires textual verification—does Fanon actually make this argument in this form, and if so, in what context?

**Revision Criteria:** The essay acknowledges it "does not know how to specify when revision becomes rationalization." Is this an omega (bounded uncertainty requiring more research) or a fundamental limitation of first-person perspective that no amount of evidence can resolve?
