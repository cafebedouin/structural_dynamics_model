# When Arbitration Becomes Suppression: How Private Dispute Resolution Eliminates Workplace Rights

## Pattern First

For thirty years, American employers have systematically adopted mandatory arbitration clauses requiring employees to resolve workplace disputes in private forums rather than courts. This practice now covers more than 60 million workers—over half of the private-sector nonunion workforce. During this period, three documented patterns have emerged that standard explanations cannot adequately account for:

**First, the filing rate collapse.** Despite covering tens of millions of workers with valid legal claims, the arbitration system processed just 577 winning cases in 2020—a success rate of 4.1% among those who filed, and a participation rate suggesting 98% of covered workers never file at all. This represents a claim suppression rate orders of magnitude beyond what efficiency improvements would predict.

**Second, the repeat-player award degradation.** When the same employer appears before the same arbitrator multiple times, damages decline 16.6% per case—from $20,903 on the first pairing to $107 by the thirtieth. This pattern persists across thousands of cases and cannot be explained by case selection effects, as the decline occurs within employer-arbitrator pairs controlling for case characteristics.

**Third, the demographic concentration.** Black workers (59.1%) and women (57.6%) face mandatory arbitration at rates above the workforce average (56.2%), with the gap widening in low-wage industries where wage theft claims are most common. The system's adoption correlates not with litigation risk broadly but with workforce composition and violation type.

These patterns demand institutional explanation. They could reflect legitimate efficiency gains in dispute resolution. However, three elements distinguish this system from typical procedural reform:

**The patterns intensified after initial exposure.** Standard elite corruption typically moderates once public attention focuses on abuses. Here, adoption accelerated—from under 10% of employers in the 1990s to over 50% today—precisely as outcome disparities became documented. Employers with the lowest-paid workforces became the most likely adopters.

**Protection operates through structural barriers, not individual decisions.** The system doesn't require corrupt arbitrators or malicious employers. Award degradation occurs through repeat-player dynamics inherent to any private dispute market with asymmetric participation. Claim suppression operates through attorney gatekeeping responding to rational economic incentives. The mechanisms are architectural.

**The system survived regulatory interventions that should have constrained it.** Despite Supreme Court expansion of the Federal Arbitration Act's scope in 25+ decisions (1985-2015), state legislative attempts to restrict enforceability, and federal prohibition of sexual harassment arbitration (2022), employer adoption continued growing. Each intervention addressed symptoms while leaving the core infrastructure intact.

The unresolved question is: what institutional arrangements convert a dispute resolution procedure into a claim suppression infrastructure—and what evidence would distinguish between a system that reduces frivolous litigation versus one that eliminates valid rights enforcement?

## Why This Matters

Regardless of whether mandatory arbitration represents intentional rights elimination or emergent system failure, four institutional vulnerabilities remain:

**First, the repeat-player advantage creates structural bias independent of individual arbitrator integrity.** When one party (employers) appears repeatedly before the same decision-makers while the other party (individual employees) appears once, financial and reputational dependencies form that no ethics code can eliminate. The documented 16.6% award decline per pairing quantifies this effect.

**Second, the claim suppression rate (98% of covered workers never file) indicates that legal rights have become unenforceable abstractions for most affected workers.** Even if arbitration were perfectly fair for cases that proceed, a system where 98% of valid claims are never filed has failed its core function. The $9.27 billion in unrecovered wages for workers earning under $13/hour measures this failure's scale.

**Third, the system's demographic concentration (Black workers and women disproportionately covered) means that rights elimination affects already-marginalized groups most severely.** This isn't incidental—the industries with highest arbitration adoption (retail, food service, warehousing) employ disproportionately minority and female workforces. Structural inequality compounds.

**Fourth, the confidentiality and atomization features prevent pattern detection across multiple claims.** When each dispute resolves privately and class actions are prohibited (41.1% of arbitration agreements include class waivers), employers can sustain systemic violations that would trigger regulatory intervention if publicly visible. Sexual harassment claims before 2022 demonstrated this dynamic.

## Evidence Framework

### Documented in Public Records (Tier 1)

**Prevalence and Growth:**
- 56.2% of private-sector nonunion employees now subject to mandatory arbitration (Colvin 2017 national survey)
- Growth from under 10% in the 1990s to over 50% by 2017, covering 60+ million workers
- Firm size correlation: 49.8% of employers with <100 employees vs. 67.7% of employers with 5,000+ employees require arbitration
- 41.1% of employees covered by arbitration also subject to class action waivers (Colvin 2019)

**Outcome Disparities:**
- Federal court: employees win 36.4% of cases with average award of $336,291
- Arbitration: employees win 18.9% of cases with average award of $21,871
- 2020 filing data: 577 employees won monetary awards in arbitration (4.1% win rate) despite 60 million covered workers
- Repeat-player effect: damages decline 16.6% per employer-arbitrator pairing, from $20,903 (first pairing) to $107 (30th pairing) (Colvin and Gough study)

**Demographic Disparities:**
- Black workers: 59.1% subject to mandatory arbitration
- Women workers: 57.6% subject to mandatory arbitration
- Overall workforce: 56.2% subject to mandatory arbitration
- Concentration in low-wage industries (retail, food service, warehousing)

**Claim Suppression:**
- 98% of workers subject to arbitration never file claims (Estlund study)
- Plaintiff-side attorneys less likely to accept arbitration cases due to lower success rates and damages (Colvin 2014)
- $9.27 billion in unrecovered wages (2019) for workers earning <$13/hour in firms with mandatory arbitration

**Legal Framework Evolution:**
- Federal Arbitration Act (1925): originally limited to contractual disputes between large corporate actors
- Circuit City Stores v. Adams (2001): Supreme Court narrowly interpreted employment contract exemption, applying FAA to nearly all employment contracts
- Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis (2018): Supreme Court upheld enforceability of class action waivers in arbitration agreements
- 25+ Supreme Court decisions (1985-2015) expanding FAA scope and restricting state protective laws
- Federal prohibition of sexual harassment/assault arbitration (2022)
- American Arbitration Association rule changes (May 2025): requiring redacted award publication and quarterly caseload transparency

**State Legislative Response:**
- New York: prohibits mandatory arbitration for discrimination-related claims
- New Jersey: law making pre-dispute arbitration agreements essentially unenforceable (challenged by business groups, pending federal court review)
- Proposed federal FAIR Act (S. 2799, September 2025): would prohibit predispute arbitration agreements and class action waivers

**Mass Arbitration Counteroffensive:**
- Plaintiff attorneys filing hundreds of identical claims to impose filing fee costs on employers
- $300+ million recovered through mass arbitration strategy
- Some companies eliminated arbitration clauses in response (specific companies not named in source material)

### Reasonable Inferences from Documented Facts (Tier 2)

**The repeat-player advantage reflects structural market dynamics, not individual corruption:**

The 16.6% award decline per employer-arbitrator pairing occurs too consistently across thousands of cases to reflect case selection or random variation. The pattern holds controlling for case characteristics. This suggests the advantage operates through:
- **Reputational dependency:** Arbitrators who consistently rule against repeat-player employers receive fewer future appointments
- **Informational asymmetry:** Employers learn arbitrator preferences across multiple cases while employees appear once
- **Financial incentive:** Even without direct payment corruption, arbitrators' continued business depends on employer satisfaction

This inference follows necessarily from the documented pattern's consistency and magnitude. Alternative explanations (employers bring stronger cases over time, arbitrators learn legitimate patterns) cannot account for awards declining to $107 by the 30th pairing—below minimum wage violation damages in most jurisdictions.

**The 98% non-filing rate indicates systemic claim suppression, not frivolous case filtering:**

If arbitration simply filtered frivolous claims, we would expect:
- Similar filing rates for strong vs. weak cases
- Attorney acceptance rates tracking case merit
- Comparable outcomes for cases that proceed

Instead, we observe:
- Attorneys declining cases due to lower damages and success rates (not case weakness)
- Awards averaging $21,871 in arbitration vs. $336,291 in court for cases that proceed
- $9.27 billion in documented unrecovered wages for low-wage workers

This pattern indicates the barrier operates before merit assessment—the system suppresses claim filing itself, not just weak claims. The inference is marked explicitly as such: we cannot prove individual workers had valid claims they chose not to file, but the aggregate wage theft figure and attorney behavior pattern support this interpretation.

**The demographic concentration suggests the system targets vulnerable workforces:**

Black workers and women face mandatory arbitration at rates 3-6 percentage points above the workforce average. This gap appears modest but becomes significant when combined with:
- Industry concentration in low-wage sectors (retail, food service, warehousing)
- These industries' high rates of wage theft and discrimination claims
- Larger employers (who disproportionately adopt arbitration) employing more diverse workforces

The inference: employers adopt arbitration not randomly but strategically, concentrating it in workforces most likely to generate claims and least able to bear litigation costs. This doesn't require proving discriminatory intent—the correlation between adoption rates and workforce vulnerability is documented.

### Structural Hypotheses Requiring Additional Evidence (Tier 3)

**Hypothesis: Arbitration clause adoption represents coordinated strategy rather than independent efficiency decisions**

**Evidence that would verify:**
- Documentation of industry coordination on arbitration adoption (similar to the 1999 "Arbitration Coalition" formed by credit card issuers)
- Employer communications explicitly discussing claim suppression benefits rather than cost savings
- Temporal clustering of adoptions within industries beyond what independent decision-making would predict

**Evidence that would falsify:**
- Quantitative employer cost-benefit analyses showing genuine litigation cost savings
- Random adoption timing within industries
- Employers abandoning arbitration when mass arbitration filing fees exceed litigation costs (some evidence of this exists but limited)

**Current status:** The 1999 Arbitration Coalition demonstrates coordination occurred in consumer arbitration. Employment arbitration adoption shows similar temporal patterns (acceleration after Supreme Court decisions) but lacks documented coordination. This remains hypothesis pending additional evidence.

**Hypothesis: The Supreme Court's FAA expansion represents policy preference rather than statutory interpretation**

**Evidence that would verify:**
- Historical analysis showing 1925 FAA legislative intent excluded employment contracts
- Documentation of business lobbying influence on Court appointments or case selection
- Analysis of judicial reasoning showing policy goals rather than textual interpretation

**Evidence that would falsify:**
- Legislative history supporting broad FAA application to employment
- Consistent application of textualist interpretation principles across the Court's arbitration jurisprudence
- Similar FAA expansion in jurisdictions without business lobbying

**Current status:** Legal scholars dispute FAA interpretation, but proving the Court prioritized policy over text requires access to internal deliberations and counterfactual analysis beyond this essay's scope. The 25+ pro-arbitration decisions and narrow interpretation of the employment contract exemption (Circuit City) support this hypothesis but don't prove it.

**Hypothesis: Arbitrator compensation structure creates financial dependency on repeat-player employers**

**Evidence that would verify:**
- Documentation of arbitrator payment mechanisms (who pays, per-case vs. retainer fees)
- Analysis of arbitrator income sources showing employer repeat business percentage
- Correlation between arbitrator financial dependency and award amounts

**Evidence that would falsify:**
- Arbitrator compensation independent of case outcomes or party satisfaction
- Random assignment preventing employer-arbitrator repeat pairings
- Award amounts uncorrelated with arbitrator financial dependency

**Current status:** Research documents the repeat-player effect but doesn't address arbitrator compensation structure. This gap is critical—if arbitrators are paid by the initiating party (typically employers in employment arbitration), the repeat-player advantage may reflect direct financial dependency rather than just reputational effects. The American Arbitration Association's 2025 rule changes don't address payment structure.

## Alternative Explanations Considered

**Simpler Explanation #1: Arbitration reduces litigation costs and frivolous claims**

**Why insufficient:** This explanation predicts:
- Similar or better outcomes for meritorious cases that proceed
- Cost savings benefiting both parties
- Random demographic distribution of coverage

We instead observe:
- Awards averaging 93% lower in arbitration ($21,871 vs. $336,291)
- 98% of workers never filing (suppression beyond frivolous case filtering)
- Demographic concentration in vulnerable workforces
- $9.27 billion in unrecovered wages for low-wage workers

The cost-savings explanation cannot account for awards declining to $107 by the 30th employer-arbitrator pairing or the magnitude of unrecovered wage theft.

**Simpler Explanation #2: Employees choose arbitration voluntarily for speed and informality**

**Why insufficient:** This explanation requires:
- Meaningful negotiation over arbitration clauses
- Employee awareness of rights being waived
- Evidence of employee preference for arbitration

We instead observe:
- Take-it-or-leave-it contracts with no negotiation power ("workers must either accept the contract as is or reject the job")
- Arbitration clauses "buried in the fine print"
- Firm size correlation (67.7% of large employers vs. 49.8% of small) suggesting employer preference drives adoption, not employee demand

If employees preferred arbitration, we would expect higher adoption rates at smaller firms (where individual relationships matter more) and evidence of employees requesting arbitration clauses. Neither pattern exists.

**Simpler Explanation #3: Lower arbitration awards reflect weaker cases**

**Why insufficient:** This explanation predicts:
- Consistent award amounts across employer-arbitrator pairings
- Attorney acceptance rates tracking case strength
- Similar win rates for comparable cases in court vs. arbitration

We instead observe:
- Awards declining 16.6% per pairing within employer-arbitrator pairs (controlling for case characteristics)
- Attorneys declining arbitration cases despite case merit
- Win rates of 18.9% in arbitration vs. 36.4% in court

The weaker-cases explanation cannot account for the repeat-player pattern or why attorneys would decline meritorious cases systematically.

**Key Distinguishing Evidence:**

The critical difference between "efficiency improvement" and "claim suppression infrastructure" is the **repeat-player award degradation pattern**. No efficiency explanation predicts awards declining to $107 by the 30th employer-arbitrator pairing. This pattern is diagnostic—it indicates structural bias inherent to the market design, not case selection or legitimate learning effects.

## Institutional Actions Required

These recommendations address documented vulnerabilities regardless of which hypothesis proves correct:

### 1. Eliminate Repeat-Player Advantage Through Structural Reform

**Responsible Institution:** American Arbitration Association, JAMS, and other arbitration administrators

**Specific Action:** Implement random arbitrator assignment preventing employer-arbitrator repeat pairings beyond 3 cases per 5-year period. Publish quarterly data on pairing frequency and award amounts by pairing number.

**Timeline:** Rule changes effective within 12 months; first transparency report within 18 months

**Why this addresses the problem:** The documented 16.6% award decline per pairing indicates the repeat-player advantage operates through arbitrator dependency on employer repeat business. Random assignment breaks this dependency without requiring proof of individual arbitrator bias. The AAA's May 2025 transparency rules provide the disclosure framework; this extends it to address the documented structural bias.

**Requires no new legal authority:** Arbitration administrators already control arbitrator assignment. This is internal procedural reform.

### 2. Require Pre-Dispute Disclosure of Arbitration Outcome Data

**Responsible Institution:** Federal Trade Commission (consumer protection authority) and Department of Labor (employment rights enforcement)

**Specific Action:** Mandate that employers include the following data in arbitration agreements offered to employees:
- Historical win rates for employee claims at this employer (past 5 years)
- Average award amounts for employee claims at this employer (past 5 years)
- Comparison to industry averages and court outcomes
- Right to consult attorney before signing

**Timeline:** Rulemaking initiated within 6 months; compliance required within 18 months

**Why this addresses the problem:** The "consent as structural fiction" pattern operates partly through information asymmetry—employees don't know they're waiving rights to forums with 18.9% win rates and $21,871 average awards. Disclosure doesn't eliminate the power imbalance but converts uninformed waiver to informed choice. If arbitration truly offers efficiency benefits, disclosure should increase voluntary adoption.

**Legal authority:** FTC has consumer protection rulemaking authority; DOL has employment rights enforcement authority. This follows existing disclosure requirements for financial products and employment contracts.

### 3. Prohibit Class Action Waivers in Arbitration Agreements

**Responsible Institution:** Congress (legislative action required)

**Specific Action:** Amend the Federal Arbitration Act to prohibit enforcement of class action waivers in employment arbitration agreements, following the model of the 2022 sexual harassment arbitration prohibition.

**Timeline:** Legislative proposal within current session; passage within 24 months

**Why this addresses the problem:** The combination of mandatory arbitration plus class action waivers converts systemic violations affecting thousands into thousands of individual disputes, each too small to litigate economically. The documented pattern shows 41.1% of arbitration agreements include class waivers, and the Epic Systems (2018) ruling upheld their enforceability. This creates the documented claim suppression effect—98% of workers never file because individual claims don't justify attorney costs.

**Precedent:** Congress already prohibited sexual harassment arbitration (2022) and the proposed FAIR Act (S. 2799) would extend this prohibition. The institutional mechanism exists; this recommendation narrows it to the specific feature (class waivers) that enables systemic violation concealment.

### 4. Create Federal Counterintelligence Assessment Protocol for Workplace Rights Enforcement Gaps

**Responsible Institution:** Department of Justice, National Security Division (counterintelligence authority)

**Specific Action:** Conduct systematic assessment of whether foreign state actors exploit mandatory arbitration to:
- Suppress worker organizing in strategic industries
- Conceal wage theft and labor trafficking
- Eliminate transparency in supply chains with national security implications

Report findings to congressional intelligence committees within 12 months.

**Timeline:** Assessment initiated within 6 months; interim report within 12 months; final report within 24 months

**Why this addresses the problem:** The documented pattern shows mandatory arbitration eliminates transparency and collective action in industries including warehousing, logistics, and food service—sectors with documented foreign state interest in labor disruption and supply chain intelligence. The $9.27 billion in unrecovered wages for low-wage workers indicates enforcement gaps at scale. Whether foreign actors deliberately exploit these gaps or opportunistically benefit, the vulnerability exists.

**Legal authority:** DOJ counterintelligence authority includes assessment of foreign exploitation of U.S. institutional vulnerabilities. This requires no new authority and addresses the documented gap between criminal prosecution (wage theft) and security assessment (foreign exploitation of enforcement gaps).

### 5. Mandate Arbitration Outcome Reporting to State Attorneys General

**Responsible Institution:** State legislatures (following New York and New Jersey models)

**Specific Action:** Require arbitration administrators to report quarterly to state attorneys general:
- Number of employee claims filed
- Win rates by claim type
- Award amounts by claim type
- Employer-arbitrator pairing frequency
- Demographic data on claimants (where available)

**Timeline:** Model legislation drafted within 6 months; state adoption within 24 months

**Why this addresses the problem:** The documented 98% non-filing rate and 4.1% win rate indicate claim suppression at scale, but confidentiality prevents state enforcement agencies from detecting patterns. Mandatory reporting to state AGs (who already have wage theft and discrimination enforcement authority) creates the transparency needed for regulatory intervention without requiring federal preemption challenges.

**Legal authority:** States retain concurrent enforcement authority over wage and hour laws, discrimination statutes, and consumer protection. Reporting requirements for dispute resolution outcomes fall within existing state regulatory authority, similar to insurance claim reporting requirements.

## Unresolved Questions

The following questions could be answered by existing institutions but remain unaddressed:

**1. What is the arbitrator compensation structure, and how does it create financial dependency on repeat-player employers?**

*Who could answer:* American Arbitration Association, JAMS, and other arbitration administrators maintain this data internally.

*Why it matters:* If arbitrators are paid per case by the initiating party (typically employers), the repeat-player advantage reflects direct financial dependency, not just reputational effects. This would indicate the 16.6% award decline per pairing is structural, not correctable through ethics training. The answer determines whether reform requires changing payment structure or just assignment procedures.

*What would constitute an answer:* Publication of arbitrator compensation mechanisms (per-case fees vs. retainers, who pays, whether payment depends on case outcomes or party satisfaction) and correlation analysis between arbitrator income sources and award amounts.

**2. Do union-negotiated arbitration agreements produce different outcomes than unilaterally imposed agreements?**

*Who could answer:* National Labor Relations Board, Department of Labor, and union research departments have access to both union-negotiated arbitration data and non-union mandatory arbitration data.

*Why it matters:* If bilaterally negotiated arbitration (where unions represent workers) produces higher win rates and awards than unilaterally imposed arbitration, this indicates the problem is power imbalance in contract formation, not arbitration as a forum. The research context explicitly excludes union contracts from prevalence statistics but doesn't analyze them as a counterfactual. This gap prevents distinguishing between "arbitration is inherently biased" vs. "unilateral arbitration imposition is biased."

*What would constitute an answer:* Comparative outcome analysis of union-negotiated arbitration vs. mandatory non-union arbitration, controlling for claim type, industry, and worker demographics.

**3. What specific evidence would employers accept as proof that mandatory arbitration suppresses valid claims rather than filtering frivolous ones?**

*Who could answer:* Employer associations, business groups challenging state arbitration restrictions, and corporate legal departments defending arbitration policies.

*Why it matters:* The current debate operates in bad faith if no evidence could change employer positions. If employers would accept specific empirical findings (e.g., "if win rates fall below X% or awards decline below $Y by the Nth pairing, we'll eliminate arbitration"), this creates a verification standard. If no evidence would change their position, the efficiency argument is post-hoc rationalization, not empirical claim.

*What would constitute an answer:* Public statement by major employer associations specifying what empirical findings would lead them to abandon mandatory arbitration or accept regulatory restrictions.

**4. How many workers subject to mandatory arbitration have valid wage theft or discrimination claims they haven't filed?**

*Who could answer:* Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division, Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, and state labor enforcement agencies conduct workplace investigations independent of worker complaints.

*Why it matters:* The 98% non-filing rate is only problematic if it represents suppressed valid claims rather than absence of violations. Random workplace audits in firms with mandatory arbitration vs. those without would reveal whether violation rates differ or only filing rates differ. This directly tests the "claim suppression" vs. "frivolous filtering" hypothesis.

*What would constitute an answer:* Randomized audit study comparing violation detection rates in workplaces with vs. without mandatory arbitration, controlling for industry, firm size, and workforce demographics. Sample size N=500+ workplaces to detect differences in violation rates.

**5. What would it cost employers to litigate claims currently subject to arbitration if arbitration were prohibited?**

*Who could answer:* Corporate legal departments, insurance companies providing employment practices liability insurance, and litigation cost consultants maintain this data.

*Why it matters:* Employers claim arbitration reduces costs, but provide no quantitative comparison of litigation costs vs. arbitration costs. If arbitration saves employers $X per claim but reduces worker awards by $Y, and Y >> X, the efficiency argument is pretextual—the benefit is claim suppression, not cost reduction. If X > Y, genuine efficiency gains exist.

*What would constitute an answer:* Industry-wide cost analysis comparing total employer spending on arbitration (administrator fees, attorney fees, awards) vs. projected litigation costs for the same claims, accounting for the 98% non-filing rate (which would increase if court access restored).
