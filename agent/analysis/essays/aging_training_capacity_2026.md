[THE ONE-INCH FRAME]

A 62-year-old surgeon jumps onto a box. Not for performance. Not because it feels good. Because the capacity to generate explosive power in the sagittal plane — the ability to propel your body upward against gravity — erodes predictably with age if untrained, and once lost, the life that requires it becomes unavailable.

---

## The Narrowing

Human movement capacity does not decline uniformly with age. It declines along the gradient of use. The neuromuscular patterns you train remain available. The ones you don't train disappear.

This is not metaphor. Skeletal muscle adapts to imposed demand with extraordinary specificity. Train sagittal-plane movements (walking, running, cycling) and sagittal-plane capacity is preserved. Neglect frontal-plane movements (lateral shuffles, side lunges) and lateral stability degrades. Avoid rotational power (throwing, swinging) and the ability to generate force through rotation atrophies. The body does not maintain what it does not use.

The mechanism is well-established: motor unit recruitment follows use-dependent patterns, muscle fiber composition shifts toward trained modalities, and neural pathways that coordinate complex movements require regular activation to remain functional. This is not aging per se — it is disuse atrophy accelerated by the reduced movement demands of modern life and compounded by the biological reality that recovery from disuse becomes harder with each passing decade.

Dr. Howard Luks, an orthopedic surgeon now 62, has spent 25 years observing this pattern in his patients. The functional decline he describes is not controversial among clinicians: patients lose the capacity to perform movements they have not practiced, and the loss is often invisible until a life situation demands the absent capacity. The question is not whether use-dependent atrophy occurs. The question is whether it can be reversed, and at what cost.

The evidence suggests a critical period. Intervention before severe atrophy is more effective than intervention after. But "before severe atrophy" is a moving target — what counts as "severe" at 45 is different from what counts as "severe" at 65, and the threshold at which intervention becomes futile rather than merely difficult is not well-mapped. This is **Ω_E** (empirically resolvable): longitudinal studies tracking intervention efficacy across age cohorts and atrophy severity levels would establish the window. The data exist in fragments but have not been synthesized into a usable decision framework.

What is clear: the narrowing is real, it is measurable, and it proceeds along the axis of neglect.

## The Dose-Response Problem

Public health guidelines recommend 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity. This is the floor, not the ceiling. It is the minimum required to avoid the worst outcomes, not the volume that optimizes survival.

The mortality data are unambiguous. Arem et al. (2015) and Mandsager et al. (2018) document a dose-response relationship that continues well beyond the public health minimum: mortality risk continues to decline as weekly aerobic volume increases from 150 minutes to 450 minutes, with benefits extending to 750 minutes per week in some cohorts. The highest-fitness individuals (top 2.3% of VO2 max) show an 80% reduction in all-cause mortality compared to the lowest quintile. Cardiorespiratory fitness predicts mortality more strongly than smoking, diabetes, or hypertension.

This creates a coordination problem. The public health minimum serves the population that cannot or will not do more — it sets a floor that is achievable for most adults and prevents the worst outcomes. But the dose-response curve does not flatten at 150 minutes. The mortality benefit continues to accrue at higher volumes, and the gap between the public health floor and the mortality-optimizing ceiling is not small.

Dr. Luks trains 600+ minutes per week. This is not performance optimization — he is explicit that he has abandoned intensity in favor of volume, trading the higher VO2 max he could achieve through harder training for the lower injury risk and faster recovery that volume-based training permits at 62. His goal is not to maximize fitness. His goal is to preserve the capacity to move across all planes and domains so that the life he wants to live remains available.

The question this raises is indexical: **for whom is 600 minutes per week good advice?**

For someone with Dr. Luks's resources — time flexibility, knowledge base, injury management access, intrinsic motivation — it is defensible. For someone working two jobs, managing chronic pain, or lacking safe places to train, it is not actionable and potentially harmful if presented as a standard. The dose-response relationship is real. The accessibility of the dose is not universal.

This is **Type C** — the tension dissolves when you specify the index. "How much should people exercise?" is three questions:

1. What is the minimum to avoid severe harm? (Public health floor: 150 min/week)
2. What volume optimizes mortality reduction? (Dose-response ceiling: 450-750 min/week)
3. What volume is achievable given this person's constraints? (Indexed to resources, injury history, competing demands)

The appearance of paradox — guidelines say 150, data say 600 — comes from treating these as a single question. They are not. The guidelines serve the population that needs a floor. The dose-response data serve the population that can access the ceiling. The gap between them is not a scientific failure. It is a resource distribution problem.

## The Comfort Gradient

Aging populations preferentially train comfortable movement patterns. This is observable: gym floors are full of people walking on treadmills, cycling, and performing sagittal-plane strength exercises. Lateral movement drills, rotational power work, and jump training are rare.

The mechanism behind this preference is underspecified. Three explanations are plausible:

**Psychological (risk aversion).** As injury consequences become more severe with age — longer recovery times, greater loss of function during recovery — the perceived risk of unfamiliar movements increases. Training what you are already good at feels safer than training what you have lost.

**Physiological (pain avoidance).** Movements that have not been practiced for years hurt. The discomfort is real — dormant movement patterns require neural and muscular adaptation that is genuinely uncomfortable in the early stages. Avoiding discomfort is not weakness; it is a rational response to immediate sensation.

**Structural (fitness industry programming).** The fitness industry optimizes for adherence, and adherence is highest when training feels achievable. Programming that emphasizes comfortable, repeatable movements retains clients better than programming that demands deliberate discomfort. The industry is not conspiring to harm people — it is responding to market incentives that reward comfort over comprehensiveness.

This is **Ω_C** (conceptually underspecified) — the mechanism determines the intervention point. If the driver is psychological, the solution is education and gradual exposure. If the driver is physiological, the solution is pain management and adaptation protocols. If the driver is structural, the solution is changing fitness industry incentives or creating alternative training contexts.

What is not ambiguous: the comfort gradient exists, and it produces a training distribution that preferentially maintains already-strong capacities while neglecting already-weak ones. The result is accelerated narrowing — the movement patterns that are disappearing are the ones least likely to be trained, because training them is uncomfortable.

Dr. Luks's framework is a direct response to this gradient. His training week includes deliberate discomfort: box jumps for sagittal-plane power, lateral shuffles for frontal-plane stability, rotational medicine ball throws for transverse-plane force generation. These are not the movements his body is good at. They are the movements his body is losing, and he is training them specifically because they are uncomfortable.

The question this raises is whether comfort-driven training is a personal failure or a coordination failure. If individuals are choosing comfort because they lack information about use-dependent atrophy, education solves it. If individuals are choosing comfort because the fitness industry does not program multiplanar work, the coordination problem is structural.

The evidence leans structural. Most aging adults are not aware that capacity loss is domain-specific, that sagittal-plane training does not preserve lateral or rotational capacity, and that the movements they are neglecting are the ones they will lose first. The information asymmetry is real, and it is not being corrected by the institutions that deliver fitness programming.

This is a **tangled rope** — the constraint is coordination-dependent, but the coordination is failing. The fitness industry could solve this by changing programming defaults. It does not, because the market rewards adherence over comprehensiveness, and comprehensive programming is harder to adhere to.

## The Extraction Question

Multiplanar capacity preservation, as Dr. Luks practices it, requires resources most aging populations do not have: time (600+ min/week), knowledge (understanding of movement planes and use-dependent atrophy), access (gym equipment, safe training space), and injury management (orthopedic expertise when things go wrong).

This raises the question: is multiplanar training a genuine capacity-preservation strategy, or is it a specialist-dependent intervention that extracts from the general population by creating a new fitness industry niche?

The structural analysis is ambiguous. From the powerless position — someone without time, knowledge, or access — multiplanar programming looks like a **snare**: a new set of requirements that create dependency on specialists and equipment while offering benefits that are inaccessible without those resources. From the institutional position — fitness industry professionals — it looks like a **rope**: a coordination problem with a clear solution (better programming) that benefits everyone if adopted.

The perspectival fracture is real. The constraint behaves differently depending on where you stand. For someone with Dr. Luks's resources, multiplanar training is a defensible strategy. For someone without those resources, it may be extractive — a new way to sell training services without addressing the accessibility barrier.

The key question is whether multiplanar capacity preservation can be achieved at lower volumes, with minimal equipment, and without specialist supervision. If yes, the constraint is a rope — a coordination problem that can be solved by better information distribution. If no, the constraint is a snare — a specialist-dependent intervention that benefits the fitness industry more than the population it claims to serve.

This is **Ω_E** (empirically resolvable): controlled trials comparing minimal-dose multiplanar protocols (e.g., 10 minutes per day of bodyweight lateral and rotational movements) against comprehensive programs would establish whether the benefits are accessible without high resource investment. The hypothesis is testable. The data do not yet exist.

What is defensible now: **if multiplanar capacity preservation requires specialist supervision, high time investment, or specialized equipment, then it is not a population-level solution.** It is a solution for the resource-rich, and presenting it as universal advice is coordination-washing — hiding the extraction behind the appearance of functional guidance.

## The Weight Check

Does this argument survive frame rotation?

From the powerless position (no time, no access, no knowledge), the argument looks like this: "Exercise more, in ways you don't know how to do, using resources you don't have, or accept that your functional capacity will narrow." This is not actionable. It is a description of constraint without a path forward.

From the institutional position (fitness industry, public health agencies), the argument looks like this: "Current programming is failing to address use-dependent atrophy because it optimizes for adherence over comprehensiveness. The coordination problem is solvable but requires changing industry incentives." This is actionable but threatens existing business models.

From the analytical position (researchers, clinicians), the argument looks like this: "The dose-response data and neuromuscular mechanisms are clear. The accessibility problem is real. The gap between what works and what is achievable is a resource distribution problem, not a scientific problem." This is accurate but does not solve the coordination failure.

The argument is **locally coherent** from the analytical position — it explains the mechanisms, documents the evidence, and names the coordination failure. It is **not position-invariant** — from the powerless position, it assigns blame without providing tools; from the institutional position, it threatens revenue models without offering a transition path.

This is the limit. The argument holds mechanistically but does not serve all positions equally. Acknowledging this is not hedging — it is honesty about what the framework can and cannot do.

## Open Questions (Ω)

**Ω_E: biological_vs_disuse_partition** — Empirically resolvable. What proportion of age-related capacity loss is irreversible biological aging versus reversible disuse atrophy? Partition requires longitudinal intervention studies tracking capacity recovery across age cohorts and atrophy severity levels.

**Ω_C: comfort_driven_training_mechanism** — Conceptually underspecified. Is comfort-seeking in aging populations driven by psychological risk aversion, physiological pain avoidance, or structural fitness industry programming? The mechanism determines whether the intervention point is education, pain management, or industry incentive restructuring.

**Ω_E: minimal_effective_dose_multiplanar** — Empirically resolvable. Can multiplanar capacity preservation be achieved at volumes accessible to general populations (e.g., 10-20 min/day bodyweight protocols), or does it require specialist supervision and high time investment? Controlled trials comparing minimal-dose protocols against comprehensive programs would establish accessibility.

**Ω_P: socioeconomic_access_mortality** — Structurally irresolvable / preference-dependent. Does the dose-response mortality curve reflect biology or socioeconomic confounding? If high-volume exercisers have systematically different resources, stress levels, and healthcare access, the mortality benefit may not be causal. This is a values question requiring stakeholder decision about acceptable evidence thresholds for population-level recommendations.
