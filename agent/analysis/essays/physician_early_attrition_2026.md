# The Extraction Gradient: How Physician Workforce Attrition Reveals Structural Coordination-Washing in American Healthcare

## I. Situation: The 9-Year Collapse

Between 2008 and 2024, the mean age at which U.S. physicians exit clinical practice fell from 57.1 years to 48.1 years—a 9-year compression representing approximately 15% of a typical clinical career. A 2024 national survey of 971 clinically inactive physicians, funded by the American Medical Association and published in *The Permanente Journal*, documents this accelerating departure. The study population completed residency between 2000 and 2022; 63.9% identified as women, and 11% never practiced after completing graduate medical education.

The top-cited departure reasons were "hassle factor" (44.7%) and "too stressful" (44.5%), followed by "increasingly unrealistic patient demands" (41.1%) and "lack of professional satisfaction" (38.4%). In 2008, the comparable survey ranked malpractice premiums third (24.4%); by 2024, malpractice concerns had dropped from the top four entirely. The locus of attrition shifted from external liability risk to internal organizational burden.

Women physicians exit clinical practice at a median of 9 years, compared to 12 years for men. Women cited childcare responsibilities as an exit reason at five times the rate of men (21.3% vs. 4.2%), and were more likely to report leaving due to health concerns (13.8% vs. 3.8%) and stress (31.7% vs. 12.9%). This gender asymmetry compounds as women approach 55% of medical school matriculants and 48.2% of active residents. The Association of American Medical Colleges projects a physician shortage of 13,500 to 86,000 by 2036; national attrition rates rose from 1.7% in 2010 to 3.1% in 2018.

The study's 1.6% response rate and retrospective design limit generalizability, but the temporal trend is consistent across data sources: physicians are leaving earlier, faster, and increasingly cite organizational structure rather than clinical practice as the reason.
