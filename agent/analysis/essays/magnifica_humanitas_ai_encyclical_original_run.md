# The Encyclical and the Engine: When Moral Teaching Meets Structural Diagnosis

## I. The Document's Claim and the Engine's Finding

Pope Leo XIV's *Magnifica Humanitas* arrives as the Catholic Church's first systematic engagement with artificial intelligence, synthesizing 135 years of social teaching into a framework for evaluating AI through the lens of human dignity, the common good, and solidarity. The encyclical presents itself as a moral compass for the digital age, calling humanity to choose between two biblical paradigms: the Tower of Babel (technocratic dominance, self-sufficient power) and the rebuilding of Jerusalem (shared responsibility, subsidiarity, communion under God).

The document's central structural claim is clear: AI and digital transformation can be guided toward the common good through the application of Catholic Social Doctrine's principles—dignity, subsidiarity, solidarity, the universal destination of goods, and social justice. The encyclical frames these principles as both timeless and adaptive, capable of addressing new technological realities while remaining faithful to the Gospel. It diagnoses a "technocratic paradigm" that reduces persons to data and efficiency metrics, and proposes an alternative: technology at the service of integral human development, regulated by just institutions, oriented toward the civilization of love.

But when this document's structural claims about AI's impact on work, governance, truth, and power are translated into formal constraint specifications and subjected to the Distributed Representation (DR) engine's analysis, a pattern emerges that the encyclical does not name and cannot see from within its own framework: **the engine consistently computes extraction where the document declares coordination**.

Across nine constraints derived from the encyclical's core axes—from work dignity and automation to private technological power, from digital slavery to AI weapons—the DR engine finds not Mountains (immutable physical limits) or Ropes (functional coordination mechanisms), but **Snares** (extractive traps) and **Tangled Ropes** (coordination mechanisms degrading toward extraction). More precisely, the engine finds that **the constraints' types depend on observer position**: what appears as benign coordination from institutional vantage points reveals itself as extraction when viewed from the powerless, and what the encyclical treats as unified moral challenges fragment into incompatible classifications across different scales of power and time.

This is not a minor diagnostic detail. The engine's findings challenge the encyclical's operational coherence at a structural level. If the document's framework cannot distinguish extraction masked as coordination—if it systematically misclassifies Snares as Ropes when viewed from positions of institutional power—then its prescriptions (transparency, accountability, subsidiarity-respecting governance) may inadvertently reinforce the very dynamics they claim to oppose.

Applied to the encyclical: the kernel (Gospel + CSD principles: dignity, common good, subsidiarity, solidarity, universal destination of goods) is treated as **fixed and perennial** (§2.1: "the unchanging core of revealed truths"). The interpretive accretion pattern allows the encyclical to **absorb the drift into interpretation** without recognizing it as structural.
