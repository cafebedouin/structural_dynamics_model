# On the Autonomy of Work

There is a man I knew who spent thirty years answering to others and called this, without apparent irony, a career. He was not unhappy. He had arranged his unhappiness into something manageable: a salary, a title, a routine. I have thought about him often, not with pity, but with the particular unease one feels before a mirror that is angled slightly wrong.

The question of work is, at bottom, a question about who holds the pen. One can labor enormously, even brilliantly, and still be dictating to another man's agenda. The difference between a craftsman and a clerk is not skill — both may be highly skilled — it is *authority over direction*. To own your assets, to act on your own principles, to call your own tune: these are not luxuries of the self-employed. They are the minimal conditions under which work can be said to belong to you at all. Without them, you are performing work for someone else's essay on their life, and your hours are footnotes.

I do not think this is a counsel of solitude, or of pride. Montaigne worked, after all — he administered estates, sat on councils, negotiated with kings — but he did so, he tells us, on his own terms, and when terms became intolerable he retired to his tower. The tower was not idleness. It was the reassertion of authorship. *Make an agenda for yourself and no one else*, he might have written, had he been more aphoristic and less digressive. Which is perhaps why he wasn't.

The second danger is subtler and, I think, more insidious than subordination: it is the trap of routine. We imagine that once we have secured our independence we are safe. But a man who has freed himself from a master and then falls into mechanical repetition has simply exchanged one form of servitude for another. Routine undercuts autonomy not because it is comfortable — comfort is fine — but because it operates without judgment. The autonomous worker must keep returning to the question: is this still *mine*, or have I merely inherited it from yesterday's self? The critical path should be kept small, independent, revisable. When dependencies proliferate, so do obligations that predate any morning's decision.

And yet — here I find myself turning, as Montaigne always did, back toward the personal — the goal is not merely freedom from constraint. Anyone who has worked entirely for themselves knows that pure self-direction without meaning is its own vacancy. The real inheritance of autonomous work is not the absence of a master but the presence of something harder to name: a gradual, earned understanding of what one actually is. *What is in work is the opportunity to find yourself.* This sounds, written out, like the sort of thing printed on motivational posters above open-plan offices, and yet I believe it is true, and the posters are simply the corpse of the idea. To make something — to make it on your own authority, answerable to your own judgment — is to leave evidence of yourself that you can then examine. Mastery is not achievement; it is accumulation of honest evidence. Meaning follows not from the subject matter but from the fact of genuine authorship.

I will not pretend the picture is clean. Autonomy of work is not equally available to everyone, and the advice to *create the job you want* lands differently depending on where you are standing when you read it. The aphorisms I have been turning over carry a faint smell of privilege — they presuppose enough slack in one's circumstances to exercise a choice. This is worth naming. To answer to no one, to retain complete control: these are partly achievements of character and partly achievements of luck and position, and conflating the two produces a moral accounting error.

But within whatever room one actually has — and most people, I think, have more room than they use — the principles hold. Own what you can own. Protect independence at the margins. Do not let routine become a deputy master. And do the work that, when you are finished, leaves you knowing slightly more about who you are than you did when you started.

That man I mentioned, the one with the career: I do not know whether he found himself in his work or not. Perhaps he did, in ways invisible to me. This essay is, I realize, mostly about not wanting to make the same mistake — which is, I suppose, Montaigne's point about essays in the first place.
