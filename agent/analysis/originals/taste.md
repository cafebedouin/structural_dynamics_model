# Everything Becomes Taste

A stranger is at your door with a clipboard, raising money for a cause that may well be good. You say: *I don't give money to people I don't know at my door.*

You decided that years ago, in a quiet room, with nobody's face in front of you. What happens on the step isn't a decision. It's a retrieval.

Almost everything worth knowing about how a life gets made is in the gap between those two rooms.

## Three instruments

There are three basic ways to change what a person does.

You can change **what they want**. Call it formation.
You can change **what an option costs**, and leave the choice with them. Call it pricing.
You can change **whether the choice reaches them at all**. Call it jurisdiction.

Steven Lukes cut power roughly this way in 1974 — decision-making, agenda-setting, and the shaping of preferences — and he thought the third the most insidious of the three, because it prevents the grievance from ever forming. People don't resent what they can't imagine wanting.

Lukes was writing about power over other people. The useful move is to turn it around. You run all three instruments on yourself, constantly, and mostly you are the only one running them. The door policy is jurisdiction: it takes the decision away from the version of you standing in a doorway with a human face six feet off, and hands it back to the version in the quiet room. That isn't a diminished case of political power. It's the same instrument with the operator and the target folded into one body.

## The arrow

Here is the part that makes this a model rather than a list. The instruments interconvert, and they interconvert in one direction. Everything runs downhill into formation.

Jon Elster named the road in 1983. He called it adaptive preference formation, after Aesop's fox — sour grapes, and its overlooked twin, sweet lemons. We downgrade what recedes and upgrade what arrives. Price something out of reach for long enough and people stop wanting it. Nobody experiences not flying private as a foreclosed option. They experience it as not caring about planes.

That's pricing arriving at formation by the slow road, and the arrival is total: the price is still there, and it has stopped being felt as a price.

Jurisdiction takes the same road on a longer clock. Keep a choice off the agenda for a generation and it stops being a choice withheld and becomes a thing nobody thought of. Lukes's third face isn't a different instrument from his second. It's what the second becomes if you leave it running.

Social norms are the fast lane. A norm begins as pricing — the cost is a look, a silence, being the person who did that — and it converts to formation faster and more reliably than money ever does. Which is why your prices still feel like prices decades on, and your norms feel like values. Same instrument. Different transit time.

One caution, because the arrow is often misread as fate: not every price runs downhill. Some people want at seventy exactly what they couldn't afford at twenty, and the wanting never adapted. The claim isn't that constraint always converts. It's that when it converts, it converts this way — and you don't get a notification.

## The mast

Elster gave us the other half, and the other half is why this isn't only a theory of oppression.

Ulysses at the mast is self-jurisdiction. He doesn't resist the sirens. He arranges, in advance, for resistance not to be required, because he knows the man who will be there is not the one who should decide. That's the door policy in worse weather.

And the mast isn't only for refusing. Consider an introvert who becomes an excellent reference librarian — genuinely good, better than the job requires. He isn't a shy man talking to a stranger. He's a librarian answering a question. The desk holds the jurisdiction, and what the desk decides is *yes*: it starts encounters his temperament would never have started. Same instrument as the door. Opposite direction. Both work by taking the decision away from the self who is standing there.

So the whole moral weight of the thing lands on one distinction: preferences your circumstances shaped without your consent, versus preferences you shaped on purpose. Much of Elster's project is the attempt to keep those apart. The distinction is real.

It is real at the moment you make the rule. Afterward, you can't see it.

## Why you can't see it

Both succeed the same way.

A precommitment that still costs you willpower hasn't worked yet. You're paying, every time, at exactly the moment the rule existed to route around. The rule is working when it's free. When the face at the door doesn't pull. When the cupboard doesn't contain a cookie — it contains a shelf.

And free is precisely what a sour grape feels like. Same interior: no pull, no counterfactual ache, nothing foreclosed. The fox is not lying. That's the part people miss. He isn't pretending the grapes are sour. He has come to find them sour, sincerely, and if you ask him he'll tell you he never much cared for that kind.

You cannot tell, from inside, whether you built a character or had one installed.

The obvious objection is that there's a residue. Adaptation leaves a faint defensiveness — *I didn't want it anyway* — while a chosen rule leaves endorsement: *I'm glad I don't want it.* Surely you'd feel the difference.

You wouldn't, and the reason is structural: the residue is a report, and reports are the thing under suspicion. Confabulation is generous. It will supply an origin story for any preference you hold, on demand, in a voice indistinguishable from memory. And it supplies endorsement preferentially, because endorsement feels better than apathy. The fox who *knows* he chose to stop wanting grapes is the fox the mechanism produces. Introspection isn't a sensor here. It's a press office.

## But the evidence isn't gone

Here is the correction that matters, and it's the thing I had wrong.

Introspection is destroyed. The evidence isn't. It moved.

A preference you installed and a preference that installed itself may be identical while the environment holds still — and diverge the moment it doesn't. Take someone who became vegetarian on purpose and someone who became vegetarian because they spent a decade somewhere meat was scarce and expensive. Ten years on, both report the same thing: don't want it, don't miss it, that's just who I am. Now put both of them somewhere meat is cheap, ordinary, everywhere, and unremarked. If the deliberate one holds more often, the distinction survived. It only stopped being visible from the inside and started being visible in what people do.

Which means the difference between a chosen life and an adapted one is not a fact about your experience. It's a fact about what you would do if the world handed the option back.

That is not knowable by reflection. Not ever, not by anyone, not with more honesty or better attention. It is knowable only by test.

## Re-exposure

So the practice isn't a nice supplement to the model. It's the only reading instrument that exists.

Periodically, go stand where the rule costs you. Not to break it — usually you'll keep it, and keeping it after standing there is worth something that keeping it by default is not. The point is that the answer to *is this mine* is stored outside you, in a room you've arranged not to enter, and there is no other copy.

This changes what the quiet means. A rule goes quiet when it works; painlessness is the goal, not a warning sign. Plenty of good rules go silent simply because they've been integrated, and nothing is wrong. But the quiet is also the loss of your feedback channel, and once it's gone you will never spontaneously want to revise — because wanting to revise is exactly what left. You can still revise. You just won't. That's an open loop wearing a closed loop's face, and it is the ordinary condition of a settled adult life.

## Match the clock

A rule works when it is harder to revise than the pressure it defends against, and easier to revise than your life. Call the gap the tempo margin. Both edges kill.

Too fast, and the rule revises under the pressure it was built for. That isn't a rule, it's an intention. *I'll just have one* has zero margin.

Too slow, and the instrument outlives its reason and grinds whoever's attached to it. Indissoluble marriage is the clean case: revision slower than a bad decade, defending against a pressure that runs for life. It needn't be exploitative — two equal people, equal stakes, equal footing — and it still destroys, because nobody holds the authority to change it. The margin is the entire difference between a mast and a coffin.

The rule sorts the instruments by the clock of the pressure:

**Seconds.** The doorstep, the end-of-day deadline, the last call, the checkout page. You will lose. The encounter is engineered to make the you who is standing there worse at deciding than the you who isn't. Use jurisdiction: don't be reachable. There is no willpower answer, and looking for one is the mistake. This runs at the perceptual layer too — putting the fruit where you'll see it and the biscuits where you won't isn't a price, and it isn't a change of heart. It's jurisdiction over what reaches your attention.

**Months.** The diet, the budget, the habit. Use pricing. Keep the choice, move the cost. You don't yet know whether the rule is right, and jurisdiction destroys the evidence by removing the case.

**A life.** Who you become. Only formation works here, and you can't aim it directly. You aim it by choosing rooms and roles, and then waiting. The librarian didn't decide to be outgoing. He took a desk.

## What's yours

If reflection can't audit your preferences, you need probes that don't require the interior.

The one that gets quoted is: *your real preferences are the expensive ones.* It's half right, and the wrong half matters. Cost is evidence **for** — a taste you've paid for has at least demonstrated it isn't a rationalization of your circumstances. But cheapness is not evidence against. Some things cost you nothing because your temperament happened to fit; the librarian loved reading before anyone paid him to sit at the desk. Absence of friction isn't adaptation. Sometimes it's just luck, and treating every easy pleasure as suspect is a bleak Calvinism with nothing behind it.

The sharper probe is timing. Adaptation is *defined* by a correlation: the new taste arrives exactly as the alternative leaves. So don't ask what a preference costs you. Ask when it showed up. A taste you had before the option closed is yours. A taste that arrived right on schedule — the year the money ran out, the year the body changed, the month you realized it wasn't going to happen — that one wants looking at. The fox's tell isn't that he doesn't want the grapes. It's that he stopped wanting them at four in the afternoon.

## Who this helps

Not everyone, and not equally, and the split isn't the one you'd expect.

At the fast end, this is nearly free and nearly sufficient. Scams, deadlines, sales scripts, defaults, doorsteps — a discrete adversary running a seconds-long instrument on you. Knowing the mechanism *is* most of the defense. It costs nothing, and it works regardless of what you have. That's reconnaissance, and reconnaissance is the cheapest thing there is.

At the slow end, it buys much less, by its own logic. If formation has already run, the evidence that it ran is precisely what's missing. You can still test — that's what re-exposure is for — but the test needs a world willing to hand the option back, and some worlds won't. Amartya Sen's cases are the hard ones: the tamed housewife, the hopeless beggar, people who have adjusted their wants down to their opportunities so thoroughly that no probe returns what was taken. They aren't deluded. They're standing upright on very little. Adaptation is traction, purchased with your wants, and it is the traction available to people who can't afford anything else.

So sort by what the rule closed. Some rules close one route. The doorstep is one channel among many; you can still give on Tuesday. Knowing it's one route tells you where the others are, and that is the whole of the scam case. Other rules close every route. Knowing that tells you only what you lost, precisely and permanently.

Be honest about what that second case is. This essay is itself a re-exposure probe. It hands you a reason to stop feeling fine about feeling fine. If you're standing on a road you could leave, that's worth having — the road stays legible *as* a road instead of settling into ground, and legibility is the one thing formation cannot survive. If you can't leave it, this is pain with a philosophy attached, and I'm not going to dress that up as empowerment. Awareness isn't power. It's the last thing that's free, and sometimes free is exactly what it's worth.

---

The rule takes the memory that it was a rule. That's the last thing it can take from the inside — and the inside was never where the answer was kept.

You can't remember whether you chose this. Nobody can. The machinery that would have told you is what the rule consumed on its way to working. But the record survives, intact, in the only form that doesn't answer to introspection: what you would do if the door opened again.

It's just that the record is kept in the one room you built the rule to keep you out of.
