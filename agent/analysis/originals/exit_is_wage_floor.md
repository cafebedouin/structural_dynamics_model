# Exit Is the Wage Floor

### The instrument that measured American farm wages was discontinued. The rule that depended on it was rewritten around a survey that does not sample farms. No one had to plan this.

**Draft 3 — 2026-07-22. UKE_W v2.2. Executes Ruling 1 (lower ε, emergent extraction) and Ruling 2 (kernel = instrument). Change log in metadata. Not for publication: see OPEN VERIFICATION ITEMS.**

---

In late August 2025, the USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service discontinued the Farm Labor Survey — the instrument that had for decades measured what American farmworkers were paid, and whose regional wage means set the Adverse Effect Wage Rate, the wage floor for the H-2A agricultural guest-worker visa. The stated rationale, approved by OMB, was that improvements to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program made OEWS the superior barometer for measuring farm wages.

On October 2, the Department of Labor issued an interim final rule — effective immediately, comments due December 1 — replacing the AEWR methodology with one built on OEWS. It added a lower "entry-level" wage tier set at the average wage of the lowest-paid third of workers in the occupation, and a downward "adverse compensation adjustment" deducting the estimated value of employer-provided housing from the hourly rate. The housing adjustment ran from $0.71 per hour in Puerto Rico to $3.18 in Hawaii. In California, Florida, and Washington, the adjusted federal wage floor now sits below the state minimum wage (CRS R48833, IF12408).

**OEWS does not sample farms.** By BLS's own technical documentation the survey excludes the majority of the agricultural sector, and it publishes no wage estimates for NAICS 111, Crop Production, or NAICS 112, Animal Production (CRS R47944, R48368). It covered farms and ranches from 2011 to 2013 under the Green Goods and Services program; BLS eliminated that program in 2013 under Budget Control Act sequestration, and a $1,137,000 request in the FY2024 President's Budget to restore farm and ranch collection was not funded.

What OEWS does cover in agriculture is NAICS 115, Support Activities — farm labor contractors, and only those filing unemployment insurance in their state. Farm Bureau's own read of the rule notes this skews accuracy where contractor presence is thin or agricultural UI is not required, and that the IFR commits DOL to work with USDA to add direct farm employers to the sample later. Agricultural UI reaches all but the smallest farms, but H-2A workers are themselves excluded from UI in many states (CRS R48368). And OEWS captures base hourly wages, not the piece-rate earnings that dominate harvest work, which the discontinued survey did capture.

So the wage floor for foreign farmworkers is now derived from a survey that does not sample farms, that samples the labor intermediaries instead, and that in many states cannot see the workers whose wage it sets.

Set this beside one of the largest interior immigration enforcement expansions in American history — $45 billion appropriated for ICE detention in July 2025, an internal ICE memorandum of February 13, 2026 targeting 92,600 beds, roughly 12,000 officers added, a detained population near 73,000 in January 2026 with more than 70 percent lacking any criminal conviction — and the two policies point opposite ways. One removes agricultural workers. The other expands and cheapens the legal channel replacing them. DOL's own analysis projects more than $1.7 billion moving from H-2A workers to their employers in 2026 and roughly $24 billion over ten years, as the program grows past 500,000 jobs, which the Department expects.

**Here is the claim, and it is smaller than it looks.** No one had to coordinate any of this. NASS had an expensive survey with real methodological problems. DOL needed a defensible replacement and reached for the largest wage survey the federal government runs. Agricultural employers, facing three years of AEWR increases, lobbied for relief and won some. DHS enforces immigration law under separate authority for separate reasons. Every one of those decisions is defensible on its own terms, and their composition produces a $24 billion transfer that none of them chose.

That is the finding, not a concession to critics. **A statutory protection can be dismantled by the ordinary operation of agencies each behaving reasonably, and the result is worse than a conspiracy because it has no author to remove.**

---

## What actually broke

The Immigration and Nationality Act bars admitting H-2A workers on terms that adversely affect the wages of similarly employed US workers. That guarantee is intact. Nothing repealed it, and this essay does not claim otherwise.

What changed is that the guarantee is now **unprovable in court.**

An adverse-effect test is a comparison. It requires a measurement of what similarly employed US farmworkers earn. The Farm Labor Survey was that measurement — sector-specific, annual, public, and statutorily wired into the AEWR formula. Its replacement is a survey that by design does not sample the establishments where the comparison population works. A litigant asserting adverse effect must now establish it against a federal wage series that cannot see the relevant employers, in a sector where the pay form most workers are on is outside the series' scope.

This is not a metaphor about legislation. It is a live administrative-law problem with a named remedy path. Under *Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass'n v. State Farm*, agency action is arbitrary and capricious where the agency has entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem. The coverage gap was not obscure: it is stated in BLS's own survey documentation, in two Congressional Research Service reports predating the rule, in a failed FY2024 budget request to fix it, and — implicitly but unmistakably — in the IFR's own commitment to eventually add farms to the sample, which concedes they are not in it. Whether that record supports the substitution is exactly the question an APA challenge asks, and **it is a question about the adequacy of reasoning, not about anyone's motive.** Arbitrary-and-capricious review does not require proving intent. This is why the low-intent reading is the stronger one legally as well as analytically: it fits the standard that would actually be applied.

The general form of this, offered as conjecture and not as a finding: wherever a statutory protection is defined against a measured baseline, discontinuing the measurement suspends the protection without touching the statute. I have no systematic evidence that this pattern recurs, and readers should treat the generalization as a hypothesis I have not tested.

## What changes when the channel changes

An unauthorized farmworker has almost no rights and one asset: they can leave a bad employer on Tuesday and work for a different one on Wednesday. The employer cannot report them without exposing themselves and cannot follow them. It is a thin protection, routinely defeated by wage theft and isolation. It is also why unauthorized workers historically cluster around whichever operations in a county pay best.

An H-2A worker's lawful presence is attached to a specific certified employer. Quitting is not changing jobs; it is falling out of status.

**How much this actually binds is the essay's largest unknown, and I want it stated here rather than buried.** If a substantial share of H-2A workers leave certified employment before contract end and find other work, the exit constraint is formal rather than practical and this section is weaker than it reads. Neither DOL nor DHS publishes separation rates, and I have not found a credible estimate. What follows is a mechanism I can describe, not a magnitude I can defend.

The mechanism is ordinary monopsony and requires no employer to behave badly. Employer power here does not come from the wage statute; it comes from the worker's inability to reach an alternative employer without losing status. Search frictions generate monopsony rents in low-wage labor markets generally. In this market the friction is written into the visa.

The historical rhyme is worth one paragraph and no more. When Black agricultural workers began leaving the American South in large numbers, Southern legislatures did not cut wages — that would have accelerated departure. They constrained mobility: taxing labor recruiters, and separately criminalizing a worker's own breach of contract after taking an advance, which the Supreme Court eventually struck down as peonage. The target was not the wage. It was the exit. *(Case citations remain OPEN and unverified — see below. If they do not check out, cut the paragraph; nothing downstream depends on it.)*

## The best argument for the current arrangement

The strongest defense of employer-tied status is a contracting argument, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a dismissal.

An H-2A employer sinks recruitment fees, visa costs, transport, and housing before the worker produces anything. Under full portability, a competitor can hire that worker on day two and free-ride on the first employer's investment. Penalties for early departure are effectively unenforceable against workers with no attachable assets. Employer-tied status solves this: a performance bond the state enforces, with loss of status as the penalty. On these terms it is not incompetence but a mechanism-design response to a real hold-up problem, and it is the argument the industry makes when it is being candid.

Grant it entirely. It still does not deliver employer-tied status, for two reasons.

**A financing problem has financing solutions.** If the barrier is a sunk recruitment cost, transfer the cost rather than binding the person. A certified recruitment-cost schedule, reimbursed pro rata by a receiving employer when a worker transfers mid-season, resolves the hold-up without anyone holding anyone's residency. This is how costly-training labor markets handle mobility generally. It is a rulemaking-scale design problem.

**And the argument proves too much.** The H-1B visa is portable. Employers sink comparable per-worker costs and hire anyway, because output exceeds recruitment cost by enough to absorb departure risk. The hold-up defense therefore concedes something its proponents do not intend: that the arrangement only pencils if the worker cannot leave. That is a statement about how thin the margin is at the offered wage, not about the impossibility of contracting.

## What would show this is wrong

Standard labor economics predicts that removing a large share of a sector's workforce raises the relative wage of those remaining. This essay predicts it will not, because the replacements have no exit-based bargaining position. Early data leans that way — a Wall Street Journal analysis of Labor Department figures found hourly earnings across 41 immigrant-reliant industries rising 3.5 percent year-over-year in February 2026 against 3.8 percent for all workers — but one month is not a test.

**And a directional wage forecast cannot settle it.** A competing account predicts the same null: removing workers also removes their consumption, so demand falls with supply. Both stories produce flat wages.

**What separates them is a discontinuity at state lines.** If the mechanism runs through a collapsed federal floor, effects concentrate where the new floor binds — and it binds differently across borders. In California, Florida, and Washington the adjusted AEWR sits below the state minimum, so the state minimum binds and the federal cut does not fully pass through; in neighboring states without a higher minimum, it does. A demand collapse is a national shock and produces no border effect. **If H-2A offered wages for 2026–2028 show no discontinuity between binding-minimum and non-binding states, the wage-floor mechanism is not doing the work claimed here.** Testable from OFLC disclosure data, which reports offered wages by worksite state. F9/F10 below.

**Second falsifier.** If H-2A certifications flatten while immigrant-reliant sector employment also falls sharply, enforcement is functioning as straightforward supply reduction and the channel-conversion account is decoration. There is a live signal in that direction: employment in those 41 industries fell 90,400 year-over-year in February 2026 while overall employment grew, with automation absorbing part of the work.

**Third, and it bears on the essay's central section.** If the OEWS coverage gap biases the AEWR neutrally or upward, or if the IFR's commitment to add farm employers closes it on schedule, then the instrument substitution is an ordinary regulatory transition and the argument reduces to a complaint about two discretionary parameter choices. I have assumed downward bias — farm labor contractors compete on price and are the low-wage segment of the sector — and that assumption is not established.

## Why agriculture and care work, and not software

*Interpretive model, not measurement.* "Separability" is a category imposed on the evidence to organize it, in the tradition of Alan Blinder's offshorability index and the trade-in-tasks literature. It is not derived from data, is not evidence, and earns its place only through the dated predictions below.

Some work separates from the body and ships: software, back-office processing, radiology reads. When the wage gap is large and the task separable, the work moves and the worker stays home. Nobody calls that immigration and nobody polices it. Some work does not separate — a harvest, a night shift on a dementia ward, a roof. Automation changes the tasks; it has not made physical presence separable the way a radiology read is.

These are also the sectors where productivity growth is slowest: Baumol cost-disease sectors, where costs rise because wages must track economy-wide productivity gains the sector never achieves. Which gives the compact statement: **if offshoring is Baumol arbitrage in tradable tasks, migration is Baumol arbitrage in non-tradable ones.** Economically the same operation; politically opposite, because offshoring suppresses the wage bill in another jurisdiction and migration does it where the voter lives.

Aging societies expand the Baumol sector on a demographic schedule no election alters. Paying market-clearing wages for care would mean absorbing cost-disease prices into public budgets and household accounts, which is harder than letting the wage settle low. You cannot offshore a bedpan. The pressure resolves somewhere, and the AEWR rule shows one place it has: not in fewer workers, but in workers with less ability to refuse.

### Tier 2 — Inferences

- **The two AEWR parameter changes were discretionary.** Logically necessary: losing a data source compels a new source, not a new tier structure or a housing deduction.
- **The replacement instrument does not sample the population the statute's comparison requires.** Follows from OEWS scope documentation plus UI exclusion of H-2A workers.
- **The INA adverse-effect test is therefore substantially harder to establish evidentially than before.** Inference, not a legal conclusion; a court could hold the statutory guarantee independently provable by other means.
- **The composition of independently motivated decisions produced the transfer.** No document establishes coordination and none is claimed; this is a statement about outcome, not process.
- **Employer-tied status raises the price of refusing a deal.** Follows from H-2A's statutory design. Magnitude unestablished.

### Tier 3 — Structural Hypotheses

- **H1: Enforcement in non-offshorable sectors functions as channel conversion, not supply reduction.** → Tier 2 if H-2A volume rises materially while sector employment holds. Falsified if both fall together.
- **H2: The wage effect runs through the collapsed floor and exit constraint, not through supply reduction.** Falsified by crop wage growth exceeding economy-wide growth, or by absence of a state-line discontinuity.
- **H5 (new, replacing the intent hypothesis): The OEWS substitution fails arbitrary-and-capricious review because the administrative record does not address the coverage gap.** → Tier 2 on a district court holding to that effect. Falsified by the rule being upheld on the merits, or by a holding that instrument adequacy is severable from statutory compliance.
- **H3: Non-offshorable care work is the next sector to receive an employer-tied channel.** Falsified if care shortages are met by automation, domestic wage increases, or portable visas.
- **H4: The detention build-out locks in through owned and long-term-financed capacity rather than leased capacity.** Falsified by a sharp drawdown after January 2029.

## Alternative Explanations Considered

*Note: Draft 2 listed "two unrelated agency actions" and "ordinary interest-group politics" as separate rivals. They are one account, and under Ruling 1 it is no longer a rival — it is this essay's own frame. The genuine rivals are below.*

- **OEWS is the better instrument.** DOL's position, and the serious one. OEWS offers finer geographic and occupational granularity, higher frequency, larger sample, and excludes overtime distortions that inflated some regional AEWRs; the IFR commits to adding farm employers. If the coverage gap closes and the bias proves neutral, this essay's central section reduces to a complaint about two parameter choices. **This is the rival to beat, and it is not beaten yet** — see the third falsifier.
- **Employer-tied status solves a genuine hold-up problem.** Addressed in-body; conceded as valid and answered on financing grounds and the H-1B comparison.
- **Demand fell with supply.** Predicts the same null on wages. Separated by the state-line discontinuity test, not by directional forecasts.
- **Automation absorbed the work.** Live and partially supported by the February 2026 employment data. If dominant, H1 fails.

## Institutional Actions Required

1. **Restore a sector-specific farm wage measurement.** Fund OEWS farm and ranch collection — the FY2024 request was $1,137,000, roughly one tenth of one percent of the annual transfer DOL projects — or reinstate the Farm Labor Survey. *USDA/BLS via appropriation.* Until an instrument samples farms, the INA's adverse-effect standard cannot be established by anyone, in either direction. That cuts both ways: employers cannot prove no adverse effect either.
2. **Visa portability within sector, with certified recruitment-cost transfer.** Permit H-2A and H-2B holders to move to any certified employer in sector without loss of status; require the receiving employer to reimburse documented recruitment and transport costs pro rata for the unexpired term. *Congress, or joint DHS/DOL rulemaking.* Answers the hold-up objection directly; changes nothing about admission numbers. It will be litigated and is CRA-exposed — the demand is structurally minimal, not politically easy.
3. **GAO review of the interim final rule's administrative record.** Specifically whether the record addresses OEWS's exclusion of farm establishments, and whether a federal wage floor falling below a state minimum is consistent with the INA's adverse-effect bar. *House Education & Workforce or Senate HELP; 12 months.*
4. **Publish H-2A separation data** — rates and dispositions of workers leaving certified employment before contract end, by employer and state, from existing certification and petition records. *DOL ETA/WHD and DHS.* This is the number that would confirm or refute the essay's own mechanism.
5. **Statutory publication of ICE detention composition** — per-facility monthly conviction status, contract and ownership type, financing structure, per-diem rate. *DHS, codified.*

## Unresolved Questions

**At what rate do H-2A workers leave certified employment before contract end, and where do they go?** The fulcrum. If separations are common, the exit constraint is formal rather than practical and the mechanism section overstates. DOL and DHS hold the records — certification files, I-129 petitions, wage-and-hour dispositions, overstay analytics. Not withheld for any stated reason; simply not compiled.

**Does OEWS's exclusion of farm establishments bias the computed AEWR downward, or is the direction indeterminate?** The essay assumes downward on the reasoning that farm labor contractors are the low-wage segment. That is an assumption. The test is a comparison of AEWR trajectories under FLS and OEWS for the same commodity and region cells over the years both were in the field, controlling for the IFR's phased inclusion of farm employers. **If the bias is neutral or closing, the coverage-neutral reading wins and this essay's central section reduces to a parameter complaint.**

**Is the operative kernel the statutory text or the instrument?** This essay takes the second position: an adverse-effect test without a measurement of the comparison population is not enforceable as written. A narrower textualist reading holds the guarantee stands independent of any instrument, with adequacy a severable administrative-law question. An APA merits ruling would settle it, which is why F11 scores the reasoning and not just the outcome.

**Who decided to discontinue the Farm Labor Survey, on what analysis, and when relative to the rulemaking?** The sequence is documented; the decision record is not. This question is not asked to establish intent — under this draft's frame, intent is not required — but because the timing bears on whether the resulting gap was foreseeable, which is exactly what arbitrary-and-capricious review examines.

---

## Ending

The minimal demand is a survey.

Not fewer visas or more. Not amnesty. Not enforcement restraint. An instrument that measures what American farmwork pays, in the sector where it is paid, sampling the farms that pay it. The last request to build one was $1,137,000 and went unfunded. The rule written in its absence moves, by the Department's own estimate, $24 billion over ten years.

Note what this costs the other side: nothing. An accurate instrument is as available to growers arguing that no adverse effect exists as to workers arguing that one does. A statute that forbids adverse effects on American wages, enforced against a measurement that cannot see American farms, protects no one and proves nothing — in either direction.

Which is the question for anyone defending the arrangement, and it is not about immigration at all. Are you willing to fund the thermometer?
