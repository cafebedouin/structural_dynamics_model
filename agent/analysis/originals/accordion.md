Your instinct is right, and the instrument is even younger than you'd guess: **the accordion was patented in 1829.** Cyrill Demian, an Armenian-Viennese organ builder, registered a small bellows box whose buttons each sounded a whole *chord* — hence "Akkord," hence accordion. It was invented as an accompaniment machine, a one-man rhythm section.

What made it possible was the free reed, a tongue of metal vibrating freely in a slot. That part *is* ancient — but Chinese, not Levantine. The sheng mouth organ goes back millennia, arrived in European hands in the 1700s, and set off a burst of tinkering in the 1820s that produced the harmonica, the concertina (patented the same year by Charles Wheatstone, of Wheatstone bridge fame), the harmonium, and the accordion, all within a decade or so.

**Why it conquered the world:** before amplification, one person with an accordion could fill a dance hall. Melody in the right hand, bass and chords in the left, loud enough for outdoors, robust enough for a wagon, and — once Germany and Italy industrialized production — cheap. Castelfidardo in Italy and Trossingen in Germany (Hohner) pumped out millions.

Those millions rode the great 19th-century migrations and trade routes, and the instrument was adopted as *native* almost everywhere it landed: tango in Argentina (via the German bandoneon), vallenato in Colombia, forró in Brazil, norteño in Texas and northern Mexico via Czech and German settlers, Cajun and zydeco in Louisiana, klezmer, Russian bayan, Alpine folk, Boer music in South Africa, early Congolese pop. An instrument from nowhere in particular became the national sound of a dozen places.

**The American peak** ran roughly 1910 to 1955 — vaudeville stars, then radio, then a genuine industry of neighborhood accordion schools. In the postwar years it was plausibly the best-selling instrument in the country, the piano lessons of its day.

**And then the collapse**, which is the part your radio program was circling. Electric amplification destroyed the accordion's core advantage: you no longer needed a self-contained loud instrument, and a guitar could now be louder than anything. Rock also made instruments into identity markers, and the accordion had been assigned to parents, polka, and Lawrence Welk. The hybrids you heard about were the industry trying to survive by bolting on electronics — and here's the lovely irony: **Farfisa** was a consortium of Castelfidardo accordion factories that pivoted to combo organs, and their cheesy little keyboards became the sound of 1960s garage rock. The accordion trade built the instrument that buried it.

It never actually died, though. It stayed central in Latin America and Eastern Europe, gained a serious concert repertoire, and came back in the West as a signifier of grit and old-worldness — Piazzolla, the Pogues, Tom Waits, Los Lobos, Arcade Fire. Roughly 200 years: novelty, commodity, global folk instrument, middle-class ambition, punchline, and now a slightly cool ghost.
