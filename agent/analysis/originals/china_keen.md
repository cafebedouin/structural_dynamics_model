Gemma:

You’ve pinpointed the exact friction points between heterodox economic theories (like Steve Keen’s) and the "deleveraging" reality China currently faces. The "bear case" you're outlining is essentially the argument that China is hitting a wall where the marginal productivity of capital ($MPK$) has turned negative.

Here is a breakdown of how those contradictions play out when we look past the transcript’s optimism:

### 1. The "Soviet Model" & Investment-Led Growth
You’re right to compare this to the late-stage Soviet Union. When investment is directed by a centralized vision (Xi’s "New Quality Productive Forces"), you often get misallocation. 
* The Conflict: Keen argues that investment creates demand. However, if you build a bridge to a city that is shrinking due to demographics, that investment doesn't "pay for itself" through future productivity. It becomes a "sunk cost" that still carries debt on the books.

### 2. The Casino vs. The Command
The Western "casino" (ZIRP-era venture capital and speculation) is chaotic, but it functions as a discovery mechanism. It finds winners like NVIDIA or OpenAI. 
* The Trade-off: By suppressed "speculation," the CCP also suppresses the "animal spirits" required for true, bottom-up innovation. China is essentially betting that they can *direct* innovation (EVs, Fusion, AI) more efficiently than a market can. History suggests this works for "catch-up" growth but fails at the "frontier."

### 3. Real Estate and State Banks
The inventory issue is the "white elephant" in the room. 
* The Reality: State banks can keep developers solvent on paper, but they cannot manufacture demand. With a shrinking population, China has effectively "pre-built" housing for a generation that won't exist. This leads to the "LGFV (Local Government Financing Vehicle) trap," where local debt is so high that they can no longer provide basic services, let alone "experiment" with new policies.

### 4. The Reserve Currency Paradox
Your point on the Triffin Dilemma is the strongest counter-argument to the Yuan’s rise. 
* The Barrier: To be a reserve currency, China would have to run trade deficits to provide the world with Yuan. China’s entire political identity is currently built on running surpluses to maintain employment. They want the prestige of a reserve currency without the "burden" of being the global consumer of last resort.

### 5. Entrepreneurial Local Government
The "uniqueness" of the Chinese model isn't the idea of local governance, but the incentive structure. 
* The Distinction: In a federalist system like the US, a state's "success" is varied (tax base, population growth). In China, for decades, it was a "Promotion Tournament" based purely on GDP targets. This led to the systemic overbuilding you mentioned—if every mayor needs to hit 6% growth to get promoted, every mayor builds a redundant industrial park.

### 6. Transition & "Xi-ism" vs. Reform
The critique here is that Xi has prioritized Security over Economy. 
* The CCP’s Dilemma: Re-implementing Deng-style reforms would require giving up control (e.g., privatizing state assets, allowing capital flight). If the CCP believes that "too much reform" led to the fall of the USSR, they will likely choose stagnation over a loss of central grip, even if it leads to the "middle-income trap."

### 7. Monetary Sovereignty & Hyperinflation
Keen’s MMT-adjacent argument is often misunderstood as "printing money is free." 
* The Nuance: He argues that a sovereign issuer can't "go bankrupt" in its own currency. However, as you noted, hyperinflation is the hard limit. If China prints Yuan to bail out every failing developer and local government while productivity is falling, the Yuan’s purchasing power (both domestic and international) will eventually collapse. 

### 8. Capacity & Global Trade Flows
This is the "Endgame" scenario. China has built enough capacity to supply the whole world with EVs and solar panels, but the world (EU, US, India) is putting up protectionist walls.
