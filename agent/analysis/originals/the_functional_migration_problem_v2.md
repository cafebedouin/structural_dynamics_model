# The Functional Migration Problem

A concept like "sunk cost fallacy" does real work in a quiet room. Someone recognizes they're persisting with a dead project because of what they've already invested, not because of what they'll gain. The recognition restructures their decision. The concept operated as a tool—it carved a distinction the person couldn't previously see, and seeing it changed what they did next.

The same concept, posted as a tweet explaining why you should leave your partner, performs a different operation entirely. The definition is identical. The inferential structure is intact. But the concept is no longer doing analytical work for the person using it. It is doing *social* work: signaling membership in a group that values rationality, providing a prefabricated justification for a decision already made, or—most commonly—establishing the speaker as someone who sees through error that others can't. The concept hasn't been dumbed down. It's been repurposed.

Why does the same concept do different work in different contexts? The answer is not that public discourse dumbs things down—though it does. The answer is that audiences and incentives change what concepts are *for*.

This is worth distinguishing from the simpler explanation, which is that public discourse just corrupts concepts—strips nuance, flattens distinctions, turns precise tools into blunt instruments. That does happen. "Gaslighting" has been stretched so far from its clinical meaning that it now covers any disagreement the speaker finds frustrating. But content degradation is a different failure mode from what's happening when the concept stays sharp and the function still shifts. A perfectly accurate deployment of "cognitive dissonance" can still function primarily as a tribal marker if its purpose in context is to signal that the speaker belongs to the rationalist-adjacent, psychologically literate in-group. The precision is preserved. The function has migrated.

This essay is about that second phenomenon—not content degradation, but functional migration. The mechanism is audience and incentive, not imprecision.

The mechanism is straightforward. Analytical concepts work on problems. Problems are specific, situated, and belong to the person holding them. When a concept migrates into public discourse, it acquires two things it didn't have before: an audience and an incentive structure. The audience means the concept must now perform for observers, not just solve problems for the user. The incentive structure means some performances get rewarded—with engagement, status, group belonging—and others don't. The performances that get rewarded are almost never the quiet, difficult, internally disruptive ones. They're the ones that look good from the outside: the confident diagnosis, the clean narrative, the moment of public clarity that demonstrates the speaker's sophistication.

This is not a claim about individual hypocrisy. The people deploying concepts as tribal markers are usually unaware of the shift. They believe they're doing analysis. The functional migration is structural, not intentional—it follows from the changed context the way water follows gravity. Put an audience in the room and the concept starts performing. Attach rewards to certain performances and those performances proliferate. No one has to decide to corrupt anything.

The counterargument that deserves weight here is that tribal marking *is itself a legitimate function* and the framing of "migration" smuggles in a hierarchy where individual analysis is the real use and social signaling is the degraded one. This objection has weight. Groups genuinely coordinate through shared conceptual vocabulary. Knowing that someone in your circle understands "emotional labor" or "principal-agent problem" tells you something real about how they model the world, which predicts how they'll behave in shared projects. The social function isn't parasitic on the analytical one—it may be the older and more fundamental function, with solitary analysis as the derivative case. From this position, complaining about functional migration looks like a philosopher's category error: mistaking your preferred use for the natural one.

That objection narrows the claim but doesn't eliminate it. The distinction that survives is between social signaling that tracks analytical competence and social signaling that has decoupled from it. When shared vocabulary reliably indicates competence—this person can apply the concept, not just name it—the social and analytical functions reinforce each other. When the signal decouples from the competence—when naming the concept *is* the demonstration, and applying it is neither expected nor rewarded—the social function has consumed the analytical one. The concept is now a badge, not a tool.

That difference is observable. You can test it by asking the speaker to apply the concept to a case that cuts against their interests. A tool works in any direction. A badge only points outward. Intermediate test: Can you apply this concept to a case that's costly to you, even if you're not wrong? If "sunk cost fallacy" only diagnoses your friend's relationship and never your PhD, the concept has migrated.

What follows from this, if it's right, depends on who you are and what you're trying to do. If you're someone who uses concepts as working tools for your own thinking, the practical implication is simple: be suspicious of any concept you've never applied against yourself. The moment a concept only diagnoses other people's errors, it has likely completed the migration in your own hands without your noticing. This is useful advice for people with analytical habits and the self-awareness to audit them—which is to say, it serves exactly the population most likely to read an essay like this one. It does not help the person who was never using the concept analytically in the first place, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of universalized prescription this observation is designed to catch.

If you're operating in public discourse—writing, teaching, posting—the implication is more uncomfortable: you cannot prevent functional migration by being more precise. Precision doesn't inoculate a concept against repurposing because the mechanism isn't imprecision. The mechanism is audience and incentive. You can be exquisitely precise and the concept will still be adopted as a badge if the context rewards badge-wearing. This does not mean precision is worthless. It means precision solves a different problem than the one most people think it solves.

The honest residue is that there may be no individual-level fix for a structural-level phenomenon. Concepts migrate because discourse environments select for social function over analytical function whenever audiences and incentives are present. Changing this would require changing the incentive structure of public discourse itself, which is not a recommendation anyone can act on over breakfast. What remains actionable is smaller: noticing when a concept has stopped doing work and started doing display, in your own use, today. That's not a solution to the structural problem. It's the only part of the structural problem that fits in your hands.

This essay is not immune to its own analysis. Shared publicly, "functional migration" will itself migrate—from analytical tool to sophistication signal. The concept cannot prevent its own repurposing. What remains is whether it does useful work *before* that happens, in the hands of someone who needs it.

### Open Questions (Ω)

**Ω1: Decoupling threshold** (empirical)—At what point does social signaling decouple from analytical competence? Is there a measurable tipping point in concept adoption curves, or is it continuous?

**Ω2: Directionality** (underspecified)—Does the analytical function emerge from the social one historically (concepts as group coordination tools first, individual tools second), or vice versa? The answer may be concept-dependent rather than universal.

**Ω3: Medium effects** (empirical)—Does the speed of functional migration vary by medium (oral conversation vs. print vs. social media)? Social media's incentive structure is unusually legible, but the phenomenon likely predates it.
