A live case that the framework illuminates sharply is the ongoing struggle to define and govern “AI alignment,” where the popular debate treats alignment as a single technical goal that responsible labs are pursuing. The framework reveals instead a structurally inevitable drift into a *diffuse reconstruction*, and it predicts why efforts to fix that drift with hard regulatory definitions will likely fail in a specific, brittle way.

### The popular understanding
In tech journalism, policy circles, and much of the AI safety community, “AI alignment” is spoken of as if it were a well‑defined target. The story goes: early researchers identified the problem (e.g., Amodei et al.’s *Concrete Problems in AI Safety*, 2016, or Bostrom’s *Superintelligence*), the major labs have alignment teams, and now the world is adding governance – the EU AI Act, the Bletchley Park declaration, the Frontier AI Safety Commitments. Disagreements are framed as honest technical disputes or as a lack of coordination. The solution is assumed to be clearer definitions, better metrics, and binding regulation.

### What the commitment‑systems framework sees
Use the four diagnostic questions from the document.

1. **Kernel and authority structure**  
   The early kernel is a loosely stitched set of texts and problem formulations: corrigibility, value loading, iterated amplification, reward misspecification. There is no single canonical document; instead, a handful of foundational papers and mission statements (OpenAI’s charter, DeepMind’s “safe and beneficial” framing, the 2021 Stanford alignment survey) serve as the reference points that participants point to when asked what alignment is.  
   The authority structure is radically distributed: individual labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind), leading researchers, grant‑making bodies, government AI safety institutes, and now international summits all claim a share. None has final interpretive authority, and each has deep extraction stakes – funding, reputation, regulatory influence – in its own reading remaining the “correct” one.

2. **Is drift occurring, and is it acknowledged?**  
   Enormous drift has occurred. The operational meaning of alignment in 2024–2026 is almost entirely *RLHF‑based instruction following and harmlessness tuning*, plus some mechanistic interpretability and automated red‑teaming. This is light‑years from the original problem of aligning a potentially superhuman agent to complex, unfolding human values. Yet no lab says, “We’ve redefined the goal; the original kernel no longer governs.” Instead, everyone insists the old language still describes what they are doing. The drift is unacknowledged in official terms, even as researchers privately note the gap.

3. **Which pattern is the system in?**  
   This is a textbook **diffuse reconstruction**. The kernel is underspecified (“alignment,” “safety,” “beneficial”), there is no centralized authority, and numerous mutually incompatible readings compete – some emphasizing corrigibility, others emphasizing current harm reduction, others emphasizing long‑term existential risk, others embedding alignment into specific regulatory compliance checklists. The pattern persists indefinitely but lacks operational coherence. It serves strategic purposes: each actor can claim fidelity to the kernel while pursuing a different agenda, and no one can be pinned down because no one owns the definitive interpretation.

4. **Who has standing to acknowledge drift, and do they benefit from pretending it doesn’t exist?**  
   The labs and the AI safety institutes have the most standing, but acknowledging drift would undermine their authority. If OpenAI admitted that “superalignment” (which it announced in 2023 and then rapidly restructured) was not the same project as the earlier alignment agenda, it would have to explain why its safety story keeps changing. If a regulator admitted that the operational definition of “safe AI” is moving as fast as the technology, the regulatory kernel would lose its claim to be a stable reference point. So the system evolves elaborate ways of not admitting drift – publishing new “alignment frameworks” that silently shift the goalposts, issuing joint statements that use the word “alignment” without defining it, and treating the proliferation of meanings as a sign of healthy debate rather than a structural inability to commit.

### The failure prediction the framework adds
The popular understanding predicts that more precise regulation (the EU’s risk categories, binding codes of practice) will converge operational practice back to a stable kernel. The framework predicts the opposite for any attempt that forces a **fixed, unrevisable kernel** without an interpretive‑accretion layer.

If, say, a treaty solidifies a definition of “aligned AI” based on 2025 understanding, the result will be an *anchored fixity without interpretive accretion*. The kernel will be formalized and unchangeable, and the authority structure (international oversight bodies) will draw its legitimacy precisely from the kernel’s permanence. But AI capabilities and deployment contexts will continue to change much faster than the treaty amendment process. Drift denial will become the source of authority rather than a side effect. The framework then unambiguously predicts **brittle‑fixity failure**: accumulating gap, increasingly fictional compliance, and eventual catastrophic breakdown when an AI system that satisfies the letter of the frozen kernel causes harm that the kernel was never designed to capture. The Spartan parallel (breakdown at Leuctra) is exactly the shape: a rigid system that works until the environment shifts enough, then collapses suddenly.

Meanwhile, those who benefit from the ambiguity – labs that can claim alignment while chasing commercial deployment, governments that can claim they are regulating while outsourcing interpretation to industry – will resist any move that makes the kernel precise enough to be falsified. The framework names this not as bad faith but as a structural effect of the authority structure’s extraction stakes.

### Why this explanation is invisible to the popular framing
The standard conversation is a moralized one: “Companies must align their AI” versus “Alignment is a moving target that stifles innovation.” It cannot see that the very inability to fix what alignment means is *the system working as a diffuse reconstruction*, and that the move to hard regulation repeats the anchored‑fixity pattern that has broken civilizational‑scale systems before. The commitment‑systems framework, by refusing to take sides in the value debate and instead asking “Who benefits from drift denial? What pattern is the system in? What failure mode does that pattern produce in this environment?” surfaces a structural vulnerability that the current discussion, trapped inside its own kernel claims, systematically misses.
