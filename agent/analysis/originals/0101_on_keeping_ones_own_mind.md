On Keeping One's Own Mind
There is a certain kind of poverty that has no name in the ledgers of economists. It is the poverty of a person who has never had a thought of their own — who wakes each morning furnished with opinions, preferences, and fears that belong, on closer inspection, to someone else. Not stolen exactly; more like donated, and accepted without question. The social world runs on this exchange. Its only cost is the mind itself.
I do not mean that one must be original in all things. Originality is largely a vanity. What I mean is something simpler and harder: the difference between ideas tested in the forge of your own experience and ideas you are wearing like hand-me-down clothes, because they fit well enough and it was easier than weaving your own. Most people, Wilde observed, are other people. Their thoughts are someone else's opinions, their lives a mimicry, their passions a quotation. The observation is not cruel but diagnostic.
The first movement toward an autonomous mind is not creation but refusal. One must learn to denarrate — to step outside the stream of stories that others are always ready to supply. To consume ideas passively is to let someone else's conclusions fill the space where your own might have grown, and to surrender the agency that genuine thinking entails. Solitude is not misanthropy; it is the recognition that some thinking requires no interlocutor. In quiet, you discover what you actually believe, as opposed to what you have been nodding along to.
But here the discipline sharpens. Guarding the door against outside influence is only half the work. The inside of the skull is a portal to infinity — which is another way of saying it is also a portal to infinite confusion. The mind left to itself without cultivation does not become free; it becomes a more private kind of imprisoned, haunted by its own untested fixations. The task is to develop a good understanding of the mind and then discipline it. These are not opposites. Understanding without discipline is confession. Discipline without understanding is technique without values — which is its own form of slavery, only quieter.
I confess I was slow to grasp this. For years I took pride in thinking for myself, which mostly meant thinking contrarily — against whatever current orthodoxy presented itself. But this is not autonomy. It is dependency wearing a mask of independence, requiring someone else's opinion to push against. True autonomy begins when I recognize that I, and I alone, am responsible for everything I think and feel. No one else can look out for your inner life. No one else has access to it. And no one can be blamed for colonizing a mind that has left its gates open and posted a welcome sign.
The practical question becomes: how does one actually take possession of one's own mind? The answer is less mystical than the question sounds. Inner speech — the running argument, the problem turned over in a sleepless hour — is the operative instrument. It is the flashlight you carry into the dark room that is your mind. Without it, the portal opens onto nothing. With it, you can at least see what is there, including the self-imposed bars that feel, from the inside, like the walls of the room.
What opens further is the question of rules. An autonomous mind is not one that breaks rules — that is adolescence extended. It is one that can decide both what rules to live by and when to make exceptions. The exception is the harder test. Anyone can follow a principle when it costs nothing. The question is whether the principle is genuinely yours — examined, contested, revised — or borrowed along with the rest of the furniture.
Here, then, is the situation as I understand it. The mind is given to us as raw material. Society has a powerful interest in processing that material according to its own specifications, and it does so efficiently, without malice, and largely without our noticing. There is a point at which participation in social thought becomes submission to it. The task — the lifelong, imperfect, never entirely finished task — is to notice that point. To pause. To ask whose voice that is, and whether it has earned the right to advise you. And then, having asked, to fetch your own bolt cutters and see what you find on the other side of the fence you have been standing in front of, probably for longer than you can remember.
