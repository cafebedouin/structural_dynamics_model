# On Our Native Affliction

Consider the ordinary morning. A person wakes, resolves to be patient, measured, clear-eyed—and by nine o'clock has already confirmed every suspicion held against them by their nearest enemies. This is not tragedy. It is the human condition, running on schedule.

What should astonish us is not how often we fail, but how thoroughly our failures are *structural*. E.O. Wilson, who spent his career watching creatures behave according to their nature, put it plainly: we have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and god-like technology. The candor of this formulation is still underappreciated. He is not describing a temporary maladjustment, some cultural lag to be corrected with better education or improved incentive structures. He is describing a species whose instruments of perception were calibrated to a world that no longer exists—and who have since handed those instruments access to fission.

The mismatch is most acute in time. Our minds are exquisitely responsive to the sudden and the loud. A sharp noise, an angry face, a rumor of threat—these mobilize us instantly, at some cost to everything else. But the slow accumulation of consequence—the decade-long erosion, the compound interest of neglect—this we cannot feel in our bones. We are structurally miscalibrated for the long view. That is simply true of us, and the first honest thing is to admit it. What we are asking of ourselves when we plan for the future is something close to asking a compass to tell time. The instrument is not broken; it is just not that instrument.

We compound this by the way the mind processes what it encounters. Pattern recognition—the very faculty that allows us to function at all—is the same faculty that locks us into error. We sort faster than we verify. We prefer the thought that costs nothing. Denial, in this light, is not a failure of will; it is the mind's preferred energy state, the comfort zone that requires no effort at all. And because the errors happen before awareness, they arrive already wearing the clothes of truth. We do not experience them as errors. We experience them as perception.

I do not exempt myself. I have, this week alone, acted on several things I know to be false, confirmed two opinions I held before I looked at the evidence, and avoided one small discomfort with a tenacity I have never brought to an actual task. "What sane person could live in this world and not be crazy?" asks Ursula K. Le Guin—and she means it as a kind of mercy. The world presses back with exactly enough pressure to justify whatever shape we have already bent ourselves into.

We fear what we do not know. But what we know that is not so is what undoes us. The fear of the unknown at least keeps the door ajar. It is the certainties that close it.

And then there is the weight we carry from before we could choose what to carry. The dysfunctions and idiosyncrasies of childhood become, by the time we reach adulthood, invisible—not gone, but naturalized, built into the floor of what we take to be ordinary. We do not drag our pasts behind us like luggage; we wear them, forget they are clothes, and mistake them for skin. What we most resent in others tends to be what we have most carefully avoided naming in ourselves. The problem—stated plainly, without consolation—is that people are susceptible, prideful, bullheaded, egotistic, dumbstruck, and lazy. I read that sentence and feel it as an accusation before I feel it as a description. That gap is itself part of the argument.

Andrew Huberman, who studies the neuroscience of behavior, has argued that most of the disorder and dysfunction in the world comes from lack of impulse control. This is, of course, contestable as a complete account—poverty, structural violence, and institutional failure are not reducible to any individual's failure of restraint, and to say so would be its own convenient self-deception. But the claim lands because it points at something real: the gap between knowing and doing, between the self we announce in the morning and the one who shows up by noon. Blaise Pascal diagnosed the same wound three centuries earlier from a different angle: all of humanity's problems, he observed, stem from the inability to sit quietly in a room alone. The two observations are not so far apart. The room is merely the smallest possible test of the impulse.

There is a temptation, here, to conclude. To find in the catalog of our deficiencies some organizing principle that redeems them, some framing in which the Paleolithic emotions and the medieval institutions and the god-like technology resolve into a legible lesson. But Montaigne—who invented this form precisely because he found honest sitting-with a question more useful than quick resolution—would recognize that move for what it is: one more application of the cognitive machinery that allows us to function by not looking too directly at what is there.

The most that can be offered is a small discipline: to notice, in the moment before the pattern fires and the conclusion arrives, that there was a moment there. It does not solve the problem. But it is the difference between a flaw that is invisible to the one who has it, and a flaw that is merely very difficult to correct. That difference is not nothing.
