# On What We Want

The hardest thing in life, someone once wrote, is to know what you want; most people never figure it out and so wind up pretending they wanted what they could get. I have been turning this over for years, and the longer I hold it, the more it reveals. There is a cruelty in it, but also a kind of mercy. The cruelty lies in the word *pretending* — which implies that somewhere beneath the accommodation, a truer desire waits, muted and patient. The mercy is that pretending is itself a form of survival. We are not all equipped to locate what we truly want, and the world does not pause while we search.

John Updike observed that living is a compromise between doing what you want and doing what other people want. He said this as description, not complaint, and the distinction matters. A complaint seeks remedy. A description invites you to look squarely. The compromise Updike names is not a failure of nerve or a surrender of self; it is the structural condition of a person who exists among other persons. The world was here before us, populated and arranged. To pretend that pure self-determination is available to us — that we might step clear of inherited obligations and social obligations and the obligations we have made ourselves through prior choices — is to mistake the ideal for the achievable. The negotiation is continuous and never fully resolved. Most lives are spent inside it.

And yet we are not merely passive within this negotiation. Every decision sends you down a path that could turn out to be the wrong one. This is the weight we carry into every fork: not just the road taken, but the quiet arithmetic of roads abandoned. The philosopher's term for this is opportunity cost; the human term is regret. Regret is peculiar. It operates backward while we move forward, and it compounds in proportion to how seriously we took the choice. We do not much regret the trivial misstep. We regret the years given over to something that ultimately did not suit us — the career chosen to satisfy a parent, the relationship prolonged past its honest end, the decade spent in proximity to someone else's ambition.

This is where the accounting becomes genuinely painful: we squander our lives on trivia. Not through laziness exactly, nor through malice. The culprit is the gravitational pull of small urgencies — the inbox, the errand, the social obligation that consumes an afternoon, then a week, then a year. No single squandering seems catastrophic, which is precisely what makes it so. The aggregate is what kills us. We look up one day and realize that the years passed largely in service to purposes we never explicitly chose, and that these hours did not accumulate into anything we would have selected had we been asked. By then the pattern is largely set.

Montaigne knew this and looked at it without flinching. He made his own mind into a laboratory and his essays into the record of that experiment — examining himself not to confess but to understand, to take the measure of a life from the inside while it was still being lived. He did not pretend to have answers. He pretended only to honesty and rigor, which are more useful. What he found, repeatedly, is that a man is not one thing but many, and that what we present to the world is a negotiation between inner multiplicity and external demand. He revised his essays for thirty years, which is itself a kind of argument: that clarity about what you want is the outcome of a lived life, not its precondition.

We all have chapters we would rather stay unpublished. This is not quite shame — it is more like the natural reluctance to have one's provisional self treated as definitive. We were always, at every stage, works in progress; the choices that now carry weight were made by someone with less information and less perspective than we have now. The metaphor of publication is apt. A published chapter is fixed. You cannot revise it after the fact. You can only write forward, with whatever you have learned.

What can be said in the face of all this? That choices have consequences, that most desires are poorly specified, that regret is cheaper and more common than the genuine confrontation with what we actually want — none of this is particularly comforting. The more useful observation may be something simpler: the question is not how to eliminate regret or engineer optimal choices. It is how to build a relationship with your own life honest enough to make the choices that follow livable. That means attending to what you actually want — not what is easy to want, not what others have arranged for you to want — and then bearing the consequences without excessive resentment.

Forgiveness, it has been said, is better than regret. But this should not be read as an invitation to carelessness. It is, rather, a recognition that we are finite creatures making consequential choices under genuine uncertainty, and that the self who made those choices — earnest, ambitious, confused — deserves something more useful than a life sentence of second-guessing. We squander our lives on trivia, in part, because we are too hard on ourselves to dare to want something large and pursue it without guarantee of success. The correction is not to want less. It is to want more honestly, and to accept that the wanting itself changes us, regardless of outcome.
