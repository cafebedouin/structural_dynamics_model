# Where the Idea Comes From

In 2018, Kieran Healy described two computing revolutions. The first abstracts the technology away from the user — phones, photo apps, the digital descendants of the typewriter and the index card — and optimizes for ease. The second exposes more capability and asks more of the user — Emacs, git, Pandoc — and optimizes for work that doesn't fit the business-office metaphor. His claim was that the choice of tools encodes a mode of work, and that convenience defaults misshape work that belongs to the other revolution. It is not only Healy's cut; the same line shows up wherever people distinguish a tool that amplifies what you bring from a tool that supplies it in your place. But Healy named it cleanly, so I'll use his terms.

The chat interface inherits from both lineages, and it inherits unevenly. Underneath, the model is closer to Emacs than to Word: it tolerates open-ended composition, supports arbitrary depth, rewards discipline brought to the interaction. At the surface it presents as a messaging app — blank field, send button, the design language of SMS. The interface does not lie about what it is so much as default to the most legible prior and decline to signal the rest. That default picks a revolution for the user, and for a large fraction of the work people actually do in the field, it picks the wrong one.

The mechanism is concrete, and naming it matters more than the metaphor. The chat field collapses three things a capability-extension workflow needs kept separate. It collapses *state* — no visible working memory, no versioning, no artifact boundaries; there is only the scroll. It collapses *roles* — brainstorming, drafting, auditing, and stress-testing all happen in the same undifferentiated box. And it collapses *time* — no persistent trajectory of thought, just a conversation that resets. Stateless interaction is fine for convenience work and corrosive for thinking that has to accumulate. This is why the mismatch is structural and not a matter of user attitude.

It becomes legible the moment the interface changes shape. Claude Code does not add a feature to the chat box; it changes the shape of the interaction — working directory, files, version control, subagents. More generally: the instant a tool puts state, persistence, and artifact boundaries in front of you, "chat" stops being a plausible mental model. The capability-extension user suddenly has somewhere to live, and the mode is legible in the tooling rather than held privately in the user's head. The same work is possible in a plain prompt field — bring a half-formed argument, push on it across many turns, keep the thread coherent yourself — but there the field still insists, by its shape, that this is casual messaging. Two structurally different kinds of work, one surface, and the surface advertises the convenient kind.

This has a cost that shows up in the discourse. Part of the standing "AI is unreliable" complaint is a frame collision: convenience-tool expectations meeting capability-extension behavior. Bring a search-and-replace-for-typing model to the prompt and a confident fabrication feels like betrayal; bring an iterate-with-a-sharp-interlocutor model and the same output is material to work against. The frame does not *create* the failure — fabrication under uncertainty and citation error are real, stable, and frame-independent — but it determines whether a failure is read as a disqualifying defect or as friction the user is expected to handle. There is a quieter version of the same error underneath: the convenience frame implicitly assigns responsibility for correctness to the system, the way search or a calculator does, so when the model fills a gap confidently the user experiences not just a wrong answer but a broken contract. The contract was never offered. It was inferred from the shape of the box.

A specific inversion makes the modes concrete. A phrase circulates among some users — "the idea fairy" — applied to language models, the implication being that the model is the source of ideas and the human is the editor, the filter, the one who selects. From inside the convenience frame this is honest reporting. If the bottleneck is *I need an idea, any idea, to start moving*, then brainstorming-as-a-service is exactly the service, and the framing names the experience accurately. The position is structurally legitimate; many people have that bottleneck and the tool meets it well.

From inside the capability-extension frame the picture is closer to inverted, though not as cleanly as the slogan suggests. The user arrives with the idea — half-formed, often weird, with a shape they can feel but not yet articulate — and the model's job is resistance: where does this break, what is the steelman against it, what is the hidden assumption. The honest qualification is that the model is not inert even here. It surfaces associations the user didn't hold, recombines pieces, sometimes pushes the idea somewhere the user wouldn't have reached alone. The cleaner statement is not "the user generates and the model edits" but: the idea originates with the user and is transformed through the interaction. And the modes are not stable within a session — you can start with your own sketch and, a few compelling elaborations later, find you've slid into letting the model drive. Good capability-extension practice involves periodic checks on who is actually steering, a kind of version control for authorship.

That instability is why the diagnostic has to be a counterfactual rather than a question about who typed first. Ask, of anything you're making with the model: *where would this idea be if the model vanished right now?* If the answer is *nowhere* — it would not exist — you are in convenience mode, and that may be exactly right for the task. If the answer is *in my notes, but less articulated, less tested* — you are in capability-extension mode. The test survives hybrid sessions and authorship drift because it asks about the idea's center of gravity, not its first keystroke. It is local and phenomenological; you can run it mid-session without deciding anything about your identity as a user.

A serious objection deserves weight here. The distinction flatters the analytically sophisticated. Calling capability-extension a separate revolution looks like a way for one group to draw a line and stand on the good side of it — and the plain-text tools Healy praised do, in practice, carry the social register of academics and developers. Import the frame and you risk importing the coding: serious users here, casual users there. The objection is sometimes correct about how such frames get used.

The response is not that the objection is wrong but that it is aimed at the wrong target. The two modes are not better and worse; they are different objective functions. Convenience mode optimizes for speed, low activation energy, throughput — and is the right tool when those are what the task needs. Capability-extension mode optimizes for rigor, coherence under pressure, and the development of an idea over time. Neither objective dominates the other in general. The harm the essay points at is not convenience use; it is capability work done under convenience assumptions, which the interface actively invites. The "idea fairy" framing is honest from one position and a category error from the other, and the field's shape is what makes the second position easy to fall into without noticing.

Naming this serves the people doing capability-extension work without language for it. They usually sense that the interaction is neither brainstorming nor search, but the interface gives them no vocabulary and the dominant discourse gives them no peers, so they end up inventing private disciplines the tool neither acknowledges nor scaffolds. Naming it also serves convenience users, by relieving them of an implicit demand to perform a rigor they neither need nor asked for. It does not serve actors — commercial or institutional — who benefit from "AI" staying a single undifferentiated category whose value or danger can be debated at the level of the technology rather than the level of the use. Keeping the funnel wide depends on keeping the convenient illusion intact; that is an economic interest, not only a design accident.

The chat box is the new Word — well-shaped for the work the office metaphor was built around, badly shaped for the work that isn't. The question was never which tool to use; it is the same tool. The model can sharpen an idea, recombine it, sometimes hand you one you didn't have. What it cannot hand you is the reason this idea and not another is worth the work — the stake that makes the thing yours. That part doesn't transfer to the field, however the field is shaped.

### Open Questions (Ω)

**Ω_E — Distribution of use-modes.** Empirically resolvable. What fraction of chat-interface use is convenience versus capability-extension? Resolvable through usage research — session length, iteration depth, edit and retention patterns, branching versus single-shot. Plausibly known to providers, not public. Best current guess, explicitly a guess: convenience dominates volume; capability-extension is a minority pattern that generates a disproportionate share of high-value outcomes and user loyalty.

**Ω_C — Granularity of the cut.** Conceptually underspecified. Whether "two revolutions" is the right grain or whether use-modes are better described as a continuum or a finer set. Dissolves once the analytical purpose is fixed: two suffices for naming the interface mismatch; designing differentiated tooling may need more.

**Ω_I — Intervention efficacy.** Empirically resolvable, and the one with the most product weight. If the diagnostic is surfaced — taught, or built into the interface — do users actually change modes, or do they relabel the same behavior and carry on? A frame that names a real distinction but changes no practice is a description, not a tool. Tractable through A/B work; currently untested.

**Ω_P — Should the interface surface the mode?** Structurally values-dependent. Making the mode visible at the interface, as Claude Code does, likely serves capability-extension users while onboarding fewer convenience users; a mode-agnostic field keeps the broadest funnel at the cost of the confusion described here. The trade-off is economic as much as functional and is not resolvable by analysis. Decision authority sits with product teams and encodes which population they prioritize.
