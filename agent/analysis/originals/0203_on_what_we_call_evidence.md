# On What We Call Evidence

*— a first draft*

---

I have spent some years collecting rules about knowledge the way a carpenter collects proverbs about wood. *Evidence outweighs testimony. Data over narrative. Look for disconfirming evidence.* These feel like commandments, and perhaps they are. But commandments need commentary, and the commentary is where all the trouble lives.

Begin with the obvious: we cannot trust only what we have personally witnessed. The world is too large, and we experience very little of it directly. Most of what passes for knowledge — about history, about continents we have not visited, about the composition of the air we breathe — comes to us through instruments, records, the careful counting of other people's careful counters. This is not a defect in knowledge; it is its condition. The alternative is a solipsism no one actually practices. And so we rely on statistics, on journalism, on accumulated testimony. We must. And we should do so without embarrassment.

But here is where the first complication presents itself. Testimony is evidence — and yet evidence outweighs it. How can both be true? Quite simply: a single account, however honest and detailed, carries less weight than a pattern verified across many accounts checked against each other and against physical record. The historian who reads one memoir performs a different act from the historian who reads a thousand. The clinician who listens to a patient is doing something different from the epidemiologist working across a population. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient. *Analysis without numbers is only opinion* — but the numbers, too, require someone to collect them, frame them, and interpret them. Data does not speak; it is spoken. We might say data is computation wearing a trenchcoat: dressed in the costume of the physical, still human work beneath.

The more troubling complication is one I can demonstrate with my own behavior. I find that my standards for evidence shift with my desire to believe. When a person I admire is accused of something, I suddenly discover methodological objections I had never bothered to raise. When a study confirms my intuition, I accept its methodology without ceremony. When it contradicts me, I become a careful reader at last. This is not unique to me — the masses, Gustave Le Bon observed with perhaps too much contempt but not too much inaccuracy, have never thirsted after truth. They deify their errors and turn aside from evidence that does not please them. We all do this to a degree we generally underestimate.

Alain would say: the remedy is the will. Discipline yourself to look at what you do not wish to see. I half believe him. What he calls will, I would sometimes call stubbornness or luck of temperament. Montaigne might add — and here I think he is right — that the man who is most certain he has conquered his own biases has usually conquered only the pretense of confronting them. The task is real and the success is always partial. *Glossing over data and going straight to interpretation* is not laziness; it is a deep human reflex, and the reflexes do not yield to mere instruction.

Then there is the strange problem of proof itself. Push any demand for further evidence far enough and you arrive, inevitably, at three exits: circular argument, infinite regress, or a dogma taken on faith. The Münchhausen trilemma, some philosophers call it, after the Baron who supposedly pulled himself from the swamp by his own hair. There is no bedrock beneath bedrock. We are all stopping somewhere and calling that somewhere a foundation. The honest epistemologist does not pretend otherwise; she makes her stopping point explicit and argues for why it is a reasonable place to stop. This, too, is a judgment — which means that *evidence still requires judgment*, however much we might wish otherwise.

What follows practically from all of this is, I think, the most humbling conclusion: we cannot wait for certainty. *Making policy is the art of taking good decisions on insufficient evidence*, as Wayland Young wrote. Not because we are sloppy, but because complete evidence arrives, if it arrives at all, long after the decision was required. The captain in fog does not anchor until visibility improves; she reduces speed, sounds the horn, consults the chart, and keeps moving.

One further thing wants saying. You can refute forty claims with a single well-aimed fact, but forty facts will not necessarily instruct a single committed fool. This asymmetry is worth sitting with. It does not mean evidence is useless — it means evidence operates in a context of willingness, and willingness is not itself produced by evidence. The epistemologist's tools sharpen the thinking of those who already wish to think. For the rest, something else is required, and I am not sure what to call it. Perhaps just time.

What I draw from all of this: evidence is not a destination but a discipline. Something you practice, not something you arrive at. *Even the best ideas get brushed aside by real-world data* — this is not a tragedy but the mechanism. Do not take it personally; take it seriously. The world corrects us. Our only freedom is in how quickly and honestly we allow it to.
