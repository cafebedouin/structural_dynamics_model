The marketplace metaphor is doing exactly the work you're suspicious of — it implies an equilibrium where good ideas outcompete bad ones through some invisible-hand mechanism. But ideas don't compete on truth; they compete on transmissibility, emotional resonance, identity-signaling value, and fit with existing priors. "Socialism is poison" transmits faster than "institutional arrangements involve tradeoffs across justice, security, and welfare provision that no single ideology resolves." The bad idea has a structural advantage. So if there's value in challenging error, it can't be because the marketplace will sort it out.

What unchallenged assertions actually do is shift the Overton window through repetition. Each unchallenged claim is a small vote that this is sayable, normal, within the range of acceptable opinion. The mechanism isn't persuasion, it's normalization. People calibrate what's reasonable partly by watching what gets pushback and what slides by. A claim that goes unchallenged ten times reads as consensus on the eleventh, even if no one actually agreed — they just didn't object. This is why bad-faith actors flood zones: not to win arguments but to exhaust the challenging capacity of people who would otherwise object, until objection itself starts to feel exhausting and pointless.

So the value of challenging error isn't really about converting the person making the claim, or even about the marketplace. It's about denying the claim its free pass through the normalization mechanism. You're not voting for your view to win; you're voting against the assertion's right to count as background consensus. That's a more modest goal but probably the realistic one. It also explains why the cost-benefit shifts with venue. In a comment thread with five lurkers, one challenge probably matters. In a flooded zone where every assertion gets ten more behind it, individual challenges accomplish little and burnout is the actual outcome the flooders are optimizing for. Knowing which situation you're in is most of the practical question.

The piece your instinct is tracking, I think, is that "marketplace of ideas" smuggles in the assumption that the system tends toward truth on its own. It doesn't. Truth-tending requires active maintenance by people willing to do unrewarded work, and that maintenance is a commons that can be depleted. Challenging error is contributing to the commons. Whether to contribute on a given day is a question about your own resources, not about whether the commons matters.
