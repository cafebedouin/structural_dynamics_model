# The Registry of John Winkler

*A Tale, updated from the papers of the late Diedrich Knickerbocker, who did not live to see the machine that would have made his authentications unnecessary*

---

The following was assembled from a case file, a parole record, and a public entry that outlived both. Of the three, only the last still exists in a form anyone can search, and it is therefore the one held to be beyond the possibility of doubt. It gives a name, a photograph, a range of dates, a distance to be kept from certain buildings, and a single word for what a man is. The other documents were the kind that get boxed, and boxes get thrown out. The entry does not get thrown out. It is the story now, and the story is short: it fits in a field.

The old historian whose method this borrows used to authenticate his tales by a certificate signed with a country justice's cross, and believed the record thereby placed beyond dispute. His descendants have improved the certificate. It no longer needs the cross, or the justice, or the man. What follows is longer than the entry, and less certain, because it comes from before the field was filled in — and I have rendered it in the plain speech of now rather than his, keeping only his one true trick, which was to let the public record stand in for the truth and hope no one checked the difference.

---

Whoever has driven south down the middle of Florida will remember the country around Lake County — the flatness of it, the retention ponds bright as coins, the subdivisions named for the wetlands they replaced: Heron Cove, Cypress Run, Sawgrass Landing, none of which contain a heron, a cypress, or a landing. The good wives of hillier counties once read their mountains for weather. Here there is nothing to read. The land does not change with the season or the hour. It is the same in the morning and at dusk, the same in the year a man goes away and the year he comes back, and this sameness is a kind of cruelty, because it lets you believe nothing has moved while everything has.

In one of these subdivisions, in a house that was already tired when he bought it, there lived a good-natured, useless man named John Winkler.

He was, everyone agreed, the nicest man on the street. This was said of him the way it is said of dogs and weather — without expecting anything in return. He could not keep a job, for reasons no one could ever specify; he simply drifted out of them, cheerfully, leaving behind employers who liked him and would not rehire him. He fixed the neighbors' mowers for nothing. He was the one who stood at the end of the cul-de-sac on summer evenings, running the garden hose into a shimmering arc while the block's children ran screaming through it, and he knew all their names, and he taught the small ones to ride bikes by trotting behind the seat with his hand out, letting go a second before they knew he had. Every parent on the street had, at one time or another, left a child in his yard without a second thought.

I set this down plainly, because later every warm thing in it would be turned over and made to cast a shadow it never had. That is the special cruelty of the thing that happened to him: it does not only take the future. It reaches back and repaints the past, the way you can repaint a sign.

His wife's name was Diane. She was not cruel. She was tired in the specific way of a woman who has done the arithmetic of a household for twenty years and found that it will not close, and who has understood, before her husband ever will, that his sweetness and his uselessness are not two facts but one. She kept the books. She kept the appointments. In the early years she had packed lunches for the jobs he would lose, and written the same two flat words on the napkin each time in ballpoint — *there you go* — and he had thrown every napkin away without keeping one. She kept, in a drawer, the mounting evidence of everything he had not done, and she brought it out at intervals, and he received it the only way he knew how: a shrug, a shake of the head, the eyes cast up at a ceiling that offered no help. This enraged her more than argument would have. You cannot argue with a man who has decided to be weather.

Whether Diane was a trap he was caught in or a rope he had let rot, I will not say, and I notice that I cannot. The man himself never decided. That un-decided thing sat at the center of him like a stone in fruit.

His refuge was a sports bar off Route 27 with a name I have lost, where a rotating court of unemployed and half-employed men held session under a wall of televisions, and where the nearest thing to a king was a heavy silent man named Vic who owned the place and adjudicated all disputes by the direction he blew his cigarette smoke. When Vic exhaled slowly toward the ceiling, you had won your point. When he sent it sideways and short, you had lost. John was well liked there too. He was well liked everywhere except the one house with his name on the mortgage.

It was from that house, on a Friday in the late summer of 2002, that Diane finally routed him — not with a broom but with a sentence, delivered flatly across the kitchen, to the effect that she did not care where he went as long as it was away. And John, who had spent his life being agreeable, agreed. He got in the car. He did not go to the bar. He drove south, toward Miami, with the radio on and the windows down and a feeling in his chest he mistook for freedom, and which was, in the narrow technical sense, exactly that.

---

What happened in Miami I will give once, and plainly, because the man himself could never afterward assemble it into a shape, and the record's sworn version is the one I trust least.

He had answered an ad. What he had driven three hundred miles to buy was small and tawdry and real — an hour, cash left on a dresser, the kind of wrong a man tells himself is nobody's business but his own. That much John did, and I will not pretend he was innocent of it. He was not, and he knew the shape of what he was doing well enough to go and do it far from home.

But the room had authored something he had not come for. The air conditioner labored in the window. Down the corridor an ice machine dropped its load with a sound like distant applause; the carpet gave off a chemical sweetness; a sweating glass was put into his hand that he had not asked for, and he drank from it, because he was a man who drank what was put in his hand. And somewhere in the transaction, threaded in so smoothly he never saw the knot tied, a second party appeared who did not exist — a screen name, a number typed into a log, an age chosen from a menu by someone whose job it was to choose it. In the language the machine spoke, this fiction was a minor. John had not asked for it. The room had supplied it, the way the room supplied the drink. He was still trying to understand the evening when the thunder that had been rolling somewhere under the floor of the whole thing finally broke over him, and the door came open, and there were more men in it than there should have been.

He signed what they told him to sign. His court-appointed lawyer, a decent exhausted man, laid out the arithmetic the way Diane used to: the number if he fought and lost, the number if he did not fight. The numbers were not close. John, who had never in his life been able to argue with arithmetic, took the smaller number.

The smaller number was twenty-two years.

The courtroom was quiet for a moment that did not feel like mercy. Outside, the buses ran. Somewhere a lease was signed and a child entered a school that would close and reopen twice under different names before the child graduated. A date was set, far enough away that the world would not be the same world when it came.

Then John went under.

---

Prison is not sleep. This must be said, because the old story it resembles is a story about sleep, and the resemblance is a trap. Rip Van Winkle lay down under a tree and the twenty years came to him as a single dreamless night, and this was the whole mercy of his tale: he did not have to be there for it. John had to be there for all of it.

Time did not pass for John. It was spent, coin by coin, and he was awake for every coin. The cell had a light that was never fully off and never fully on, a gray constant thing, and a noise that was never silence — a hum, a far door, a man three cells down who talked all night to someone who wasn't there. Between the light that would not go dark and the noise that would not go quiet, the part of John that had once been able to become weather was sanded down to nothing. He read. He learned to weld, and was not permitted, later, to weld. He grieved his father through a slot in a door.

The world reached him in fragments, always late. Letters came, then thinned. His daughter's handwriting changed between one envelope and the next, rounded child's letters going upright and then going to nothing, because at some point a person stops writing to an address that frightens her friends. His son stopped first, and completely. Diane wrote twice, businesslike, about the house, and then filed the papers that unmarried them, and John signed those too, in the same hand he had signed everything — and felt, and could never explain to anyone, a disloyal thread of relief, as if a rope he had been holding for twenty years had finally been cut at the far end and he could stop pretending his arms were not tired. What he thought of, that night, awake under the gray light, was the napkins. He had not kept one. A pandemic came and went through the door-slot. He heard about it the way he heard about everything, secondhand and diminished, a rumor of a changed world.

And while he slept his open-eyed unsleep, the law repainted the sign over the door.

He had been sentenced under one set of rules. Somewhere in year four, and again in year nine, men he would never meet, in a building he would never see, decided the rules had been too soft, and wrote new ones, and — this is the part no one in the courtroom had promised him, because no one in the courtroom had known — made the new ones reach backward. The category he had been filed under was widened and hardened around him while he was inside it, unable to object, the way you cannot object to weather. He had gone in one kind of man in the eyes of the state. He would come out another kind, reclassified, without a hearing, by a hand he never saw. The portrait over the tavern door of his life still had his face on it. But the red coat had been painted over blue, and the scepter changed for a sword, and the word underneath was new.

---

He came out in the spring of 2024, with a bus voucher and a phone the state issued him, a black rectangle with no buttons that he did not know how to unlock.

His joints were stiff. His references were dead. He gave, at the reentry office, the address of the house on the cul-de-sac, and the caseworker — kind, harried, twenty-six years old, born the year he went in — told him gently that he could not live there. Not because it was sold, though it was. Because it was too close to a school, and a park, and a thing called a daycare that had not existed when he left. The map she turned toward him had circles drawn on it, wide soft circles of prohibition, and where the circles overlapped there was almost no county left. The places a man like him was permitted to live were the places no one wanted anyone to live: the edges, the highway margins, a particular motel that everyone in his situation seemed to end up at, so that it functioned as a kind of unofficial reservation — a garden rock the boldest hunter would not enter.

The bar was a vape shop. Vic was dead. The hardware store was a café, and John learned other things there, by watching, while he waited for a coffee he did not know how to order. He kept his eyes on the counter. Keeping his eyes on the counter was a thing he now did by reflex in any room that might hold a child, a discipline installed so deep he no longer felt it as effort. So it was at the low edge of his vision that he watched a young father at the next table hold a phone up to his own small daughter, who was eating a pancake, and narrate her eating of it in a bright performing voice, and send the little film of her off to an audience of strangers the father had never met. No one stopped him. It was called, John gathered, love, or content, or both. And John understood, sitting very still with his eyes down, the arithmetic of the world he had woken into: that his every recorded nearness to a child was a crime the machine would keep forever, while this man broadcast his own child's face to the whole earth and was thought a good father for it.

This was the thing that undid him, more than the circles on the map. In the world he had left, to be watched was a punishment; it was the specific thing that had been done *to him*. He came back to find that everyone had climbed into the watching on purpose. They carried it in their pockets and fed it their faces and their locations and their children; they looked each other up before dinner; they lived, cheerfully, inside the exact apparatus that had been built as his cage, and called it convenience. The watching had not gone away while he slept. It had gone everywhere. And only his still had the teeth. Everyone was findable now. Only John was findable in the way that came with a map pin, a photograph, a distance, and a word.

He learned this at the motel, on the second night, when a man he had never met knocked after dark with a phone held up like a badge. The screen lit the man's face from beneath and threw John's own face back at him — younger, from the year he went in, glowing and fixed — while the John on the doorstep stood aging in the wet Florida dark, breathing, sixty now, and less real to the man than the picture. There had been, the man explained, a notification: the application pinged the neighborhood when a new entry appeared within a certain radius. The man was not, particularly, cruel. He was frightened, and organized, and he had children, and from where he stood the application was a rope everyone held together to keep the dark out. He genuinely could not see John. He could see the entry. He asked John, not unreasonably from inside his own life, whether John understood that he was not wanted here.

It was the first of many such knocks, and they had the quality of the crowd that surrounds the returned stranger in the old tale and demands to know *which side he votes* — Federal or Democrat, Tory or patriot, one of us or one of them — except that the question now was not which side. The question was *what are you*, and the answer had already been filled in, in a field, by someone else, and no answer John gave in his own voice could overwrite it.

---

His daughter agreed to see him once, in a chain restaurant equidistant from their two lives, chosen because it was not near a school.

Her name was Katie. She was thirty-one. She had a child of her own now, a boy, whom she did not bring, and John understood why she had not brought him, and did not blame her, and something in him died quietly at the necessity of understanding it. She had her mother's tiredness already, come early. She had made a decision before she arrived, he could see, and the lunch was the executing of it: she would tell him where her mother was, and she would not tell him where she herself was, and that would be the shape of the thing.

Diane, she said, had died. Two years back. A stroke, fast, in a condo near the water she'd bought after the divorce, with a man named Ron whom she had, by every account, been happy with. Katie watched her father's face when she said it, watched for the grief, and the grief came — and underneath it came the same disloyal thread from years four and nine, the far end of the rope going slack. He was so ashamed of it that he looked down at his hands, and Katie, seeing him look away, read it as the guilt she had come prepared to see, and something in her closed that he would not get open again.

"What is it you want," she said. Not unkindly. It was the question everyone asked now — the caseworker's, the man-at-the-motel's, the whole changed country's: name yourself, in a field.

He opened his mouth. He got as far as the start of it — *I taught you to ride a* — and heard, in the half-second before the rest, how it would land: the word over the door was in that sentence too now, in *taught*, in the children he might reach for, in every warm thing, and it would arrive at her ear already curdled, already evidence. He watched the sentence spoil before he could finish it. He closed his mouth and did the old thing instead — the shrug, the head shake, the eyes cast up at a ceiling that had never once helped.

And Katie, watching him not-answer, did something with her own shoulders. A small helpless lift and drop. The exact gesture. She did not know she was doing it; she had spent a childhood hating it on her mother's behalf, and it had gone into her anyway, under the skin, without anyone's permission. John saw his own shrug come back at him off his daughter's body like a watermark held to the light — the one thing he had managed to pass down — and it was this, not the miles or the divorce or the word, that undid him at the table. Katie paid the check. She did not offer to see him again. He did not ask. That, too, they did with the same shoulders.

---

There is a moment in the old story when the returned man, pressed to say who he is, comes apart: *I'm not myself — I'm somebody else — that's me yonder — no, that's somebody else got into my shoes.* He points across the square at a young man leaning against a tree, his own son, the very image of him, and for a vertiginous instant cannot tell which of them is real.

John had his version, and it lived in the phone.

When at last a volunteer taught him to unlock the black rectangle, and some weeks later to look himself up — everyone could be looked up, and he had wanted, with a hunger he did not examine, to see what everyone saw — he found the entry. The photograph. The dates. The map with the pin on the motel. The distances. And the word. And he understood, looking at it, that this was the *him* that existed now. Not the man holding the phone. The entry was the one leaning against the tree, ragged and public and permanent, and it was the one the world meant when it said his name. He was the somebody-else who had gotten into his own shoes. The living John was the rumor. The entry was the fact.

There is a character in the old tale, old Peter Vanderdonk, the most ancient man in the village, brought forward to settle whether the returned stranger is who he claims. And Vanderdonk settles it — corroborates the whole impossible story — on the authority of *what his ancestor the historian had written down.* The village believes the record over the man in front of it. The record wins, and its only claim to authority is that it is written, and old, and does not change.

The application was John's Peter Vanderdonk. It corroborated him. Strangers consulted it and were satisfied. It was believed for the Vanderdonk reason, which is that a fixed thing feels truer than a living one, and a living one is exhausting, and who has the time.

---

I would like to tell you that he fought it. A better man in a better story fights it, files the petitions, forces the hearing, wins his name back inch by public inch, and the village, shamed, takes him in. But that story needs a machine capable of shame, and of relenting, and the machine John woke into was built precisely so that no one who ran it would ever have to feel either. The caseworker could not delist him and would have been insane, from inside her life, to want to. The legislator who hardened the rules slept fine. The man at the motel door was protecting his children. Everyone in the chain was a reasonable person doing a reasonable thing, and the chain as a whole did what no one in it intended and no one in it could stop. That is the modern horror, and the reason it never quite has a villain: it is a machine assembled out of decent, frightened people, running.

So he did not fight it. He did what Rip Van Winkle did. He got old.

He got a room, eventually, that was legal — at the edge, near the highway, in the unofficial reservation. And he found there a court of men like the one he had lost at Vic's: men wearing the same word, thrown together because they were permitted to be near almost no one else. He was well liked, even there. Some things the machine cannot reach.

One night, late, in the motel lot under a light full of moths, the oldest of them — a man who had been marked longer than John had been gone — said the thing aloud that none of them said in daylight. That the machine, in taking everything, had taken the weight too. That out here at the edge, with no job the world would let them keep, no family that would claim them, no future anyone expected them to build, they were, in the one way that mattered and that they could never admit to a soul who still had a life, *free.* Nobody's project. Nobody's disappointment. He laughed when he said it, to take the curse off it.

And another told the story they told each other the way John's own tale would later settle — that somewhere a case was moving through the courts, had been moving for years, and one day it would arrive, and wake, and undo the whole apparatus at a stroke, and unmark them all. He said it half-ironically, the way you say a thing you keep believing precisely because it will not come: an emperor asleep in a mountain, who will rise when the need is greatest. John had heard that one as a boy, in another life, about another sleeper.

He did not agree, and he did not argue. He shrugged — the old shrug, the family shrug, the one his daughter now carried without knowing it — and the men took it for whichever thing they needed it to be.

Because here is what I have to report, and it is the whole point, and it is the thing the old story also refused to decide. John was, at the end, something close to content. His obligations were gone — the machine had stripped them from him with everything else, and a man with no future the world will let him build is also a man of whom nothing is expected, and John had spent his whole life being unequal to what was expected of him. The petticoat government, the mortgage arithmetic, the drawer of everything undone — all of it over, cut at the far end. He could go in and out of his legal room whenever he pleased, near the men who understood him, useless and unbothered. Weather at last. He had wanted, forty years and one bad Friday ago, to drive south with the windows down and stop being anyone's project, and he had, in the most catastrophic way available, gotten it.

Whenever his old life on the cul-de-sac was mentioned — the hose, the bikes, the children whose names he still knew — he would shake his head, and shrug his shoulders, and cast up his eyes; which might pass either for grief at everything the word had reached back and taken from him, or for something harder to look at straight: the deliverance of a man finally released, by the very machine that erased him, from a life he had been trying to sleep his way out of all along.

I have looked at the gesture from both sides. I cannot tell you which it was. Neither, I think, could he. That un-decided thing sat at the center of him to the end, the stone in the fruit, and the machine, for all it kept, never had a field for it.

**NOTE.**

The foregoing, one would suspect, had been suggested to the compiler by that little German superstition of the emperor asleep in the mountain, who will wake to redeem a fallen world — or by the older Dutch tale of the idle man who dozed twenty years and woke a stranger in his own town. The subjoined particulars, however, show that it is an absolute fact, narrated with the machine's usual fidelity: the entry exists; it may be searched; it gives the name, the photograph, the dates, the distances, and the word. Being a record, it is beyond the possibility of doubt. That it is beyond the possibility of doubt, and that it is the least true thing anyone ever wrote about the man, are held, in the country he woke into, to be the same fact — which is perhaps the only genuinely new thing this version has to add to the old one.

The numbers will be cited, in due course, in a policy brief read by no one who was in that courtroom, in support of leaving the machinery exactly as it stands — on the ground, never quite demonstrated and never quite abandoned, that somewhere out past the edge of the county it is holding back the dark. Whether it holds back any dark at all is the one thing the record does not say. It was not built to find out. It was built to remember. It remembers everything, and it forgot the only thing that might have saved him — that once, on a summer evening, on a street that trusted him, he ran the hose in an arc and let the children through.
