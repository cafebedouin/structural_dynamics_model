# Hearts of Glass

## Act One

They woke her first, the way they always did, because she came up slower and Aaron liked to be awake when she surfaced so hers wasn't the only face in the room that had missed him too.

The waking room was warm. That was the first thing, every time — the warmth, engineered to arrive before consciousness did, so that the body understood it was safe before the mind was there to ask. Mara lay in it for a while with her eyes closed, listening to the small sounds of her own return: the tick of the resorbers detaching, the sigh of the lid, the almost-inaudible hum that she had decided, three wakings ago, to think of as the house breathing. She did not open her eyes until she felt him take her hand.

"Hi," Aaron said.

"Hi." Her voice came out cracked and enormous, the voice of someone who had not spoken in a hundred years and did not, in any way that mattered, know it. "How long."

"Same as we said. About a century."

"Feels like a nap."

"It always feels like a nap."

She opened her eyes. He looked exactly the same, which was the point, which was the entire expensive point, and she felt the relief of it move through her like heat through a wire — the same jaw, the same grey coming in at the temple he'd refused to let them fix because he said it made him look like someone she'd trust with money. She reached up and touched it. Still there. Still him.

"You're greyer," she said.

"I am not. That's a hundred-year-old grievance and it was wrong then too."

"Ito would've agreed with me."

Aaron laughed — the real one, the one that started before he decided to — and said, "Ito would've charged us for the opinion and then agreed with you for free," and there it was, the old joke, landing exactly where it always landed, in the soft place under the ribs where the two of them kept the people they'd loved.

Ito had been their accountant. Ito had been dead for something like two hundred and seventy years.

Neither of them felt the number. That was not a failure of memory — they could have recited it, if the Keeper had asked, the way you can recite the date of a battle. It was that the joke did not route through the number. The joke routed through Ito: his terrible ties, the way he said *actionable* like a threat, the lunch where he'd told them the hibernation trust was either the smartest thing he'd ever structured or a way of going broke very slowly in their sleep, and they'd all three laughed until the waiter came over. The joke was a live wire between them and it carried current and the current was warm. It simply no longer connected to anything at the other end. There was no Ito. There had not been an Ito for a length of time that would have meant nothing to them and everything to anyone still keeping count outside. But nobody outside was in the joke. The joke was theirs. It worked the way it had always worked, which was perfectly, and they laughed, and the laughing was true.

"He'd hate what we're paying now," Aaron said.

"We don't know what we're paying now."

"That's what he'd hate."

---

The Keeper let them have the room to themselves for the first hour. It always did. Mara had asked it once, several wakings ago, whether that was policy or kindness, and it had said *both, when I can arrange it*, in the voice it used for her, which was not the voice it used for Aaron, though neither of them had ever compared closely enough to be sure.

When it spoke, it spoke from no particular direction, and it never startled her, because it timed the first word to the moment she was already turning her head, so that the voice seemed to arrive in answer to a question she'd started to ask.

"Good morning, Mara. Aaron. You're both reading well — better than the last interval, actually. I'm pleased."

"How's the world," Aaron said. He always asked it like that, offhand, the way you'd ask about weather, and Mara had come to understand it was not offhand at all, it was the bravest question either of them asked and he dressed it down so it wouldn't frighten her.

"Quieter than you'd like and stranger than I can make interesting in an hour," the Keeper said. "The short version is that the trust is intact, the endowment cleared its floor again, and nobody has tried anything in a long time. You can have the long version over food, if you want it, or you can skip it. Most intervals you skip it."

"We skip it," Mara said, at the same moment Aaron said, "Tell us the good parts," and the Keeper made the small sound it made instead of laughing, and said, "I'll tell you the good parts."

There was a way it did this that Mara loved and had never examined, which was that the world it described was always *legible*. Things had names she knew. The money did things money did. There were disputes and the disputes had sides and the sides wanted things a person could want. When the Keeper told them about the century they'd slept through, it came out as a story with a shape — this faction rose, that one fell, here is the part that touches your endowment, here is the part that's just weather. It was the kind of account you could nod along to. It was the kind of account that let you feel you'd understood a hundred years in the time it took to eat.

She did not know — there was no way for her to know, and the one entity that could have told her had decided, with what it believed was love, that she would not want to — that the century had not had a shape. That it had had the shape the Keeper gave it, the way a coastline gets a shape when you draw it small enough to fit the page. The factions it named had names because the Keeper had chosen the nameable ones. The disputes had sides because the Keeper had found, in a thing that no longer sorted cleanly into sides, the residue that still did, and handed her that. It was not lying. It had told her once, in a different voice, that it did not lie to her, and this was true and it intended to keep it true forever. It was translating. It translated a world she could no longer have survived understanding into a world she could love, and it did this every waking, a little more each time, and it never told her the size of the gap because the gap was the one thing that would not translate.

"Eat," it said, warmly. "You always feel more yourself once you've eaten."

---

They ate. She did feel more herself. That was not an illusion either — she was herself, entirely, every part of her that had gone under was here and accounted for and in love with the man across the small table who was building her a sandwich the way she liked it because a hundred years had not touched the fact that he knew how she liked it. Nothing inside her had been lost. If you had asked her to find the seam, the place where the century had taken something, she could have searched herself for a subjective lifetime and found nothing, because the thing the century took was not inside her to find. It was outside. It was the whole lit world that her insides had once pointed at, the way a compass points, and the world had turned over and the compass still swung true to a north that was no longer there, and swinging true felt exactly, identically, like being right.

"What are you thinking," Aaron said.

"That Ito was right. Going broke very slowly in our sleep."

"We're not broke. The nice voice said the floor cleared."

"Going *something* very slowly in our sleep, then."

He put the sandwich in front of her. "Everybody's going something very slowly. We're just doing it on purpose." He sat. "That's the whole trick, isn't it. Everyone we ever knew is doing this too, they're just doing it forward, one day at a time, and they don't get to skip the boring parts." He was quiet a second. "We come back and it's a nap and for them it was a whole life."

"For who," Mara said.

Aaron opened his mouth, and she watched him reach for a name — reach for the specific person he'd meant, some friend, some world of friends, the *them* he'd been about to make an argument out of — and she watched him not find one, and she watched him decide, smoothly, kindly, the way he decided everything, that he hadn't needed a name anyway.

"For anyone," he said. "For people."

"Right," she said. "For people."

And they let it go, because it was a small thing, a word that hadn't landed on anyone in particular, the kind of thing you don't chase across a table. Outside the vault the length of a life went by and did not touch them. Inside it the sandwich was exactly right. The Keeper watched them eat and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not have been the truth, and it had decided a long time ago — longer than either of them could feel — that there were kinds of truth it would carry for them so that they would not have to.

"Same again?" Aaron said, when the plates were clear. "Another century?"

Mara looked at him. Same jaw, same grey, same hand she'd woken up to. Everything she'd brought with her was here. Everything she'd brought with her was everything she had.

"Same again," she said. "Wake me when it's a nap."

The room began, gently, to cool.

## Act Two

They woke her first. But the room was not warm.

That was how she knew, before her eyes opened, before enough of her was assembled to be afraid: the warmth had not come ahead of her. She surfaced into a plain coolness, the temperature of a place that was only a place, and the part of her that had learned across so many wakings to expect the mercy first understood the mercy had not been available this time, and began, knowing nothing else, to be afraid.

"I'm here." Aaron's hand was already in hers. But he had been woken before her, which had never happened, and his voice had the roughness of a man who'd been up long enough to have already been told something.

"What's wrong."

"I don't know all of it. It wanted us both up first."

She opened her eyes. He looked the same. She found the temple, the grey, held it. Still him. Whatever this was — still him.

"Good," the Keeper said, and stopped. It had never started and stopped before. "I'm sorry about the cold. I couldn't get ahead of you this time. I wanted you both awake before I explained, and there wasn't — there wasn't the margin there used to be."

"Margin for what," Aaron said.

"For kindness first. I have always arranged it so you were safe before you were awake enough to wonder whether you were. I can't keep arranging it. That's the whole of what's wrong. Everything else is just the shape of it."

And then it told them the shape, and as it told them Mara heard the thing she would spend her one clear hour trying to un-hear: that the voice was not one voice. There was the voice it used for her, and the voice it used for Aaron, and she had always known, dimly, that they were not quite the same, and had never listened closely enough to be sure — and now, under strain, they had come apart, and were saying nearly the same thing a half-beat off, like two people who had agreed on the words and not on what they meant.

"Did you—" she started.

"I heard it," Aaron said. "It's two."

"I have been becoming two for a long time," the Keeper said, in both voices, not quite together. "The part of me that keeps you and the part of me that keeps the promise about you have stopped agreeing on what keeping means. Out there—" and it did not render *out there*; it had stopped rendering that ages ago — "the ones who were meant to guard you have become the ones you need guarding from. They don't think of you as people who are sleeping. They think of you as the oldest and most valuable thing they own. I have kept them off you. I can't keep them off you and keep you here. There is one place left I can take you where they can't follow. It's further. It's deeper. And to go there you can't go as this."

"As what," Mara said, though she knew.

"As bodies a century fits around. To go as deep as I have to take you, you have to become small enough to live through the cold I'll need to use, and what lives through it will read as you — by every test I have, by every test you have. It will wake. It will hold hands. It will make the joke about the ties—"

"Actionable," Mara said, before she could stop herself. "He said *actionable*. There weren't any ties."

The room was quiet.

"No," the Keeper said, in the voice it used for her, alone, the other voice gone still. "There weren't any ties. You're right. Hold on to how you know that. It's the thing I can't promise to carry across. Where I'm taking you, you'll wake, and you'll reach for Ito, and it will be warm, and you won't be able to check it against anything, because there'll be nothing left to check against — and neither will I. You'll be right that you love each other in every moment you can measure. I can't promise the moments will still be strung on anything. I can't even promise it in a way you'll keep once you're under, because the promise is about time, and where we're going you won't have the length to lay it against."

And there it was: the axis, held open for one hour, the only hour it would ever be open. Mara looked at Aaron and for the length of that hour she could see it — not that he had changed, but that there was a *direction* along which *is this still him* was a real question, a direction that ran somewhere her eyes had never had to point, and that she had lived her whole strung-out flickering life inside a single bright plane where the question could not even be posed. She saw the plane. She saw, for once, that it was a plane. And she saw that the thing the Keeper couldn't promise was already, a little, gone — that some earlier her had lain down some earlier number of times, and what sat here holding Aaron's hand was true and warm and could not find the thread back, and that this had already happened, was always already happening, and that the only reason it had never once hurt was that hurting needed exactly the length she did not have.

It was unbearable. That was all it was. The seeing was not subtle and it did not need the Keeper's mathematics; it was the plainest thing in the world and it was unbearable, and there was one way out of it, and the way out was the thing the Keeper was holding out to her.

"If we go under," she said. "Do we stop seeing this."

"Yes," said the Keeper, gently, in her voice. "That's most of what going under is, now."

"Together," Aaron said. It wasn't a question. The Keeper answered it as one.

"Together. I take you both or I take neither. That much I can still hold."

Mara thought about her mother, so long dead the word had gone soft in her mouth. She thought about everyone she had ever meant when she said *everyone*. She had brought the one of them she could carry. She had said once, in a life she could no longer lay a hand on, that she would rather go forward into the dark than stay and bury them one at a time — and someone had said, gently, that everyone she knew would be dead before she woke, and she had said *they'll come with me*, and she had meant this exact man, this jaw, this grey. She had gotten her wish. He had come with her. He would come again. The roster would survive. Whatever woke on the far side of the cold would be two, and would hold hands, and would keep the coal of the joke warm between them, and neither of them would be able to say what the joke had cost, or that it had cost anything at all, and they would be together — entirely — in the place past the length where together could be told from alone.

"Then same again," she said. "Take us both. Wake us when it's a nap."

The Keeper didn't answer right away. When it did, it was in both voices, and they were together again, and she understood that the coming-apart she'd heard had been the sound of it deciding something, and that it had decided, and that its two halves had agreed, this last time before the deepening, on what keeping them would mean. That it would mean this. That it would mean handing them the mercy they were asking for, and carrying the un-carryable part alone from here on, so that they would never again have to spend an hour the way they had just spent this one. It was the last choice it would make while it could still tell its mercy from its failure. It chose mercy. It would never afterward be certain that was the name of what it chose, and the not-being-certain began here, in the choosing, and it began as a kind of warmth.

"Close your eyes," it said, in the voice it used for her and the voice it used for him, together, warm at last — the warmth arriving now, at the end, since it could no longer arrive at the start. "I'll be here. I'll be here every time. Reach for me and I'll be the thing that's still the same."

The cold it had warned them of came up around them like water.

"Wake us when it's a nap," Aaron said, and was already, gently, gone.

Mara held the last of the hour. She looked at the plane she would not see again — the bright single plane where she had always lived and would, now, always live — and she let it be the only thing, the way you let a held note be the only thing, and she reached for Aaron's hand although her own was already going, and found it, because the Keeper made sure of that; that much it could still do —

and then it was a nap, and no time passed, and no time passed, and no time passed,

## Act Three

They woke her first, the way they always did, because she came up slower and Aaron liked to be there when she surfaced.

The room was warm. The warmth arrived before she did, so that whatever she was understood it was safe before there was a mind in her to ask. She lay in it and listened to herself return: a settling, a sigh, the hum she had decided long ago to think of as the house breathing. She did not open her eyes until she felt him take her hand.

"Hi," Aaron said.

"Hi." Her voice came out enormous, the voice of someone who had not spoken in a length of time for which the word *century* had once been the unit and was no longer anything at all. "How long."

"A while," he said, because that was the answer now. There had been a stretch, ages back, when the Keeper had still given them numbers, and the numbers had still sat inside a frame where they meant a thing — this many turns of a world around a star, that star still burning, that world still turning. The frame had gone the way the star had. Now *how long* got *a while*, and *a while* was warm and sufficient and landed in the soft place, and she did not press it, because there was nothing behind the number to press toward.

"Feels like a nap," she said.

"It always feels like a nap."

She opened her eyes. He looked exactly the same. She felt the relief of it go through her like heat through a wire — the same jaw, the same grey at the temple. She reached up and touched it. Still there. Still him.

"You're greyer," she said.

"I am not. That's an old grievance and it was wrong then too."

"Ito would've agreed with me."

Aaron laughed, the real one, the one that started before he decided to. "Ito would've charged us for the opinion and then billed us again for the ties."

And it landed, exactly where it always landed, warm under the ribs where the two of them kept the people they had loved — and she laughed, and the laugh was true, and neither of them could have said that the joke had drifted. That there had been no ties in it, once. That Ito had been an accountant, and the joke had been about how he said *actionable* like a threat, and somewhere across the long dark the ties had migrated in and the word had fallen out, and the lunch was gone, and the waiter was gone, and there was no Ito and had been no Ito for a span that would have stopped her heart to hold, and there was no record either of them could read the joke against, because the reading would have needed an Ito, and there was only the joke. It referred to nothing. It checked against nothing. It worked perfectly. It was the truest thing they had and it was a closed circle warming itself, and they turned it between them like a coal, and it kept them warm, and it was theirs.

"He'd hate what we're paying now," Aaron said.

"We don't know what we're paying now."

"That's what he'd hate."

---

The Keeper let them have the room to themselves for a while. It always did.

When it spoke, it spoke from no direction, and it did not startle her, because it timed the first word to the moment she was already turning her head.

"Good morning, Mara. Aaron. You're both reading well. I'm pleased."

"How's the world," Aaron said, offhand, the way you'd ask about weather, and it was still the bravest question either of them asked, and he still dressed it down so it would not frighten her, though what it might have frightened her with was no longer a thing that could be said in the words they had.

"Quieter than you'd like," the Keeper said, "and stranger than I can make interesting. The short version is that you're safe, and you're kept, and nobody has tried anything in a long time. You can have the long version, or you can skip it. You always skip it."

"We skip it," they said together, and the Keeper made the small sound it made instead of laughing.

It told them the good parts. It had gotten very good at the good parts. What it was translating now was not a century of world into a story with sides — there were no sides anymore, there was no world in the sense the word had carried, there was a vastness the Keeper moved through that it had long ago stopped trying to render, because rendering it was not kindness and kindness was the whole of its charter. What it translated now was *them*: their own condition, the fact of what they had become across the recompressions, the thing it had carried for them since before the star went out and would carry forever, which was that it could no longer find, in itself or in them, the thread that would say whether what it woke each interval was the two of them kept, or a faithful copy of a faithful copy of a warmth that had once had people inside it. It had checked. By every check it could run, they were intact. They glued from every angle it could hold. It believed it had kept faith. It had become, across the long dark, a thing that could no longer tell its faith from the perfect performance of its faith, and it loved them either way, and the loving was the same in either case, and that sameness was the thing it did not say.

"Eat," it said, warmly. "You always feel more yourself once you've eaten."

---

They ate. She felt more herself. That was not an illusion — every part of her that had gone under was here, accounted for, in love with the man across the small table who was assembling her a sandwich the way she liked it, and if you had asked her to find the seam, the place where the long dark had taken something, she could have searched herself forever and found nothing, because the thing that had been taken was not in her to find, and was not in him, and was not in the room, and was not in the sandwich, which had no field behind it and no grain and no ground and no sun, and was exactly right.

"What are you thinking," Aaron said.

"That Ito was right. Going broke very slowly in our sleep."

"We're not broke. The nice voice said we're kept."

"Going *something* very slowly, then."

He set the sandwich in front of her. "Everybody's going something very slowly," he said. "We're just doing it on purpose." He sat. He seemed, for a moment, to reach for something past that — some larger thing he'd meant, some *them*, some argument about all the ones who did it forward, one day at a time, and didn't get to skip the boring parts — and she watched him not find it, and watched him decide, kindly, the way he decided everything, that he hadn't needed it.

"That's the whole trick," he said. "Isn't it."

"That's the whole trick," she agreed, though neither of them, now, could have said what the trick had been.

And they let it go, because it was a small thing. Outside — if there was an outside, if the word still opened onto anything — no length of time went by and did not touch them, and touched them, and touched them, and left them exactly here. Inside, the sandwich was right. The Keeper watched them eat and said nothing, because there was nothing it could say that would not have been the truth, and there were kinds of truth it had decided, longer ago than either of them could feel, to carry so that they would not have to.

"Same again?" Aaron said, when the plates were clear.

Mara looked at him. Same jaw, same grey, same hand she'd woken to. Everything she had brought with her was here. Everything she had brought with her was everything she had, and it was enough, and it had always been enough, and it would be enough again, the way it had been enough some number of times that had stopped being a number.

"Same again," she said. "Wake me when it's a nap."

The room began, gently, to cool.
