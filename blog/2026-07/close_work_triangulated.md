CLOSE WORK

The kitchen light was the only one on in the house, and Cole was already under it when Russ came down.

Six birds on the counter, out of the plastic now, pale and sweating in the trays. Their mother had made this every year for the church supper and God knew how much of it for other people's funerals, and someone at the visitation — one of the Kellner women, an aunt by marriage — had said of course they'd want her chicken tomorrow, everyone would expect it. As if expecting were a kindness. So here they were at two in the morning, because it had to be cold and portioned by the time people started coming, and neither of them had been sleeping anyway.

"You could've woke me," Russ said.

"You were up." Cole didn't look over. He had a boning knife going along a thigh, the blade laid almost flat, riding the bone, and the meat was coming off in one piece, clean, the way their mother did it. Russ had watched her do it a hundred times and had never once been able to make it happen.

He took the knife from the block — the other one, the bigger one — and started in on his own bird and immediately felt Cole notice, the way you feel a draft.

"You're chopping."

"I'm cutting it up."

"You're chopping. Don't." He set his knife down. "The bone tells you where to go. You're trying to go through it. Ride it."

Russ tried to ride it and hit bone anyway and the thigh came apart wrong, ragged, a flap of it still hanging. He put it in the tray like that.

It had been nine years. The night Cole enlisted he had put his fist through the mudroom door, and the patch was still there, a rectangle of lighter wood their father had cut and fit and never painted to match, and every time Russ went out to the garage he saw it. That was the leaving. It had been loud enough to hear from the road — the shouting, the truck, the door. Russ had spent a long time after that bracing for the version of Cole that would come back the same way he'd gone, kicking, loud, some reckoning that would let the two of them finally have it out. He'd rehearsed his half of it in the shower for years.

What had come back instead was quieter than their mother's house. Cole took up less room than he used to. He stood at the counter now, economical, still, and the stillness was the thing Russ couldn't get past — his little brother, who as a boy could not sit through a meal, who vibrated, now holding this flat unhurried quiet like it was a thing he'd been issued. Russ had stopped waiting for the old one to come up through it. This was the one he had now. He'd filed it that way — New Cole, like a fact about the weather — and somewhere in there he'd grieved the other one, quietly, the way you grieve a thing that isn't coming back.

"They kept saying it today," Russ said. "At the door. Thank you for your service." He heard himself doing it, reaching for the register, and couldn't stop. "I stood there and watched you take it, what, thirty times. And I never — I don't think I ever actually said, what you did over there, I'm —"

"The steel's by the toaster," Cole said. "Your edge is gone. That's half your problem."

Russ got the steel. He drew the blade down it a few times, wrong, too fast, and Cole watched him do that too and said nothing, which was its own kind of comment.

"What happened over there, Cole."

He said it flat and straight, the way you'd set something heavy down in the middle of a table, and he made himself look at his brother when he said it, which their family did not do, which their mother had never once done when the thing was hard — she'd look at the sink, the window, your hands, anywhere but you, and talk to that. Russ looked right at him. Aimed it dead center.

For a second Cole didn't move. He drew a breath and kept it a moment, then let it out slow through his nose, and Russ took it for what he'd long since decided all of it was — just Cole now, gone quiet, gone careful. He picked up his knife.

"Here." He turned Russ's bird around on the board so the leg faced him. "You find the seam first. Don't cut anything yet. Put your thumb in there — there — and you'll feel it open on its own. It wants to come apart. Your whole thing is you're deciding where it comes apart. You don't decide. You follow." He set the tip in and the blade went where the joint already wanted to go, and the leg came free with almost nothing to it, a small wet sound and then it was just off, laid open, done. "See how I'm not sawing. You saw, you tear it, it looks like that." He nodded at the ragged one in the tray without unkindness. "Close in on the bone. That close. Closer than you think is safe. That's where it's cleanest."

He handed the knife back.

Russ set his thumb in the next bird and felt for the seam. It was there. He hadn't known it would be there, a giving-place, a spot where the thing was already half-decided. He set the tip in and this time didn't try to go through anything, just followed, close in against the bone, closer than felt safe, and the joint opened for him and the leg came off clean in his hand.

"Yeah," Cole said. Just that.

Something in Russ's chest went sideways and he didn't look up from the board because if he looked up it would leave. He did the other leg. It came clean too. Cole was already reaching over to take what he'd cut, trimming the last silverskin off with two quick passes and laying the pieces into the tray in rows, and without either of them saying so they had a thing going now — Russ opening the birds along their seams, Cole trimming and laying, the trays filling in even rows, and for a long stretch there was no sound in the kitchen but the knives and the soft weight of meat going into steel, and it was the closest the two of them had stood in nine years, shoulder near shoulder under the one light, and neither of them said anything at all about anything.

The window over the sink went from black to the no-color that comes before gray. Somewhere a bird that was still alive started up.

They finished the last of it as the light came. Cole washed both knives, dried them, ran the steel down each a few times — right, unhurried — and set them back in the block. He wrapped the trays and stacked them in the second fridge, the one in the pantry, the way their mother had, largest on the bottom.

Russ stood there with his hands wet and realized the thing he'd been braced for all these years — the loud reckoning, the door coming off its hinges — was not going to happen tonight, and had maybe already not happened, somewhere back in the last hour, without his being told. He only knew the bracing had gone out of him, and it left him tired in a way that felt almost like the far side of something.

He didn't feel closer to knowing what had happened over there. He wasn't going to. He saw that clearly now and was surprised to find he could stand it.

"Ma always did the wings last," Cole said, closing the pantry door. "So they'd be on top. People go for the wings first." He wiped his hands on the towel and hung it back on the oven handle, squared. "There's coffee if you want it started."

He went up. Russ heard the fourth stair, the one that had always given, take his brother's weight and let it go.

The house sold in a weekend, eight years on, to a couple who wanted the lot and were going to take the back half of the house off and build out. Their father was in the condo by the river now, two rooms and a view of the water treatment plant he claimed to like. So it fell to the two of them to empty the place, which took a Saturday, because there turned out to be almost nothing in it that anybody wanted.

The last thing was the freezer in the pantry, the second one, the chest freezer their mother had kept full and that had held about a cup of ice and a bag of somebody's garden beans for a decade. Empty it weighed what a freezer weighs, which is most of what two men can do, and it would not turn the corner out of the mudroom. They tried it three ways. The door was in the way.

"Take the door off," Cole said.

He was forty now. The stillness Russ had filed away all those years ago was still on him — he stood the way he stood, took up the little room he took up — but Russ had stopped seeing it as the fact he'd once decided it was. He'd caught it moving too many times. Around Cole's daughter it came off him all the way — Russ had stood at a screen door one July and watched his brother chase a shrieking girl across the grass with the hose, hollering, graceless, nothing held anywhere in him. The thing Russ had filed under permanent, and grieved as permanent, Cole had been doing on purpose, and had been all this time quietly setting down.

He got the flat screwdriver from the box in the truck. Cole was already at the top hinge with a nail set, tapping the pin up from underneath so it rises instead of fighting you. Russ took the bottom one. They didn't talk about who took which. The pin came up for him smooth as anything, and he thought, without much feeling attached to it, that he knew how to do this now, and that he had not always, and that he could not have told you the day he learned.

The patch was right there at the level of his hands while he worked. Their father had cut it to fit the hole the night after Cole put his fist through the door, and had set it in clean, and had never once painted it to match, so it sat in the old brown door a bright rectangle of newer wood gone gold with age, a different gold than the door but a gold now too, the two of them weathering toward each other and not there yet, going on twenty years now, however long it had been. Nobody had ever painted it. Their father was not a man who left things unfinished, and he had left that, and no one had ever asked him why.

Cole's hand was six inches from the patch, working the pin, and he was not looking at it, but it was not the old not-looking, the hard kind, the kind their mother used to aim at the sink so she wouldn't have to aim it at you. It was just a man not looking at a thing he wasn't working on. Russ watched his brother not need to look at it. That was the thing that had turned.

There had been no night it turned. That was what Russ had never been able to get used to. For years after the funeral he had kept waiting to find the night — kept going back over that kitchen, the chickens, the two of them shoulder to shoulder till the window went gray, sure that something had happened in there that he could point to and say, that, that was where we came back — and there was nothing to point to. Just birds cut clean and a rhythm found and the bracing going quietly out of him before dawn with no announcement. What he'd wanted, for the better part of nine years, was the other kind of homecoming, the loud kind, the door coming off its hinges the way it had gone on — a reckoning big enough to hear from the road, a night the two of them finally had it out and closed the thing. He had rehearsed his half in the shower. And the loud one had never come, and he saw now, taking the actual door off its actual hinges, that if it had come it would have closed nothing. There was no night to go back to. There was never going to be.

The pin cleared. Russ lifted his side and Cole lifted his and the door came off in their four hands, and they leaned it against the washer, patch out, and went and got the freezer, and it turned the corner now with room to spare, and they walked it out to the truck in the rhythm they'd had for years and set it down on the gate and shoved it in.

"You want the door?" Russ said.

Cole looked at it, leaned there against the washer, the gold patch and the older gold around it. "Nah." He wiped his hands on his jeans. "House is coming down anyway." He got the bungees out of the cab. "You hungry? There's that place still open by the interstate. The one Ma wouldn't go to."

They left the door where it was. When the couple took the back half of the house off in the spring, it went with it, patch and all, into the container, and nobody was there to see it go.

The chicken was Nora's now, and had been for a while. She'd taken it over the year her father's hands started to go, and he'd let her, which was its own kind of ceremony, though nobody in the family would ever have called it that.

She had Theo in the kitchen with her because it was his first time meeting them and she wasn't going to leave him on the couch with two old men and a ballgame he didn't care about. She put a knife in his hand.

"Okay, so the thing my dad always says —" She stopped, because she had to remember how it actually went. "You're not trying to cut through it. You find where it already wants to come apart. There's a seam. Here —" She pressed his thumb into the joint. "You don't decide, you kind of just follow it. I don't know. He says it better."

Theo made a mess of the first one, and she laughed and took it from him and did it herself without looking, the leg coming off clean, the way it had come off for her since she was tall enough to reach the counter. She'd learned it from her dad, and her dad had learned it from his mother — the grandmother Nora knew as a name on a recipe card and nothing else, a woman who had made forty portions of this for a church supper in a state Nora had never been to.

"He taught you knife stuff?" Theo said. "He seems too — I don't know. Soft."

"Softest man alive." She was working while she talked, opening the birds along their seams, laying them in rows. "My whole life. My friends used to come over just to hang out with my dad. He did every bedtime. He used to chase me around the yard with the hose till I screamed." She tipped her head toward the living room. "Russ is the one to watch out for, if you're going to be scared of anybody. My uncle. The quiet one. Dad's just —" She shrugged. "Dad."

From the doorway, Russ watched her hands.

He'd come in for water and stayed, leaning on the frame, saying nothing, watching the girl take the birds apart the way his mother used to — the way he had never once managed himself, the way Cole had done it at two in the morning a lifetime ago with the window going gray. Nora felt him there and looked up.

"You okay, Uncle Russ?"

"You've got her hands." He cleared his throat. "My mother's. That's all."

"That's what you always say." She smiled at him, fond, not understanding, and went back to the birds, and he watched a little longer and then went to find his chair.

Cole came in slow, later, when the trays were nearly full, to see about his own birthday. He stood at his daughter's shoulder and looked at the rows of chicken and didn't offer to help, because of his hands, and because she had it. She'd had it for years.

"Wings last," he said.

"I know, Dad. So they go on top. I know."

He watched her do the wings last. And something moved across the old man — Nora caught it at the edge of her sight, her father drawing a breath and keeping it, the way he did sometimes, holding it a beat too long before he let it out slow through his nose. She bumped him with her shoulder.

"Don't get weepy. It's chicken."

"It's my birthday. I'm allowed."

"You're allowed." She kissed his cheek with her hands held out to the sides, wet, and told him to go sit down, they'd eat within the hour.

He went and sat between his brother and the window. Neither of them said anything. From the kitchen came the sound of his daughter running the tap, and a lid going onto a pot, and her voice asking Theo to hand her the foil.
