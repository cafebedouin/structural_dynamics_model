# Who Finishes the Poem

A bad wedding poem and a paragraph of machine slop fail in opposite directions, and telling them apart turns out to be impossible from the page — which is either a catastrophe or a clue, depending on where you think the poem happens.

The wedding poem is doggerel. The uncle who wrote it labored over every clumsy line, reaching for something he felt and couldn't quite land. It is sincere and it is bad. The machine paragraph is the reverse: competent, better-formed, cleaner in its meter, and no one reached for anything at all. We have a centuries-old word for the first kind of failure. We needed a new one, around 2024, for the second, and the search for what that new word names has mostly gone looking in the wrong place.

The obvious answer is that slop is text with nobody behind it — text that no longer carries what people started calling, half-consciously, a *channel*: a live trace back to a mind that meant it. On this account the uncle's poem has the one thing the machine's lacks. He was there; the model wasn't; and slop is the sound of absence dressed in competence. It is a satisfying story. It flatters the reader's intuition that what moves us in writing is contact with another person, and it explains the specific creepiness of fluent machine text, the sense of a handshake from an empty sleeve.

It is also wrong, and the cleanest way to see it is wrong is to try to run it on a real case. Consider a short text I have in front of me — a litany, a piece of incantatory prose meant to be read aloud in bad moments, modeled openly on the fear-litany from *Dune*. Half of it was written by a person. The other half came out of two different machines, and a third machine wrote the commentary explaining it. The person who assembled it cannot now tell you which lines are his. On the channel theory, this text is unclassifiable at best and slop at worst: there is no single mind it traces back to, no one who was fully home, the provenance smeared across four processes and unrecoverable even to its own author. And yet it works. It does for a reader what the fear-litany does — steadies something, gives the panic a shape to move through — or it doesn't, depending on the reader and the moment. Its four-way-broken origin has no bearing on whether it lands. A theory that has to call a working text slop because it can't find one author at the source is not describing the text. It is describing a preference about where writing should come from.

So the sender was never the thing. Watch what happens when you stop assuming value flows from the author and ask instead what actually varies when a text lands or fails to. It varies with the reader, almost entirely. A teenager reads Ayn Rand and finds a revelation; the same person at sixty finds the same sentences airless and adolescent. The text did not change. *Finnegans Wake* is a masterpiece to a few hundred people and an unreadable wall to everyone else, and the difference is not in the book. Shakespeare lands or dies on whether the reader met him as a living performance or as a text to be construed for an exam, on four centuries of drifted vocabulary, on a dozen conditions sitting entirely on the receiving end. Run down the list of cases where writing succeeds or fails and the deciding variable is almost never the presence or absence of a mind at the origin. It is the state of the mind at the destination. The author does not put the value into the text like wine into a bottle. The reader completes a circuit, and the current only flows when both ends are live.

This is the move the slop panic can't make, because the panic is a form of authorship-nostalgia — it mourns the disappearance of the sender because it never noticed that the sender was not where the meaning lived. We were never moved by someone being at the origin. We were moved by something completing at our own end, and the someone at the origin was a reliable enough correlate that we mistook it for the cause. Machine text removes the correlate while leaving the completion intact, and the vertigo people feel is the vertigo of discovering that a thing they credited to the author was theirs all along. The handshake from the empty sleeve unsettles because the warmth was never in the other hand. It was in the grip.

But this is exactly the point where the argument wants to overshoot, and the overshoot is as wrong as the thing it corrects. If the sender isn't the locus, the tempting conclusion is that the reader is — that meaning is purely in reception, that a text is a blank the reader fills however the moment allows. That theory is refuted just as fast, and by the same method: hold the reader fixed and vary the text. A grocery list will not become *Finnegans Wake* for any reader in any mood. *Finnegans Wake* is hard for everyone; the litany affords something the phone book does not; the difference survives every variation in who is reading. Something in the marks themselves does not move when the reader moves — and whatever it is, it is real, situation-fixed, the part you must defer to. Value is not in the sender and it is not in the receiver. It is in the completion, and completion takes two.

Which finally says what slop is, and the definition has teeth the sender-theory lacked. Slop is not text with no one behind it — that definition dies on the litany. Slop is **text that affords no completion**: fluent surface offering no purchase for the reader's half of the circuit to grip, nothing under the competence for the relation to form around. This is why the best slop still reads as slop *eventually* rather than immediately. You do not detect the absent sender — there is no detecting that from the page, ever. You detect that the thing gives nothing back on the second pass, that it slides off, that there is no there for your end to close onto. The failure is in the affordance, not the origin, which is why a fluent machine paragraph and a fluent corporate memo and a fluent undergraduate bluff all read as the same species of empty regardless of how much or little human presence sat behind each. Presence was never the variable. Purchase is. And purchase is a property you can only read the way you read whether a marriage is real or a joke is funny — not from a snapshot of the surface but from whether the thing keeps completing, for varied people, on repeated contact, over time.

The kill condition is clean, and worth stating because it is what makes this more than a preference dressed as a theory. If the completion account is right, then readers blind to a text's provenance should judge "this is hollow" and "this moved me" by something in the text that a machine and a human could each either supply or fail to supply — and those judgments should *not* track who or what actually wrote it, once you control for the reader's own state. If instead people can reliably feel the absent author through the page, sorting human from machine by presence alone with the reader held constant, then the sender was load-bearing after all and this whole argument is nostalgia's mirror image, wrong in the other direction. My wager is on the first, that presence is unreadable and purchase is what people are actually responding to when they think they are detecting a soul. But it is a wager with a resolution condition, not a conviction, and the resolution is empirical and not yet run.

There is a cost to being right about this, and it falls on the writer, which is probably why the writer resists it. If the author was never the locus, then the thing writers have quietly wanted — to be the source, to have the value trace back to them, to be missed when absent — was always a sentimental attachment the reader's actual experience did not require. The reader was finishing the poem the entire time. The best a writer can do is build a text that affords the finishing well, for many readers, across time — and then let go of the authorship the culture keeps promising is where the meaning lives. It isn't there. It never was. It is in whoever picks the thing up and closes the circuit, and whether they were reading a man or a machine or four machines and a man is a question about the wiring that the current, once it flows, does not ask.
